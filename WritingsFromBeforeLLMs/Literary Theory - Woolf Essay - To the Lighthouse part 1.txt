With To the Lighthouse, Virginia Woolf crafts a stream of consciousness narrative in an attempt to dislodge the reader from the rational and open the senses to a certain communication beyond words. Woolf’s diction, metaphors and narrative style echo that of ancient philosophers and modern artists alike. From the time of Lao Tzu, who three thousand years ago wrote the Tao Te Ching, poets, philosophers, and mystics have all sought to capture a certain inexpressible sentiment that they felt was found in every aspect of life and existence. Nietzsche, a principal influence on the modernist movement, stands notably among them. The problem they recurrently encounter, however, is that of language and the limitations thereof. The actual limitations stem not from words, but from the human mind–the logic and rationale of civilized society. Virginia Woolf seeks to overcome this limitation in To the Lighthouse by making a story in which nothing really happens, while throughout showcasing the barriers of communication and different types of understanding and knowledge. The lack of an established narrative such as that found in most novels reinforces Virginia Woolf’s perspective that everything is happening all the time everywhere. To the Lighthouse serves only to pose a single question, over and over again, through the perspectives of multiple characters and the passage of time.
        Lao Tzu, who first and most famously wrote of an unspeakable mystery called the “Tao”, or “The Way”, authored the Tao Te Ching as a guide to this strange aspect of reality. While the mainstream opinion considers the Tao Te Ching a book of philosophy and spirituality, the Tao Te Ching actually speaks of a real and undeniable experience that requires no faith or belief. The first chapter, quoted here in entirety from the translation by Brian Browne Walker, offers a succinct introduction to the nature of this experience: 


“Tao is beyond words 
and beyond understanding. 
Words may be used to speak of it, 
but they cannot contain it.
Tao existed before words and names, 
before heaven and earth, 
before the ten thousand things. 
It is the unlimited father and mother 
of all limited things.


Therefore, to see beyond boundaries
to the subtle heart of things,
dispense with names, 
with concepts, 
with expectations and ambitions and differences. 


Tao and its many manifestations
arise from the same source: 
subtle wonder within mysterious darkness.
This is the beginning of all understanding.”


~


The words of the Tao Te Ching, to those who have not experienced the Tao personally, at most read as a pleasant philosophy, no different than any other religion or book on spirituality. However, when examining other sources, whether Nietzsche or Virginia Woolf, a similar sentiment arises without fail, indicating a far stronger basis in reality than previously assumed. In Virginia Woolf’s essay On Being Ill, she writes three telling ideas on the nature of the logical mind, and the sensual impression of experience:


* “There is a virgin forest in each; a snowfield where even the print of birds’ feet is unknown. Here we go alone, and like it better so. Always to have sympathy, always to be accompanied, always to be understood would be intolerable. But in health the genial pretence must be kept up and the effort renewed–to communicate, to civilise, to share, to cultivate the desert, educate the native, to work together by day and by night to sport. In illness this make-believe ceases.”


* “In illness words seem to possess a mystic quality.”


* “In health, meaning has encroached upon sound. Our intelligence domineers over our senses. But in illness, with the police off-duty… if at last we grasp the meaning, it is all the richer for having come to us sensually first, by way of the palate and the nostrils, like some queer odour.”


~


The Tao is the most difficult and simplest thing to express. To those who have experienced it, a single word will suffice to convey a reference of it to another. However, to those who do not recognize the Tao, known variously by the names of “Logos” “Music of the Spheres” “Will to Power” “The Father” and many more, a thousand books are written and yet the understanding does not increase. Virginia Woolf recognizes the experience of the Tao, and much of her writing reflects the frustration of attempting to communicate what is, in essence, an incommunicable sentiment. She encourages the reader to abandon the surface feelings and social constructions that plague and fill our lives. Virginia Woolf considers these fabrications a distraction from the essence of life, the essence of reality, which is this Tao, though she did not know it by that name, choosing instead to refer to it as “life”, despite struggling to find a fitting name for it. The sentiment of only knowing the essence of reality through direct experience fills her essay On Being Ill, her essay Fiction as Art discusses using fiction as a more direct means of communicating this “life,” and To the Lighthouse focuses on the difficulties of communicating that experience, and is itself an example of those difficulties.




Virginia Woolf grew up in great familiarity with the city life and industrialized society, and often found herself as an undevoted follower of societal mores and customs. In relation to this, she found that sickness offered one an excuse to excuse oneself from what she viewed as arbitrary fluff. She writes in On Being Ill, “We float with the sticks on the stream; helter-skelter with the dead leaves on the lawn, irresponsible and disinterested and able, perhaps for the first time for years, to look round, to look up–to look, for example, at the sky.” Virginia Woolf felt that people live their lives ignorant of life, of the simple reality around them, and live rather in an illusion within their minds, not in the world, but in their idea of the world. She continues: 


“The first impression of that extraordinary spectacle is strangely overcoming. Ordinarily to look at the sky for any length of time is impossible. Pedestrians would be impeded and disconcerted by a public sky-gazer. What snatches we get of it are mutilated by chimneys and churches, serve as a background for man, signify wet weather or fine, daub windows gold, and, filling in the branches, complete the pathos of dishevelled autumnal plane trees in autumnal squares.”


Here she emphasizes the enormity of this essence of reality, and the disharmony of society and civilization. She continues: “Now, lying recumbent, staring straight up, the sky is discovered to be something so different from this that really it is a little shocking. This then has been going on all the time without our knowing it!” She echoes the sentiment of the timelessness of the Tao, that “to see beyond boundaries to the subtle heart of things, (one must) dispense with names, with concepts, with expectations and ambitions and differences” and the “subtle wonder within mysterious darkness.” 
But sadly, she is alone in her experience. A rare experience by itself, giving birth to three distinct religions already–Taoism, Buddhism, and Christianity, risen from the words of three individuals not properly understood–she struggles with this experience that she cannot make anyone else understand. In On Being Ill, she writes, “Use should be made of it. One should not let this gigantic cinema play perpetually to an empty house.” The house is empty because no one is paying attention. Virginia Woolf lives in a beautiful world, but the more beautifully she sees the world, and the more difficult it becomes to talk about it, the more alone she becomes. Recall the first words of the Tao Te Ching: “Tao is beyond words…” She turns to literature, not as an art, but as a tool of communication, hoping that somehow people will see beyond her words the beauty of the world, the Tao, that she sees. 
To the Lighthouse is Virginia Woolf’s exposé on how different types of people catch glimpses of the Tao, which in the book is referred to as ‘perfection’ or things ‘feeling just right’, but are unable to grasp it–it quickly fades, and also unable to truly communicate with each other or be understood, reflecting Woolf’s own lifelong frustrations.
