The Ultimate Warrior:
Martial Arts as a Path to Peace





        Those devoted to the Way of the warrior hold at the core of their philosophy the ability to overcome the opponent and resolve conflict. Miyamoto Musashi, a ronin of the Tokugawa Era, and his contemporary Yagyu Munenori, the shogunke heiho shihan (Martial Arts Teacher to the Family of the Shoguns), represent two extremes of samurai status. Yet, they both devote their lives to martial arts and each writes a book detailing the philosophy of martial arts. Despite proving themselves through duels, battles, and war, each believed that they did not understand the true nature of the Way of the warrior until much later in their lives, long after they had ceased fighting (Cleary xx). Their writings not only parallel each other, but also parallel the writings of Lao Tzu in the Tao Te Ching (in which “Tao” is synonymous with “Way”) and Yamamoto Tsunetomo in the Hagakure. A more recent martial artist, Bruce Lee, doubly reflects the tenets and perspectives inherent in the resolution of conflict within the self. Analyzing the common threads of these independent philosophers we find that true adherence to the Way of the warrior offers a path to inner peace and tranquility.
Miyamoto Musashi offers particularly valuable insight to the eternal nature of these philosophies due to the originality of his perspective. He writes, “I have not borrowed the old sayings of Buddhism or Confucianism, nor do I make use of old stories from military records or books on military science. With Heaven and Kannon for mirrors, I take up the brush and begin to write…”(Musashi 4). Munenori often mentions Zen buddhism as a source of inspiration, but Musashi discovers the eternal laws through independent dedication to the Way. In the introduction to his writings of The Book of Five Rings, Musashi admits that his undefeated status as a swordsman was not because he understood the Way of the warrior: “I had not been victorious because of consummate attainment of martial arts” (4). But after practicing many more years, he “spontaneously came upon the science of martial arts” (4). Coming upon this science, he immediately understood its broader implications. “Trusting in the advantage of military science, as I turn it into the sciences of all arts and skills, I have no teacher in anything” (4). “The true science of martial arts means practicing them in such a way that they will be useful at any time, and to teach them in such a way that they will be useful in all things” (6).  Musashi came to understand that martial arts makes use of universal principles, embodied in the Way of the warrior, and that these universal principles have universal application.
Yagyu Munenori, in The Book of Family Traditions on the Art of War, writes from a familiar Zen Buddhist background. “Many people remain ignorant of the Way no matter how much they study or how many words they know” (Munenori 100). To Munenori and Musashi, the final goal of martial arts is achieving not only the Way of the warrior but the Way in all things. Munenori reflects upon the purpose of learning in order to find the Way. “When everything you have learned disappears from your conscious mind and you become innocent, this is the spirit of perfecting things. At this time, you do not even know where your own mind is; neither the celestial devil nor outsiders can spy into your heart. The learning is for the purpose of reaching this state” (Munenori 102). He clarifies further, “When you do everything in the normal state of mind, as it is when totally unoccupied, then everything goes smoothly and easily. The heart of those on the Way is like a mirror, empty and clear, being mindless and yet not failing to accomplish anything” (121). The Way manifests from our unconscious feeling and the goal is to quiet all parts of our mind that hinder our following of this feeling. 
Extreme difficulty arises in writing about the Way, due to the nature of the Way, which is formless and void. Carl Gustav Jung writes, “It is not the concept that matters; the concept is only a word, a counter, and it has meaning and use only because it stands for a certain sum of experience” (Jung 33). One can understand the Way only through direct experience, which in regards to the martial arts comes through intense training and focus. The Way manifests itself through the idea of ‘emptiness’ or ‘formlessness’ and finds apt metaphor in the nature of water. Bruce Lee said, “Empty your mind. Be formless, shapeless, like water. You put water into a cup, it becomes the cup. You put water into a bottle, it becomes the bottle. You put it into a teapot, it becomes the teapot. Now water can flow or it can crash. Be water, my friend” (Lee bruce lee. com). The other philosophers echo this. “Taking water as the basic point of reference, one makes the mind fluid. Water conforms to the shape of the vessel, square or round; it can be a drop, and it can be an ocean” (Musashi 12). “Having attained a principle, one detaches from the principle; thus one has spontaneous independence in the science of martial arts and naturally attains marvels: discerning the rhythm when the time comes, one strikes spontaneously and naturally scores. This is the way of emptiness” (Musashi 14). “When what you have studied leaves your mind entirely, and practice also disappears, then, when you perform whatever art you are engaged in, you accomplish the techniques easily without being inhibited by concern over what you have learned, and yet without deviating from what you have learned. This is spontaneously conforming to learning without being consciously aware of doing so” (Munenori 101). “Forgetting learning, relinquishing mind, harmonizing without any self-conscious knowledge thereof, is the ultimate consummation of the Way” (Munenori 102). “The wise person acts without effort” (Lao Tzu 2). “A vessel is moulded from solid clay; its inner emptiness makes it useful” (Lao Tzu 11). “Everything in existence respects Tao and honors nature–not by decree, but spontaneously” (Lao Tzu 51). “Nothing under heaven is as soft and yielding as water. Yet for attacking the hard and strong, nothing can compare with it” (Lao Tzu 78). The yielding nature of water, unparalleled in its ability to adapt to its situation, exemplifies the nature of acceptance and freedom of thought and movement necessary to master the martial arts and the self.
No one can think in a capacity greater than the limitless natural law, for we ourselves, having arisen from it, are also bound by this same natural law. When we attempt to place ego-bound thought and intention over the natural laws, the natural laws do not rebuff our attempts at control and often overcome our intentions. The Way of the warrior requires the adherent to abandon selfish thoughts and limited perspectives in order to open themselves to the vast formless void of possibility. By submitting to the natural law, one acts in accordance with it, leading to the most desirable and harmonious outcome. Lao Tzu writes, “Do by not-doing. Act with non-action. Allow order to arise of itself” (3). Musashi writes, “Thinking correctly, clearly, and comprehensively, taking emptiness as the Way, you see the Way as emptiness. In emptiness there is good but no evil. Wisdom exists, logic exists, the Way exists, mind is empty” (90). By not limiting one’s thoughts or expectations, one is more prepared for every possible outcome. “The sage has no set mind” (Lao Tzu 49). Relinquishing fear, relinquishing desire, attuning ourselves to the breath of Tao, the eternal Way, we accept all that happens and meet death with a quiet mind should it come. Only by living in the acceptance of all things can one live at all. These teachings naturally arise from studying martial arts, whether with a Taoist or Buddhist background or, as is the case with Musashi, from the very nature of things themselves. The Tao Te Ching accounts for this spontaneous rediscovery by people like Musashi, “Because its essence is real and evident in the origins of all things, the name of the Tao has survived since the beginning of time” (21). Even if all the writings and oral teachings of the world were lost, one could rediscover the Way of the warrior and the Tao through simple observance of the natural world and reflection of the self.
Attaining the Way entails acting without thought, because one has attuned themselves to the natural order of things. “When in the process of self transformation, desires are aroused, calm them with nameless simplicity” (Lao Tzu 37). “In the pursuit of Tao, every day something is dropped” (Lao Tzu 48). Having an empty mind, one does not plan for the future. No rational thought or personal ambition can be greater than the limitless natural way of things. If you fight the natural way of things, swimming against the current, you will suffer and you will lose. In so many areas of life, people act in conflict and unacceptance; this is not the Way. “They turn away from the true Way by the personal biases in their minds and the individual warps in their vision…” (Musashi 90). To yield and accept a situation without regard to self reflects the nature of water, and thus reflects the Way. “If you get caught in a sudden cloudburst, you will still get a drenching even though you try to keep dry by hurrying along and taking cover under overhangs of roofs. If you are prepared to get wet from the start, the result is still the same but it is no hardship. This attitude can be applied to all things” (Tsunetomo 83). “Good men recognize that Tao provides for them and therefore they esteem it. Bad men don’t recognize this, but the Tao doesn’t stop providing for them.” The natural order occurs regardless of whether we are aware of it or not, whether we accept or understand it or not. Coming to the Way is becoming aware of or actively submitting to the natural order of things. This is the principle of doing by not-doing, and acting by non-acting. 
The root of martial arts is acceptance, and only through acceptance can one become the greatest warrior. Lao Tzu writes, “Only one knows how to die and stay dead and still go on living. Because he is constantly filling his being with nonbeing… because he has died, there isn’t any more room for death in him” (50). From his own learning and examination of the nature of martial arts, Musashi writes, “Knowing of non-existence while knowing of existence is emptiness” (89). Tsunetomo writes “Only when you constantly live as though already a corpse (joju shinimi) will you be able to find freedom in the martial Way, and fulfill your duties without fault throughout your life” (43). Bruce Lee’s own philosophical searches discovered the same principles. “Inwardly, psychologically, be a nobody” (Lee notebook). “Using no way as way; having no limitation as limitation” (Lee .com). “To change with change is the changeless state” (Lee.com). Achieving victory in the Way of the warrior requires becoming a perfect reflection of the situation, moving in response to the natural order of things, spontaneously. “If you delay for even the time it takes to blink an eye, you will have lost already” (Munemori 154). If you think rationally, or attempt to predict the moves of your opponent, you will suffer a delay in your reaction time and lose crucial adaptability.
By accepting death from the very start, you lose your greatest fear, your greatest hesitation. This is why Tsunetomo writes, “A samurai disgraced himself by not fighting back in a quarrel. To retaliate entails just frenetically throwing yourself at your adversary with the intention of being cut down. Being killed this way brings no shame. Thinking about how to win may result in missing the best opportunity to act” (70). Tsunetomo does not promote seeking death, but accepting it so that one may act without hesitation. This principle applies to every aspect of life. Only without fear can one act with the purpose and determination inherent in the Way. People are afraid out of concern for their safety and well-being, but fear keeps them limited and it prevents them from realizing their full potential. Acceptance of death, which ought to entail the acceptance of all other outcomes, allows one to act freely and independently in the greatest fulfilment of whatever duty or goal they serve. 
Practicing martial arts and studying the writings of samurai like Miyamoto Musashi,  Yagyu Munenori, and Yamamoto Tsunetomo can aid those who seek to attain a quiet mind and harmony within themselves by revealing the Way through direct experience. Iulius-Cezar Macarie, who holds a Master of Science in psychology and practices Okinawan Goju-Ryu Karate-Do, analyses martial arts in a Jungian psychological perspective. “A turning point came when I began to look beyond the ego-related distractions such as participating in Karate-do competitions and the immediate results of physical training in Karate-do, and turned my focus on the psyche’s benefits from the practice of martial arts. It was then that I began to see glimpses of what the true meaning of training in the MAs could be. Namely, MAs could be an alternative way to a journey of personal realisation” (Macarie 7). Jungian psychology deals primarily with the transformation of the self through the exploration of eternal archetypes inherent in nature and the human condition. Macarie discusses“parallels between ki and Jung’s concept of psychic energy found in the wisdom tradition of Alchemy” and how Budo “cultivates self-awareness and the immovable spirit of a martial artist following the ‘middle path’ to reach inner peace and a ‘sense of balance’ in life and spirit” (11-12) One of the foremost principles of Alchemy is the bridging of dichotomies, of opposites, to see the world as one whole, and Jung viewed alchemy as a philosophical science of transformation through the discovery and application of unifying principles underlying seemingly disparate or opposed concepts. Miyamoto independently discovered the same principle of seeing the whole instead of separate, opposing, or disparate parts. “It is essential for those who are small to know what it is like to be large, and for those who are physically large to know what it is like to be small; whether you are physically large or small, it is essential to keep your mind free from subjective biases” (Musashi 25) When there are no opposites or separate parts, there is only the whole; the whole is the Way. Lao Tzu reflects this unifying principle, “Can you marry your spirit and body to the oneness and never depart from it?” (10) “Aimless, ambitionless, it might be called ‘small.’ Everything in existence returns to it, and still it doesn’t lord over anything. Thus it might also be called ‘great’” (34). Musashi, again, discovers this independently. “Knowing the small by way of the great, one goes from the shallow to the deep” (Musashi 12). Macarie suggests that “the fusion between body, mind, and spirit is what gives a sense of balance and an overall growth to the martial artist” (18). Martial arts gives a sense of wholeness by unifying dichotomies within the self. 
The counter to this path of unifying one’s self and finding peace and harmony is the normal perspective of people in the world. Always they are torn between trying to do what they think is right, what they think benefits them, what they think others want them to do, what they think will keep them safe–all things difficult to know with certainty. This uncertainty leads to great confusion and hesitation. This confusion and hesitation is what causes people to suffer, and it is what caused samurai throughout the centuries to fail in unswervingly serving their lord. Their mind is full of thoughts, ideas, perspectives, and so they err from the simplicity of the Way. “The normal mind keeps nothing in the heart, but lightly relinquishes the past, so that the heart is empty and therefore is the normal mind” (Munenori 156). By “normal” Munenori refers to the unconscious mind, or a mind which is not consciously attempting to do anything (120-121). Emptiness of mind (or heart) in this sense is synonymous to singularity of unerring purpose. When one’s purpose or intention is clear, there is no need for conscious thought or consideration. Bruce Lee understood the philosophical implications of martial arts in regards to self-growth. During the filming of Enter the Dragon, the producers wanted to make a mindless action movie and remove the philosophical aspects of it. Bruce Lee completely refused to even enter the set for two weeks until the producers relented and allowed the philosophy to remain in the film. Bruce Lee made self-growth his life goal, and all that he did, he did to change himself into a better being. Miyamoto Musashi had the same outlook, writing “Determine that today you will overcome your self of the day before” (47). Lao Tzu encourages us also to “work towards emptiness and openness” (16). “Knowing others is intelligence; knowing the self is enlightenment. Conquering others is power; conquering the self is strength. Die without dying, and you’ll endure forever” (33). Bruce Lee wrote in his personal diary, “I realize the dominating thoughts of my mind will eventually reproduce themselves in outward, physical action, and gradually transform themselves into physical reality; therefore I will concentrate my thoughts for 30 min. daily upon the task of thinking of the person I intend to become, thereby creating in my mind a clear mental picture” (Lee notebook). Tsunetomo reflects this inherent martial art of timeless concentration on one’s objectives in order to accomplish them gradually. “Like an inchworm, it is simply a matter of advancing forward, little by little” (40). “All that matters is having single-minded purpose (ichinen), in the here and now. Life is an ongoing succession of ‘one will’ at a time, each and every moment. A man who realizes this truth need not hurry to do, or seek, anything else anymore. Just live in the present with single-minded purpose. People forget this important truth, and keep seeking other things to accomplish” (139-140). Lao Tzu, however, may have captured it best: “Meet the difficult while it is still easy; cross the universe one step at a time. Because the sage doesn’t try anything too big, she’s able to accomplish many big things” (63). Looking to the future and seeing the size of a task often dissuades people from attempting it, but those who follow the Way simply begin to do it, and gradually they progress. By acting according to the simple desire of the heart, one can accomplish a little at a time in pursuit of one’s intention. As a result, those who follow the Way can accomplish great tasks with ease.
 Practitioners of martial arts are encouraged to focus singlemindedly on the objective at hand. In this age of multitasking and distraction, who would normally attempt such a thing? As one cultivates single-mindedness through the practice of martial arts, one can apply the same focus to other areas of one’s life, to one’s duties, one’s goals, one’s well-being, forsaking the days when once one was tossed about upon the waves like a ship in a tempest.
At any time, you exist. Fears and worries, hesitations and confusion, they are all illusions based on things that no one can know. We can only know what is now. By always living in the future, or dwelling on the past, we forsake the gift of the present and fail in our duties to ourselves and others. “[Budo cultivates] self-awareness and harmony that make martial artists more spiritually aware, therefore aiding their self-realisation” (Macarie 18). By practicing martial arts and studying the writings of samurai, we can discover the natural flow of things within ourselves, and this discovery bleeds out into the world around us, as the pattern, the Way, underlies both martial arts and all else. Martial arts offers a seeker of the Way a concrete system through the earnest adherence of which they can discover the still emptiness within themselves and become open to the expression of the natural law, the Way that manifests in all that surrounds them.
