When discussing Genre theory, the question of definition often arises. Literary theorists constantly encounter the issue of distinction in terms of Genre theory. Genre, meaning kind, or class, occludes itself in the pursuit of determining where one class or kind ends and another begins. Can works of literature be classed at all?[2] It may seem simple at first but soon the problem makes itself evident when considering interpretation. Interpretation of a text, or any work of art, can give rise to various themes, definitions, and elements. For example, the cinematic world officially categorizes The Wicker Man, starring Nicolas Cage, as an horror but those familiar with Nicolas Cage’s distinct acting style likely view the film as a comedy. A more well known example, perhaps, would be Mark Twain’s Huckleberry Finn. To a child, Huckleberry Finn reads as an adventure story, especially if the child knows little of American history. Beyond the simplicity of a child, the conceptions, perceptions, and previous knowledge of the reader directly affects the interpretation, and therefore the classification of the work of literature. Genre simplifies itself in narrow definition, such as allying with the author’s intention. However, once reader response, at the most basic, or various postmodern styles of analysis, such as post-colonialism, apply themselves to the work, the question of the genre of the work opens itself to the infinite Other discussed in Jean François Lyotard’s “Something Like: ‘Communication . . . Without Communication’.”[4] The most accurate interpretation of practical genre application in literary theory is that definition of genre ultimately rests entirely upon the observer, receiver, or reader of the work of art, literature, or elsewise.[5] 
        The Other mentioned by Lyotard finds its origins with Immanuel Kant’s philosophical concept of schema,[3][4] which were not entities unto themselves but rather methods by which certain effects are produced. Kant says in his First Analogy, “This mediating representation must be pure, that is, void of all empirical content, and yet at the same time, while it must in one respect be intellectual, it must in another be sensible. Such a representation is the the transcendental schema.”[3] For Kant, there necessitated an existence of a third, intermediary miasma or ether, which provided a common foundation into which a creator may venture and out of which the audience may draw. The concept of schema relates itself to Genre theory by providing a working model upon which the question of method to create and define genre may be addressed. Kant desired to prove the existence of methods, schemas, by which might consistently be produced classifiable works.[3] Unfortunately, Kant encountered the same impossibility of classification in his attempts to classify a method by which classifications could be made.
        Culture plays a significant role in genre studies due to context. An American writing a poorly researched story on Italian culture with an intended tone of seriousness and drama might have his work grouped by actual Italians into the genres of comedy, farce, and offense, depending on their reception of the American author’s attempt at capturing their culture. For the same reasons of perceived inaccuracy, history textbooks fall into two distinct and contradicting categories simultaneously. If a history textbook might be making the argument that the purpose of the North fighting the South in the Civil War was to end slavery, Southerners with Confederate sympathies would easily classify the history textbook into the genre of fiction, simply as a matter of their emotions and perspective, despite the inherent classification of history textbooks as nonfiction. Even with the genre of nonfiction, the applicable definition obscures itself, for the genre of nonfiction has no effect on one who does not believe the classification. To exacerbate the difficulty of definition within genre, the majority of genre studies in the realm of literary theory deal with fictional works.
        In regards to the history textbook’s genre classification of nonfiction, in the impossibility of total accuracy, can the genre of nonfiction, or any genre, for that matter, even exist? In “Genre”, Philippe Lacoue-Labarthe and Jean-Luc Nancy discuss the difficulties of genre, using Romanticism as an example. They say most aptly, “The genre of Romantic poetry is still in process of becoming; it is its true essence to be always only becoming and never to be capable of completing itself.”[4] Such is the case with all genres, due to that genre bases itself entirely on subjective human interpretation. As humans and human culture evolve, so, too, must their perceptions evolve. With the impossibility of completely definite classification due to the human im-perception of objective absolutes, the classification of a work into a genre depends entirely upon a subjective reference, in effect rendering the classification temporary, obsolete, and useless in regards to the permanence sought after in literary theory.[5] It seems there lacks any complete universal approach to the development of genre, and one has to wonder whether when the lines begin to blur between one work of literature and the next, do the boundaries of works cease to bleed into each other? and if so, when? If one attempts to locate the boundary of one genre in relation to another, they will find an impossible task.[1]
        In “At what Point is a Pizza no Longer a Pizza or a Book no Longer a Book? A Look at how new uses for Traditional Genres are Redefining Audience Expectation.” Trisha Capinsky discusses the mixing of genres and non-traditional pairings as opposed to traditional pairings, or mediums, of relaying messages. The importance of Capansky’s paper to genre theory in literary criticism is its perspective on communication. Capansky classifies traditional pairings as forms of communication deemed socially example. For example, a traditional pairing, or medium, to relay condolence for a loved one’s death would be a card sent with some flowers. A non-traditional pairing, on the other hand, might be condolences sent through the medium of a clown-o-gram.[1] This mind experiment relates itself to Kant’s schema, exemplifying that there seems to be within the subjectivity of one’s culture some guiding principles to accurately relay a message or emotion.[3] However, there exist many exceptions. A cynic, for example, might welcome the novelty of the clown, having grown tired of the emotionless formalities of flowers and cards, thus making the clown-o-gram the more effective means of relaying the message. However, the intention of the one who sent the clown would rarely be initially considered, rendering the application of genre entirely dependent on the receiver’s response.[5]
        The principle and murderous deficit of genre-theory manifests itself in the definition of humans attempting to place limitations on the limitless. Genre effectually relays the effort of mankind’s ineffectual pulling and placing of the infinite Other into referential filing cabinets. The one attempting to do this finds that which he pulls from the infinite to have no definable substance; with enough effort and accurate perception, he finds that he himself is indistinguishable from the infinite Other. If that man or woman can cope and recover from the inevitable existential crisis accompanying such a realization, he or she might come to a peaceable understanding that everything and anything represents the infinite and therefore cannot be limited to any satisfying boundary of itself. Thus, in effect, aside from its ability to express the awareness of its own futility, the epiphany of non-definition, and the consequent understanding of nothingness, Genre-theory renders itself obsolete.
