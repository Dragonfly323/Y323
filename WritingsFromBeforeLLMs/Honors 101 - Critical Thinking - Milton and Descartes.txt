Critical Thinking:
Milton’s Obstinate Obedience to Authority and Descartes’ Objective Operation of Analyses


If the history of thought split into two schools, those schools would teach ancient and modern thinking. In accordance with the Neoplatonic Great Chain of Being, the ancient way of thinking reflects stasis, order, caution. The old way of thinking also adheres to the Concept of Plenitude, the belief that God ordained a set of natural laws to govern the world when he first created the world. The ancient philosophy of thought finds fulfillment in the acceptance of the present moment, beauty in order and proportion, and a disdain for chaos and disorder. This form of thought relies heavily upon the use of authority, to base arguments upon what has already been said, and sees breaking new conceptual ground as negative as it alters the existing state of affairs. Modern thinking, rife with romanticism and innovation, rejects that status of any authority naturally equal to truth and instead relies upon reason and observation to derive its beliefs. The modern elevates reason to a position of supremacy, while the ancient does not value reason above contemporarily accepted truths. The romantic aspect of modern thinking elevates nature, and thus raw human emotion,  to the status of divine and views civilization, mankind’s breaking away from nature, as inherently flawed.  The romantic view, a central aspect of modernity, praises and values both the natural state of man and man’s faculty of reason, and holds a belief in the transcendence of man through attunement with nature. There resides a simple difference between ancient and modern thought; ancient thought adapts reasoning to truths, but modern thought finds truth in reasoning.
        The advent of Descartes brightly marks the transition from ancient precepts to modern rationalizing. Descartes views order as sublime, as evidenced by his allegory of the city built by a single builder. As opposed to a city built over time by myriad unique designers, a city built by one master architect would have remarkable order, symmetry, and, in consequence, beauty. However, despite his appreciation of orderly beauty, he holds to the old ways. Descartes rebels against the ancient way of thinking taught to him in school through classical education. In contrast to his holding of age-old values in reference to aesthetic beauty, he abandons the foundation of authority and embarked alone to discover truth. Descartes desires to understand the world and existence as an individual, a blatant act of self-realization, wishing to eclipse, or at least equal, Aristotle. However, despite desiring the status of an original thinker by his own means, he remains true to the ancient methodology of using science, logic, and reason to arrive at deducible truths. If he thought as a true romantic thinks, he may have at once thrown himself into chaotic darkness, trusting in the laws of nature to bring him out of murky philosophical swamps.
        Instead, Descartes entrusts himself entirely to his own faculty of reason, rejecting existing philosophies, dogma, and doctrine. Though he desired to commence unadulterated by outside influence, Descartes fails on account that he refuses to abolish the foundation of man as the pinnacle of nature. In other words, he thinks too highly of mankind, for he places man above the rest of nature on no purely logical foundation for doing so. Though he bases his reasoning on observations of the natural world, he imposes a perception grown from his own preconceptions as to man’s place in nature and consequently judges the difference between man and animal erroneously, in a logical light. He reasons that man differs from animals in that man can communicate, while animals cannot. He reasons that no utterance of beast on earth or fowl in air or fish in sea communicates anything intelligent and thus concludes man superior. Descartes fails to renounce all prior held notions, as a romantic thinker ought to, and maintains the Church espoused belief in the elevation of superior humanity over base animals, filtering the objective nature of existence through his subjective perception of reality. As a result, he remains greatly unaware of the intricacies of nature which later science will prove. He believes all of this in accordance with the Neoplatonic Great Chain of Being, which has been embedded in his subconscious mind through the influence of his cultural and intellectual surroundings. How great a hold subtle culture and upbringing have upon a man’s mind!
        Milton differs from Descartes in that he finds himself perfectly comfortable in the confines of previous thoughts, as he adheres to the ancient way of thinking, depending on authority and a hierarchical view of existence to reason matters out. In Paradise Lost, Milton attempts to reconcile the popular perception of God with the problem of pain, and how imperfection can exist under the sovereignty of perfection. Through allegory in Paradise Lost, Milton represents Satan’s taking advantage of humanity’s free will and Eve’s bringing about of sin after succumbing to Satan’s temptations as the cause for all pain and suffering. Though humanity falls, mankind maintains a great gift in the form of reason: “in the Soule / Are many lesser Faculties that serve / Reason as chief”. Milton praises reason but only reason rightly used. In Paradise Lost, through the abuse of reason, or rather, false reason, Eve justifies disobeying God and eating the forbidden fruit. However, overall, Milton still holds reason in high regard, giving him common ground with Descartes.
        Descartes and Milton differ most in exactly how highly each respectively regards reason. For Descartes, reason reigns over his thoughts as a god, his only authority in his pursuit of truth. He has forsaken all that has come before him, believing truth must necessarily exist purely within the human consciousness, and holds reason as the key to accessing that truth. Milton, however, believes in the virtue of reason only when submitted to the will of God. His example of the Fall of Mankind shows what happens when reason does not coalign to the will of God. Milton uses his own reason to attempt to explain the contemporary view of the universe, which held the Neoplatonic Great Chain of Being as one of its foremost tenets. In opposition, Descartes rejects the idea of manipulating reason to fit held doctrine in effort to give said doctrine more credibility, for such pursuits adulterate legitimate philosophy and confuse the veritable nature of existence in the perception of the seeker. Descartes instead attempts to use his reason to arrive at truths through pure logic, the base of which he believes infallible, having been given directly by God, holding no preconceived notions of truth aside from the concept that such truths manifest self-evidently. In metaphor, to obtain ultimate truth, Milton pours reasoning into the molds of assumed truths, resulting in an oft time defective product, whereas Descartes creates molds according to reason and logic, obtaining results more excellent than the assumed, or guessed, mold of Milton’s school of thought, and pours the molten metal of existence into said reasoned molds to obtain a more accurate symbol or statue of truth. Despite Descartes’ superiority of method, his technique lacks finality as he must hold beforehand some conception or direction in which he desires to reason. In conclusion, Milton looks to the classic style of ancient thought, resists change, and instead steadfastly holds to cherished beliefs of his religion and the surrounding culture, while Descartes, an original and modern thinker, embodies the epitome of change, rejects the opinions of others both who came before and contemporary in effort to achieve originality, and stakes new territories into the realm of philosophy.
