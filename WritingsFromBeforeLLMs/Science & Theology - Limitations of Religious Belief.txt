The Limitations of Religious Belief


Though it seems that we all can accept that some ultimate truth exists, we fall immediately into disagreement about what that ultimate truth is. There are more than a few ways to go about looking for that truth, but for simplicity let them be the four ways of thinking, feeling, both thinking and feeling, and neither thinking nor feeling. Adherents to one method normally assume the others as flawed, or else they would use one other than their own. On the other hand, each person having their own way of going about matters, certain methods suit certain people. Any method is adequate in its pure form. However, much stands in the way of these pure forms.
Each of the four methods commonly suffers from the impediment of belief. Belief in this sense is any perspective or perception that one places upon that which they experience. Examples would include a theologian looking at a tree and believing it fashioned by an intelligent creator or an atheist looking at a tree and believing it the culmination of random occurrences of natural law. Both the theologian and the atheist rely on either emotion, thought, or both in maintaining their respective beliefs. Those beliefs hold the same truth value in respect to ultimate truth in that, by themselves, they supply no foundation of observable and irrefutable evidence. The atheist can explain to the theologian the natural laws that the universe followed to produce the tree, only for the theologian to ask the atheist from where the natural laws originally came. The atheist must admit that they do not know, but will reply that any lack of knowledge concerning such a matter does not preclude the existence of an intelligent creator. So both remain in an endless circle of debate, for neither perception has greater truth value than the other, and neither can be proven or disproven in respect to an ultimate truth, as for which the theologian claims God and the atheist claims anything but God.
In the presence of pure rationality, pure thought, with or without feeling, beliefs hold zero truth value in relation to an absolute truth, and thus no belief or belief system, no perception or perspective, can make any claim to absolute and ultimate truth. Christianity believes that there is a creator god just as Atheism believes that there is not a creator god. Neither belief can ever win, as they debate with equal backing the existence or non-existence of an immaterial entity. They differ in that Atheism makes no claim to an absolute, ultimate truth while Christianity does. The great portion of Christianity claims absolutely that there is a god, the God,  both omnipotent and good in nature. Consequently, the problem of evil spares the foundation of Atheism, but Christianity must answer for it. Can Christianity logically believe that God, both omnipotent and good, attributes unquestioned, allows the existence of evil in the world?
        In regards to ultimate truth, the Christians have already failed the grounds of logic and rationality by claiming that a certain belief represents the ultimate truth. Christianity also falls short on less than ultimate rationality and logic. For sake of simplicity, if evil is harm done by one human being to another, God, if good, would not allow harm to come upon any sentient entity and, if omnipotent, would have the power to enforce His will. Yet evil remains. Many answers readily present themselves, such as that God is either not good or not omnipotent, or that evil does not exist in the world. However, Christianity can compromise none of these three, as the Christian worldview relies on the perspective of a fallen humanity in a state of total depravity, a state so permanent that only an omnipotent being can alter it, and that the omnipotent being redeems humanity only because of the goodness of the omnipotent being’s will and desire. So the question arises as to why God allows evil in the first place. If truly good and omnipotent, God has no reason to, and indeed cannot, allow evil.
        Over time, Christianity has put forth a few furtive answers to the problem of evil, none quite substantial. The most common, the free will answer, reasons that mankind caused evil to exist in the world through an act of free will, or that the existence of free will requires evil as a condition, a necessity to allow for choice. The problem of evil persists, however, as an omnipotent, and therefore omniscient, god would not only have to have allowed the potential of evil but also to have foreseen that the advent of evil would definitely occur. An omnipotent and good God cannot not knowingly allow evil to come to fruition; to do so constitutes an overwhelming contradiction. At this point, Christianity usually throws its hands in the air and assumes that certain things about life one simply cannot know, or that “my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, saith the Lord.” (Isaiah 55:8, KJV) The free will argument represents a classic case of Orwellian doublethink, in which one simultaneously accepts two contradictory statements as true. The reasoning behind the acceptance of the paradox derives from an interpretation of the verse quoted above, that humanity cannot know the workings of God, on all counts a lazy and unconvincing argument. This answer fails to justify a paradoxical belief in the existence of a good and omnipotent god alongside the existence of evil.
        The Christians also present the necessary evil argument. The necessary evil argument, in short, implies that God, as an all knowing and all powerful entity, causes or allows a chain of events in which evil actions performed by humans lead to good results. This argument acts as an addendum to the free will argument but provides no additional substance. An omnipotent god would not need to use evil in order to achieve what humanity considers good, the lack of human ill will. God’s use of evil would also conflict with God’s inability to perform evil. As a supposedly omnipotent being, God’s allowing evil to occur represents either a negation of His good nature, as He would be compelled to stop it, or of His omnipotence, if He lacked the capability to stop it. Furthermore, the natural world acts, beyond a reasonable doubt, on predictable and constant laws. If God acted in the present moment, foregoing the Deist perspective of an absent God that set everything in motion in the beginning, in order to not violate the laws of nature God could only act upon the human mind, which action would conflict with free will, a central tenet of mainstream Christianity.
While the belief itself is not logically sound, it is not irrational to accept the possibility, even despite logic, that a good, omnipotent god allows evil. The key word is possibility, for, as with Agnosticism, with matters so difficult to know for certain, the best choice resides in not making a choice. It is irredeemably irrational, however, to believe that any religious belief holds more truth than any of the other innumerable religious beliefs that dance upon the world, as they all primarily rely on emotion and thus have the same strength of foundation. In other words, the acceptance of the possibility that a belief is true is not irrational but the assumption of a belief as immutably true is irrational, especially in the absence of sound, non-paradoxical logic and observational evidence. In terms of absolute truth value, beliefs can only go so far as likely true. The Christian belief of a good, omnipotent god and an evil world lack both sound logic and observational evidence. In regards to definite truth, one can only rationally assume that all beliefs bear equal potential in representing an ultimate truth, and that no emotionally, or non logically, founded belief holds greater truth value than other similarly founded beliefs, as beliefs are nothing more than perceptions laid by the will of the observer upon the reality before them. Beliefs founded on anything other than empirical evidence and sound logical reasoning all have an equally low likelihood of representing definite truth. Therefore, the belief of a good, omnipotent, yet evil-allowing god is legitimate as a possibly true belief, despite its failure in logic, as long as other beliefs are also considered as having an equal possibility of representing truth. Claiming the belief as exclusively and absolutely true, however, is illogical and irrational.
