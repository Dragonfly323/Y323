








Limitless



















“Perhaps this book will be understood only by someone who has himself already had the thoughts that are expressed in it–or at least similar thoughts. The whole sense of the book might be summed up in the following words: what can be said at all can be said clearly, and what we cannot talk about we must pass over in silence. Thus the aim of the book is to draw a limit to thought, or rather–not to thought, but to the expression of thoughts. It will therefore only be in language that the limit can be drawn, and what lies on the other side of the limit will simply be nonsense.”
-Ludwig Wittgenstein
From Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, 1921










        Ludwig Wittgenstein was obsessed with finding the absolute truth of things. A good friend of Bertrand Russell, he developed a fondness for logic. Fondness is not the right word–it was an obsession. When he first met Russell, they both were enamored with finding a logical basis for mathematics. While Russell worked diligently on set theory, Wittgenstein, a young man at the time, grew displeased with the insufficient claims of set theory. Wittgenstein demanded an unquestionable absolute. He left Russell, not out of spite, for they remained close friends, but to rid himself of distractions in his search for the fundamental. He lived in Norway when he first heard of the conflict that history would call “The Great War”.
        Wittgenstein joined the war out of patriotism, but at some point came to the conclusion that, through experience of the battlefield, he could truly strip away all that held him back from experiencing the absolute. He volunteered for the front lines, akin to a suicide mission at the time, and took the most dangerous missions, notably to direct artillery fire at the front lines. All alone on the battlefield in noman’s land, Wittgenstein huddled behind cover as artillery rounds exploded around him. Sometime during that war, he looked up at the sky and saw it for the first time. He found the absolute, and his search for the fundamental ended.
        When Wittgenstein first journeyed toward the absolute, I do not know. But I took my first steps at the age of eleven. My schooling as a child indoctrinated me in a rigid set of beliefs dealing with the nature of the world and humanity’s place in it. However, the indoctrination did not set deep teeth in my head, for I knew silence and freedom when at home. At home, I ran naked through the forests and wallowed in the mud, coming to know an Earth free of humanity’s mark. It was only I in the world in those days. And my parents, though Christian, loosely followed a more relaxed denomination, so they did not reinforce the fundamentalism of my schooling. I walked through a mirror in those early days, and every evening I would walk back and see the world a different light, however subtle. My rompings in the woods and the silence of my empty house taught me a natural and passionate curiosity, so when I found my grandfather’s forbidden book, a swarthy tome of an esoteric nature, I couldn’t help myself.
        The name of the book shall remain unmentioned, but the contents spoke in symbols. In that book I learned that symbols represented things and ideas unseen, that, for reasons of secrecy or enticement, one could not write directly. That book, my first teacher, taught me to look deeply where I would not think to otherwise look. I did not read the entirety of the book, I admit, but I followed a great man’s advice, “The art of reading consists in remembering the essential and forgetting the non essentials.” I needed to learn the importance of symbols, and so that book fed me.
        As years went by, I continued reading and learning, always looking for some way to draw closer to that inexpressible entity that dwelled behind symbols. I could not reach it. I did not know then that the wall enclosed me, and not the absolute. But I continued to learn, and found my way to alchemy, which I first began practicing in high school. No! I did not buy an alembic and a flask. I quickly realized that Alchemy also spoke in symbols. Lead represented the walled in soul, while gold represented the transformed persona. The philosopher’s stone represented gnosis, or perfected knowledge, and the elixir of life represented the natural result. Alchemy taught me what I had to do in order to reach the absolute. Then, I discovered Jung.
        Jung, a prominent alchemist himself, and a psychologist deserving of far more praise and recognition than his contemporary Freud, wrote a book he called Liber Novus, or The Red Book, due to its red leather binding. Jung’s psychology focused on symbols and he researched extensively what he called “the collective unconscious”, which might loosely be described as a chaotic amalgamation of being that gives birth to archetypes, or Platonic true forms of psychological manifestations, more or less. He was not a simple man. The Red Book was not published until 2009, due to its intimate nature, for it details Jung’s autobiographical account of conversing with the collective unconscious. 
We all share a part in the collective unconscious, or else why the word “collective”? The collective unconscious hides within each of us, or rather is us, and thus hides by using symbols, for we are often too afraid to face it directly. Jung wanted to understand whether the collective unconscious was a separate entity or some manifestation of our physical selves. In this pursuit, he bared himself to his own subconscious mind, and, well, you’ll just have to read the book. I followed Jung’s lead, and took what steps he took. More or less, I talked to myself, but for the first time, I talked to my ‘self’, my true self, in pure unbridled consciousness. In those days, in many ways, I died. And so began the alchemical process.
From those days when first I gazed deeply into the abyss, I walked now forever free, in a way. I no longer held belief of anything or any kind. But it was not the end. I had further yet to go. Before, my rigidity excluded me; now, gentle rain had softened the wall around my soul. Those days were like a dream. I wandered about increasingly aware of the fact that my own perception of the world was itself a symbol, and that the true world lay beneath. At this same time, just as Wittgenstein had fled to logic to search for truth, so did I, and the truth was simple, but not so simple to understand–that all things really were one thing. My perception of the world split it into two, that of the world and that of my perception of it, so I therefore did not understand the truth, where the two become one–the absolute.
My days continued, till one day in August, when I was driving home from my brother’s. Well, I cannot say whether I continued past that day. I thought deeply as I drove, as I often do, and in one brief moment of light, my thoughts fled me, and I saw the world, and I felt the world, and my perception was gone, and the walls about my soul had crumbled, and there I lay bare in existence for the first time in my conscious life, and so have I ever since. The alchemical process was complete. But I was dismayed, for now I was alone. I walked among the earth, as part of the earth, but no one walked with me. And I could not tell anyone of my discovery. I had encountered the same problem that Wittgenstein did when he came to the absolute, as does everyone who comes to the absolute.
During The Great War, Wittgenstein began work on Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, a book on logic and an homage to Spinoza’s Tractatus Theologico-Politicus, a book in which Spinoza equates God with nature (a claim that Spinoza later proves through mathematical logical). Wittgenstein searched for the absolute, and he found it, but he could not put it into words, though he made his best attempt with Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus. He interpreted his experience and logic as that the world was made of facts, but that some absurd facts cannot be spoken and must be passed over in silence. With his Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, Wittgenstein claimed to have solved all the world’s philosophical problems. And he did, just as Spinoza before him, and Lucretia before him, and Lao Tzu before him, but no one could fully understand. The truth is not a limitedly communicable idea, they all, and I, discovered, but is something inherent in existence, and thus undefinable.
        The absolute, which Wittgenstein sought so passionately, gave itself to him. However, he found he could not give it to others. This vexed him, as it has vexed many others who encounter the absolute. All words are symbols, and symbols do nothing by themselves, but must be understood in order to have meaning. Most come to realize that they can only speak of the absolute with others who also know it. But those who know are rare, for the absolute does not come softly in your sleep, but through strife, or longing, or sadness, or whatever it takes to dismantle the walls that envelop our souls from birth. So, the most significant experience in my life is one I can never tell anyone, not in a way they would understand. Wittgenstein and I found what we sought–the absolute, the truth, the fundamental–and as souls of curiosity and passion, to not be able to share is a sadness, but a pain borne, and in the end it will carry us home.
