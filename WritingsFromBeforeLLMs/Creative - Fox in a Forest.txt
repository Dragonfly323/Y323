Peeling wall paper greeted Ray every morning. The dim gray light of dawn exposed the molding plaster behind. At night, the leaking radiator kept the fox up, staring at her water stained ceiling. The drip filled the silence that came in the wee hours that the traffic left behind. She listened to it till the car engines drowned it out and she stared at that peeling wallpaper every morning. She felt like it was slowly peeling away the thin veneer of her soul. She never left her apartment till after nine, and even then, rarely. The creditors came unannounced but never after nine. She hid when they banged on her door and called out her name. “Miss Eldritch!”, they’d yell. “Miss Eldritch, open up!” It’d been six months since her boyfriend left her there, six months of debt and hiding. 
One day, Ray stood on the ledge of her window. A fat, crossed-eyed pigeon flew past her and landed on a bus stop sign by the road, three stories down, like an omen from some forgotten god. Ray stumbled back and fell through the window, landing on the marred wooden floor inside. She sat in stunned silence. In that moment of inspiration, she jumped to her feet. She packed her clothes, her eyes dancing frantically around the room, making sure that she forgot nothing important, but the search only reminded her of how little she owned. She donned a grey hoody worn thin from use, grabbed her bag, and ran down the stairs, out of the complex, and to the bus stop. She got on the awaiting bus and never looked back.
#
        Ray arrived at the central bus station and looked around. The large open space of frightened her after months of seclusion in her tiny apartment, but some small spirit of adventure stirred in her heart, making futile war against the conditioned helplessness that had ruled her life. No, it was not adventure that spurned her on, but desperation. She couldn’t live in the city anymore and death frightened her. Pressures from life and death propelled her to lower densities. Simple physics dictate that much. She had nothing left there anyway. 
The ticket cost most of her stashed savings. Ray found her bus and got on, the acrid smell of people and metal filling her sensitive nose as she went to the back to sit in a corner on a torn faux leather seat, leaning her head against the smudged plexiglass windows. She closed her eyes and listened to the hum of the idling diesel engine beneath her; it lulled her to sleep long before it shifted into gear.
#
        “Miss?” the bus driver rung the bell. His drooping hound eyes looked at her through the mirror. “This is the last stop.”
        Ray raised herself groggily off the seat from the best sleep she’d had in weeks. The fur on her face was matted down and felt sticky. She’d drooled. “Wha?” She said, looking around and seeing she was the only one on the bus.
        “It’s a changing stop. I can take you back to the last city, if you’d like.” The driver said sympathetically. He’d seen runaways before.
        Ray rubbed her eyes with her black paws and yawned. They were at some bus stop in the middle of a forest, the middle of nowhere. She stood up and went to the front. “No, here is fine. Thank you.”
        She was about to get off when the bus driver stopped her. “Have you got any food? There’s only two buses what come here. Might be hours before you get to the next town.”
        The bus stop looked like an art piece. A shelter, a bench, a sign, a payphone, like some comment on the solitude that comes with travel. Ray shook her head. “No, but I’ll be alright.” She was used to not eating much. She thought he might have been frowning, but she could never tell with hounds. They always looked sad.
        He reached beneath his seat and pulled out a package of bean crackers. “It’s not much, Miss, but I hope you’ll take it.”
        It reminded her that she was hungry, after all. “Thanks.” She muttered, taking the crackers and putting them in her bag. She turned to leave again.
        “Wait.” He said.
She waited silently.
        The bus driver looked at her. “Do you have money for the next bus? There’s nothing here for you otherwise. I mean to make good on my offer to take you back if you need it.”
        “I have money.” She lied and stepped off the bus.
The hound grimaced helplessly. “Take care, then, miss.” He closed the doors and, with a hiss, the bus rode away, leaving Ray alone at the deserted bus stop.
        The towering pines around her made her feel small, but secure too. The pines were alive. They were ancient and old and alive. The city animals could never build such magnificent things that took so many hundreds of years to grow. She basked for a moment in the experience. Her face lit up with a smile and she turned away from the road, setting off into the virgin forest. 
        #
        “Goddamnit!” Ray fell back in shock. That thing just moved! She stared at the strange animal that had been sitting in a tree. It was sitting in the tree, as if the 500 year old tree had grown around it. The creature’s eyes popped open at Ray’s outburst.
        “Oh” it said in a raspy, honeyed voice. “Oh, ho ho ho, quite some time. Quite some time.”
Ray gathered her wits and ran behind a nearby boulder. She had been in the forest less than a day and something like this happens. She felt the stir of adventure again in her heart, a bit stronger this time. Her cool head pushed it away. It’s no debt collector. She breathed a sigh of relief at the thought. But what is it?
        “Now, now.” the creature said, leaving a creature shaped hole in the tree as it got out. It was twice as tall as Ray. “No need to be afraid, little fox. You’ve left all your fears behind, yes?
        “Wha-” Ray breathed.
“I see everything that happens in this forest, and most everything in the world. You are safe here.” The creature smiled kindly and stood waiting.
        Ray carefully creeped around the boulder on all fours, keeping her distance, always ready to flee. She circled the odd, silent thing until concluding that it wasn’t going to hurt her. She approached and sat on her haunches in front of it. She cocked her head to the side, peering at the patiently smiling figure. “What are you?”
        “I am an animal.” It said, sitting down cross legged. “Just like you. Although, I am far older, even, than these trees around us. I have watched fifty generations of trees grow in this place.”
        “How can you be so old?”
“I am a human, and we were all once like you. We built cities and lights and cars. We always moved forward, making greater advances in the sciences till we created things that would seem to you as magic. Only, that time was not so far from where your time is now. If you all continue as you do, you shall reach in less than a hundred years the point where it happened to us.”
        “What happened?”
“We achieved immortality. Only, not everyone got it. I was one who did, one of the very very few. Then the Old Ones came. They came because we had discovered immortality. It seems the same will happen to your world as well. The moment your kind reaches immortality, the Old Ones will come again from out the deep seas. They will come and set the earth on fire.” The human shook his head, “I do not know. I have thought all of this time and I still do not understand. I can stop wars and travel the stars but I do not understand the Old Ones.” He got up and went back to his tree and nestled himself inside.
        Ray looked around. “Alright. Well. What am I supposed to do?”
“You may live your life. You are safe here.” the man mumbled.
        “But the world’s going to end!”
“Do not worry, little fox. You will die before then.”
        “Hell, what a comforting thought!” Ray threw her arms up, exasperated.
“Shh, silly creature. Don’t you understand that you’re free? No more debt collectors, no more peeling wallpaper. Live here in the woods and die old.”
        And then Ray’s demeanor softened with realization. The human was right. She breathed in deeply of the forest air. The scent of moss, and flowers, and bark, and earth mingled in a symphony of life. She collapsed on the forest floor in contentment. “Thank you.” she smiled up at the sky. 
He smiled in his tree. “You’re welcome, Ray.” Then he mused, “You would die if I were not here to protect you from starvation and savage creatures. How odd that the tragedy of an entire species would one day come to save the life of a single fox?”
