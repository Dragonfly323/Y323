The invention of the steam engine and the resultant industrialization of Western and then global society caused two noteworthy effects in regards to cultural and societal evolution. Firstly, the massive population growth and separate work environments and jobs available in the cities created a divide in the way of life between those who lived in cities and those who lived in the more agrarian societies. While this divide can be exampled endlessly, common sense ought to suffice for the purpose of this paper. The second effect arose in the countermovement of the romantics. Among other things, a desire to reconnect with the natural world motivated these romantics and the general smog and “deadness” of cities, so well-captured by Wordsworth’s “The World Is Too Much With Us,” caused them to feel an emptiness not really experienced until that point in history, an emptiness caused by hitherto unknown separation from the natural world and the evolution of society into a vast and complex form. Nature represented simplicity.
        The culture currents begun in the early 19th century continue to evolve and will do so until they reach their inevitable end, a rebalancing with nature. In general, the divide of the city and country has continued and increased, but has softened in the abruptness of this divide, with society homogenizing to a great degree. While cities have grown more comfortable and accommodating to people on lower economic scales, most cities lack that primal connection to nature, despite however many parks they make and trees they plant; the noise and business of the city never leaves. Someone might love the city life or life in the country and much of that sentiment depends on that person’s birthplace. However, not to get into all the well-researched psychology of it all, people need breaks, and people nature.
        Movements in various aspects of society to regain a closer connection to nature have gained momentum increasingly over the last few years, with each year seeing a greater increase, on average, than the last. While many of these movements probably have not been entirely inspired by a desire to be closer to nature, the end-goal is the same. Upton Sinclair’s novel, The Jungle, provides a good example of various knowledge and desires that reach the end result of moving closer to nature. The horrid meat packing practices of the time (1906) raised cultural awareness of the quality, sanitation, and ethics of meat procurement for food. As people became increasingly aware of the conditions in the meat industry, some of them began to desire better treatment not only for the workers but also for the animals. While factory farms continue to produce massive quantities of meat in the modern day, the seeds sown by Sinclair and nurtured by various activists from that point on have resulted in an emphasis on preventing (excessive) animal cruelty in the procurement of meat.
        The New Age movement, long and convoluted in its history, has also helped promote an awareness and desire to be close to nature and move back to ‘natural’ ways of living. On multiple occasions and in many industries, this broad ranging movement has attacked, on ideological grounds, the practices of traditional, industrialized society. The movement has promoted ideas of ‘unplugging’ from mechanized/industrialized society and technology in an effort to find peace and live a more healthy life. The movement, while often in disagreement with scientific opinion, though rarely to any dangerous degree, promotes a spiritual mindfulness with the natural world at the center of one’s focus and being.
        A few decades ago, as more and more came to light regarding the factory farms of the time, and more recently concerning crops, people began to worry about the use of Genetically Modified Organisms (GMOs) and pesticides in the farming industry and the use of chemicals and hormones in the dairy and meat industries. According to these industries, the purpose of using these products and techniques served to increase yield, which, according to them, was a necessity to combat world hunger. As an aside, as we now know, some one-third to two-thirds of food mass actually goes to waste. Regardless, many people grew concerned about these practices of which they knew little. They did not stand upon sand, however, in their defiance of these industrialized practices. They argued with common sense, that humans had lived their entire existence up to this point largely without the use of these compounds used ‘artificially’ in the production of their food. They argued from a medical and scientific perspective as well, that not enough testing had been done on these practices to ascertain their safety to the American Public. Factory farms and industrial-scale crop producers have done little to address these concerns, instead choosing to fund scientific studies that agree with their line of view. While these concerns demonstrate more recent occurrences of the conflict between the ‘natural’ movement and industrialized society, the organic movement in particular began in the early 20th century in response to this cultural backlash against established food businesses and artificial fertilizers. 
        The organic movement stressed the idea of growing and producing food ‘naturally’. This movement initially only filled a niche market and due to the information limitations at the time failed to gain momentum until much later. In 1962 Rachel Carson published Silent Spring, a book detailing the environmental impacts of artificial fertilizers and pesticides like DDT. This book truly kickstarted in popular conscience the ‘natural’ movement, and various other cultural aspects dedicated to moving back to nature benefited from the scientific perspective offered in the book. In the 1960’s and 70’s, many attempted to move toward a more natural way of life, and formed communes devoted to living off the land. Most people in modernized society did not feel they had the luxury, if they even had the desire, to live in such totality with nature. Despite this, the emphasis on the environment, environmental protection, and connection to nature continued to grow.
        Influence from Eastern religions such as Buddhism and Hinduism began taking hold in the early 20th century, profoundly affected, or probably even gave birth to, the New Age movement, and variously spawned numerous other cultural ideas and social movements in the West, such as vegetarianism and veganism. From around the 1980’s on, various examples of environmental destruction and violations of human rights by large companies like Nestlé and United Fruit Company further heightened in the general populace’s mind the need to hold large corporations/businesses accountable and buy food more conscientiously. The dolphin free movement offers another example of these various small impacts, which all act like drops in the growing current of the movement back to nature. The dolphin free movement brought to people’s attention the grotesque amount of bycatch in industrialized fishing businesses. This bycatch included many animals held in high-regard by the people, such as dolphins, sharks, and creatures not eaten for food by people, but critical to the environmental health of the ocean.
        All of these happenings of the past one hundred years have bred within the West’s collective unconscious a quiet distrust of large businesses. Additionally, as a result of the organic movement, people have noticed a difference in quality between organic and non-organic products. The organic movement has blossomed in the past few years. Whole Foods, a retail chain specializing in organic foods, supports the notion that demand is increasing. Whole Foods, however, is notoriously expensive, and a stigma against organic foods as a whole is that they are more expensive. Still, its rise in popularity signifies a greater overall demand for organic and natural foods. For now, the market remains largely a novelty, but other indicators are present that support the move back toward nature.
        Fair-trade, Rainforest Alliance, single origin, and other such initiatives and concepts have grown markedly in popularity the past few years. Mostly commonly encountered in products such as chocolate, coffee, honey, and sugar, these initiatives and concepts signify an increasing amount of conscientiousness in consumers. While these products also often cost more than their regular counterparts, they reconcile the price difference in quality and ‘good vibes’ from paying an extra few dollars, especially if one knows of Nestlé’s child and slave labor scandals, which they still have failed to fully address. As more and more people buy organic and fair-trade, not only does their mistrust for the monopolized giants of the past era grow, but their appreciation for nature and environmental awareness often grow as well.
        The subject of coffee merits particular mention. Third-wave coffee roasters have recently grown exponentially in popularity. Third-wave coffee roasters specialize in small-batch roasts of coffee, often from single-origin coffee producers and farms, almost universally under fair-trade agreements, and even sometimes through direct contact with the farmers. All of these factors lead coffee consumers to a better experience of smell and taste in drinking coffee, but also stresses the notions of environmental protection, and how much the environment where a coffee is grown can affect the taste and quality of the coffee. This in turn leads to coffee consumers to feel a greater connection with the earth, one symptom of many signalling the desire to return to a closer relationship with nature.

In California, a bill is moving through the state government to legalize psilocybin, the active component in many psychoactive mushrooms. While not much research has been done in these substances, a great number of people are substantially interested in performing research as soon as they overcome the legal blockages and hurdles currently in place. Psilocybin, peyote, and plants used in ayahuasca, will all receive substantial research in the near future.
