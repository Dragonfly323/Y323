The Examined Life,
How Worthiness is Better than Goodness.        


To live a life unexamined is hardly to live at all. A person can live a good life without examining it, but it will lose its potential significance and meaning. An unexamined life, in which a person does not question themselves or learn about themselves, equates to a mere reflection of the environment. While reflecting environments can result in a good, so to say, life, that life lacks the individuality and independent thought that makes a life worth living. There is no life worth living apart from the examined life, as, while something does live, if unexamined, no soul lives that life.
Life happens, whether one likes it or not. Often, most go about allowing it to happen, without knowing that it happens. This, the unexamined life, foregoes the vast potential available to those who realize that it happens. When one realizes that life is happening, they have no choice but to examine it, as a consequence of awareness. This examination of life, the questioning of one’s self, one’s beliefs, one’s thoughts, allows the examiner to discard that which has played a part of them by mere coincidence. By so doing, one becomes more than the reflection, the reinforcer, of their environment, and instead becomes a dynamic entity of that environment, changing it, influencing it, affecting it.
Those who live as mere reflections of their environment do not exist as individuals, but rather as, in conjunction with all other reflections of that environment, the singular embodiment of that environment. One can see this clearly in more flavorful cultures, where one can find a swath of similar beliefs, attitudes, and thoughts. In such a culture, possessing identifying characteristics of that culture coincide with acceptance within that culture. In most cases, such acceptance comes also with protection and provision. To discard identifying characteristics, whether ideas, thoughts, appearances, or beliefs, runs the risk of losing partly or wholly the benefits of acceptance, provision, and protection. As so, the natural instinct remains to assimilate and not question one’s identity or mind. This is the unexamined life, a safe life, a quiet life, usually.
Many rightfully consider the questioning of one’s beliefs and ways of thinking as a dangerous and fruitless endeavor, for to do so can easily ostracize one from those whom one has from a young age called home and family. This effectively casts the examiner into the unknown, the most primal of human fears. However, not always does the abandoning of one’s home and family occur as evidence of the examined life, for one can more easily exchange one tribe, or culture, for another, more of a changing of allegiance than a true examination of one’s life. In theory, the one who examines their life, to the highest and purest form, will slowly alienate themselves from all traditionally considered human. As a result, they will find themselves wanderers without a home, unable to abide amongst those whom they would consider dead, and unaccepted by those same.
This tragedy, as some might see it, occurred at a slightly lesser degree to Socrates. Socrates’ refusal to abide by the social and cultural norms of his environment encountered rejection by those who refused not to abide by the social and cultural norms. Those people, who insisted on obedience, saw that not everyone insisted on obedience as did they, and that Socrates influenced others to question themselves. The obedient of Athens feared that Socrates’ influence would disrupt the order to which they adhered, and so rid the city of the foreign body, a body foreign in its refusal to assimilate to their ways and customs.
As those who question and examine their lives feel danger in regards to the order, or the culture to which they belong, so to does the order often feel danger in regards to the questioning individual. The order’s security, peace, and longevity all rely on the unity of the order, the faith that the order acts as one conglomerate whole. The fewer the order numbers its members, the less power the order has. In extreme cases, this dynamic results in the aggressive suppression of unorthodoxy, such as with Socrates, or with Giordano Bruno, or Benedictus de Spinoza. In regards to good and evil, one cannot always consider the order evil in these cases, for the order itself often acts in fear.
Benedictus de Spinoza grew up in the Netherlands in an orthodox community of Jews in the mid-1600s. As he grew older, he questioned the beliefs of his order, and relied on logic rather than tradition or authority. His ideas, which he communicated to his friends and fellow students, caused alarm within the community, partly for that they implied that God equates to the natural world. The leaders of the community themselves did not find excessive alarm personally, but rather as a result of the political climate at the time. The Jews maintained a tense relationship with the Christians, whose numbers and influence outweighed theirs greatly. Knowing the potentially heretical nature of Spinoza’s thought, the Jewish community excommunicated him, so that the Christians would not view Spinoza’s opinion as the opinion of the Jews as a whole. In such a case, clearly, the order, the Jewish community, acted out of fear and self preservation, rather than the intentional harming of the individual.
The good life, the safe life, the secure life, can well lie in conformity with the order. The worthy life, however, comes only through examination. To not examine one’s life renders one incapable of free will, in that one cannot act outside of the environment. Therefore, to truly live as an individual entity requires examination, understanding of what has come into one’s mind and why, and whether it serves to benefit one’s self or to harm it. Only by examination can one come to rule their own life and live as an independent soul.
Does there exist a good life separate from an examined life? Yes surely, but those who would achieve greatness never settle for good. Goodness, one thing. Greatness, an entirely other. Worthiness, the meat of all. One may live a good life; one may resign themselves to mediocrity. The great and worthy life, however, requires the abandonment of blind tradition and belief in favor of rationality, introspection, and dedicated thought. One cannot, if one wishes to live a worthy life, accept anything unproven, whether by belief, culture, tradition, or social expectation, but be always in suspension of belief, as Socrates strove to be, in perplexity.
