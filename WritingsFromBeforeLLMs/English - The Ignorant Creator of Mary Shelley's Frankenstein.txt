Literary Outcasts

The daughter of an anarchist, the wife of an atheist, and an educated woman in a patriarchal society, Mary Shelley faces social ostracization in all aspects of her life. While she has many dear and close friends, society as a whole excludes her. This exclusion, paired with society’s rejection of the thoughts of those revolutionaries around her, leads Mary Shelley to understand that they, the free-thinkers, are the monsters of society. The unconventional wisdom of her father, William Godwin, who believes that each member of society must learn to think for themselves, and of her husband, Percy Shelley, who sees organized religion as corrupting the rational thought of man, though filled with all the good intention of ones motivated by love, appear as horrendous and blasphemous rebellions against the established order. The thoughts do rebel, but they rebel against harm for the purpose of good. While perhaps the thought may have appeared earlier, when rainy weather causes the Shelleys and their friends to write horror stories as a way of passing time, Mary realizes the brilliant allegory of their own plight of free thought faced with a conformed society and the downfall of a benevolent creature on account of his appearance. Mary sees the thoughts of those around her as ultimately to the benefit of man, but rejected due to their appearance and schism of the established order. Mary Shelley writes Frankenstein, or the Modern Prometheus as an allegory for society’s rejection of benevolence based upon appearance and perception.
        Viktor Frankenstein represents the initiating influence upon the birth of a free thinker and the consequent abandonment or rejection of the free thinker when the influence can no longer control them. Percy, vocal and outspoken concerning his ideals and beliefs, certainly speaks to Mary about his days at Oxford and how they expelled him for what he saw as rational thought. While Percy gets along wonderfully with William Godwin, Mary’s father, and William loves Mary above all things, when Percy and Mary confess their love for each other, they face rejection, displeasure, and abandonment by William whom they both love so dearly. These events partly influenced Mary’s prose concerning the creation of the creature in her novel: “Unable to endure the aspect of the being I had created, I rushed out of the room…” (Shelley, 42). William Godwin, a free thinker, raises Mary to think freely as he does, but when her freethought exceeds his control and expectations, he rebels against her decisions. Mary, in her relationship with Percy, undergoes a kind of disowning by her father: she does not understand how he can love them separately but not together, and how he could reject her expression of the very free thought he always encouraged. Mary Shelley lives in no stranger’s abode from her father, and feels the absence of her father’s acceptance more deeply than her habituated, intellectual ostracization from contemporary society.
        Mary expresses her ingrained frustration with the paradigm of enlightened, freethinking individuals and society at large with the creature’s reaction to other’s judging him on his appearance. When he sees William, the creature muses that social and environmental conditioning causes the appearance based rejection he habitually experiences: “Suddenly, as I gazed on him, an idea seized me that this little creature was unprejudiced and had lived too short a time to have imbibed a horror of deformity. If, therefore, I could seize him and educate him as my companion and friend, I should not be so desolate in this peopled earth.” (Shelley, 122) Alas, in the creature’s eyes, Victor Frankenstein, the embodiment of conformed society, has already influenced William through association. The child rejects the creature and the creature, in emotional frustration, kills the child, a literary occurrence influenced by Mary Shelley’s own exasperation with her stepmother, Mary Jane Vial, whom she loathes on account of her willful ignorance. Further, the creature’s outburst typifies a common reaction of those alienated from society.
        Despite the destruction wrought by the creature, Mary Shelley writes with an attitude of love. The creature explains how he suffered in all the crimes he committed: “My heart was fashioned to be susceptible of love and sympathy, and when wrenched by misery to vice and hatred, it did not endure the violence of the change without torture such as you cannot even imagine.” (Shelley, 195) Denied his only desire, acceptance, the creature acts in exasperation, not knowing what else to do. He acts in a manner of a jealous and hurt lover, wishing for the attention of the sole one they love, destroying all else that might separate them. The ultimately unsatisfying conclusion of the novel, in which the creature states he will destroy himself after having destroyed Frankenstein, demonstrates the reality of Mary Shelley’s troubles. She fails, as many before and around her, at opening the minds of the people within society to the joys of new knowledge and differing views. She views her own life’s purpose as affecting others. Failing to noticeably fulfil this purpose, the creature embodies Mary Shelley’s own anguish at watching the misery of others’ lives, when, if only they would consider the ways of the freethinkers, they could be freed from the suffering of ignorance. 
Frankenstein, or the Modern Prometheus portrays Mary Shelley’s frustration and helplessness when addressing the inferior norms of society. In her life, she encounters individuals such as her own father, Percy, and her group of free thinking friends as well as the histories of individuals come before, such as Benedictus de Spinoza, Erasmus Darwin, and her own birth mother and sees society perpetually dismissing, shunning, decrying, or even persecuting them. She views this rejection as a great tragedy, for the common thread of all these individuals lies in their great love for humankind, and their desire to relieve the suffering and pain of their fellow human beings. Mary Shelley wrote Frankenstein, or the Modern Prometheus as a tragedy where society totally rejects a potentially great benefactor of humanity because of an utter lack of any attempt to understand the creature, choosing instead to hate those ideas and people unlike them, prolonging the misery of their own darkened existence. The title of the novel suggests Victor Frankenstein as the “Modern Prometheus”, but the originally benevolent and loving nature of the creature, the creature’s desire to help mankind, and the literal imagery of the creature’s bringing firewood to the De Lacey’s and then lighting the hut on fire after they leave point to the interpretation of the creature as the “Modern Prometheus”. In accordance with the myth, Victor Frankenstein, a god in his own eyes, persecutes the creature for wanting to bring light to humankind. Mary Shelley sees humanity as their own gods, treating ignorance as divine and social conformity and held beliefs as a heavenly attribute, thus rejecting the one who wishes to strip them of their false identity, the lying belief of their superiority, and show them that all things rest in the divine. The creature strikes fear into the hearts of Frankenstein and the general populace because he embodies truth, that humans are not superior but equal, and that love, acceptance, and understanding leads to the greatest benefit of the whole. Unfortunately, as Mary Shelley experiences in her own life. The frightening appearance of such revolutionary views and ideas creates a monster in the eyes of the sheep. Mary Shelley knows with great pain that if only they would open their minds to possibilities, they would see a loving shepherd with his cane and know that he protects them from the wolves of fear and ignorance. Instead, as with Frankenstein, or the Modern Prometheus, the sheep run from their protector, who admittedly lives unperfectly, and, with tragic harm done as much to himself,  he beats them with the cane of a frustrated and rejected love.
