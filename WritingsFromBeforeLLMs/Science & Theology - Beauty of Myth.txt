The Beauty of Myth


Snorri Sturluson preserved the Prose Edda, a work detailing and explaining the mythologies of the Norsemen, not primarily to reconcile pagan stories with history or the Christian worldview, but to keep the old Scandinavian art of storytelling and poetry alive. The Icelandic peoples in the time of Snorri Sturluson’s reign were a poetic and literate peoples and they had traditionally valued highly the art of poetry and storytelling. However, due to the influence of the prose favoring Europeans of the Christian world, favor for the traditional poetic style was fading away. Snorri Sturluson preserved the Prose Edda in retaliation to its falling out of favor and as a monument of admiration for poetic art of the old norse and as a scholastic manual for understanding the essence of what makes that poetry beautiful and how to continue that tradition of excellence.
        While Snorri Sturluson did make an attempt to reconcile the Norse mythologies with what he knew of history, as when he equates the grandson of the high king Priam of Troy, Tror, with Thor(Sturluson 3), and with the Christian worldview, as when he portrays a Norse worldview of the creation of the universe and man in terminology meant to closely resemble the Genesis creation story, “He made heaven and earth and the skies and everything in them”, “But his greatest work is that he made man and gave him a soul” (Sturluson 9), his main purpose was to expound upon the deep and moving power of poetry in telling stories and understanding the nature of man. The extensive context he provides for each snippet of poetry shows how a great deal of meaning can be expressed succinctly through poetic art.
        Snorri Sturluson was not preserving old Norse mythology for the beliefs entailed but for the medium in which those beliefs were presented. According to the introduction to the Anthony Faulkes translation of Prose Edda, “Sagas were now being written, and at the court of Norway were replacing poetry as entertainment for the court and as the official record of the king’s achievements.(Faulkes xii)” Snorri Sturluson intended to preserve culture and art, not religious belief, and found prime and beautiful examples of a rapidly dying artform, in the skaldic poems, poems written in the traditional Scandinavian oral style.
 Snorri Sturluson also, as evidenced by his attempt to reconcile Norse mythology with known history at the time, saw much of Norse mythology as his people’s interpretation of factual historical events expressed in exaggerated allegory. Furthermore, Snorri Sturluson recognized the truths of many poetic tales meant to relay life lessons to those who read them, such as when Thor challenges Utgarda-Loki and is defeated by trickery, giving readers insight to the idea that appearances can be deceiving, and that brute force has its limits. From this perspective of allegorical history and moralistic tales, Snorri Sturluson had little reason as a Christian to be conflicted in preserving Pagan mythologies.
Snorri Sturluson also portrayed the norse mythologies as imperfect versions of the Christian mythology. He does so by presenting the idea of many gods being one God and even the All-father as having twelve names, All-father, Herran, Nikar, Nikus, Fiolnir, Oski, Omi, Biflidi, Svidar, Svidrir, Vidrir, and Ialg, and as being three gods, High, Just-as-high, and Third, which, in Christian terminology, is the Trinity. In another instance, Snorri Sturluson recreates the flood story from genesis with a frost giant called Bergelmir, saying “He went up on to his ark with his wife and was preserved there”(Sturluson 11). Snorri thus positions himself in place to counter any believers of the Norse mythologies by explaining that while the Norse mythologies are true, the Christian and Jewish scriptures are more true and more accurate. 
        Snorri Sturluson differed greatly from the Christians of the early church in respect to his approach to pagan myth. Many early church Fathers and writers, such as Tertullian and St. Justin Martyr,, preoccupied themselves entirely with attacking the Greek mythologies and portraying them as either false, inaccurate, or misguided. St. Justin Martyr believed that “any rays of truth that may have appeared here and there in the pagan myths and writings, are borrowed from the Jewish Prophets.”(Yong 3) and, subsequently, that the myths have become of little worth as a result of the advent of Christianity and the truth, as they thought it, of Christ. While St. Justin Martyr limited his argument to presenting a perspective of intellectual theft of Jewish wisdom by the Greek philosophers, Tertullian makes the argument that Greek philosophy and Jewish and Christian philosophy are entirely separate, saying “What has Athens to do with Jerusalem? What is there between the Academy and the Church?”(Yong 4) As Tertullian viewed the Greek mythologies and wisdoms as evil and unfounded, he called for the total rejection of the pagan myths, and for the belief in Christ alone (Yong 4). Had Tertullian’s view predominated, the mythologies would surely have been lost. Yet, there are those, the moderate, who find worth in the art of other cultures, who do not view them as competing religious systems or them as claiming any absolute truth. The moderate, such as Snorri Sturluson, preserved beauty and wisdom for beauty and wisdom’s sake. Unlike Tertullian, Snorri Sturluson understood that one does not need the Bible to understand that fire is hot, or that water is wet, or, in other words, that truths can be found independent of written divine authority. 
        Snorri Sturluson kept Pagan mythologies alive because they were wrapped in poetic beauty and he saw that as worth preserving. Snorri Sturluson, even if he did believe that other belief systems should be done away with, managed to reconcile the stories of his people with history and faith as the western Christians understood it, thus allowing him to save the Norse mythologies in their poetic form from fading away under the pressure of a dominant religion. On the other front, Snorri Sturluson was well aware that the traditional skaldic style was falling out of favor and so sought to preserve it before it was completely forgotten. His love for poetry and his desire to maintain the traditions of his people led to him writing the Prose Edda as a treatise on the art of poetry and as an act of preservation for the poetic traditions and techniques present in the skaldic verse.
        While some early Christians were reasonable in preserving Greek wisdom and mythology on the grounds that it offered understanding in regards to the human condition and Snorri Sturluson was more than justified in his reasons to preserve the norse mythologies that he did for the sake of their beauty, modern man must find its own reason to preserve myths older or newer than their own stories. While one can simply say that it is better to have too much knowledge than too little of it, and that ought to suffice, there remains the allure of beauty in mythology that speaks to some deep part of the human essence, and the historical account that mythology provides in regards to how civilization has evolved over time. Modern man preserves myth to understand himself, to understand what it means to be human. Modern man preserves myth to study and research the meaning of the monomyth, and why so many different mythologies bear such distinct similarities, such as the hero’s journey, or a flood story. Myth tells modern man not only of a people’s fears and hopes, what heaven is to them, or what hell is to them, but also of their way of life and their civilization, how they view the world and how they interact with it. Mythologies have preserved first and foremost worldviews, perspectives of the world. They cause us to wonder how we should view the world, as the Gnostics: evil, as the Norse: doomed in a long defeat, as the Native Americans: a caregiver and mother, or as any of the innumerable other worldviews offered by the cultures of different and diverse peoples. Myth should be preserved not only for the anthropological value it affords, but also for the philosophical value of the mind-broadening potential it offers by giving us different ways of looking at the world and existence and helping us to come to terms with life and death, and earliness and lateness, and why creatures must kill in order to survive and perhaps whether they ultimately need to at all. Myth allows one to break free from the culture and tradition in which they were raised and to look at the world in a fresh and new light.
