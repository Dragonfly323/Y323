Documents from student activists in Atlanta show the concerns and aims of the Civil Rights movement in an uncommon light. In addition to these documents, the speech given by Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. to the S.C.L.C in Atlanta in 1967 as well as the book Beyond Atlanta, offer confirmation and context as to the purpose and intent of the Civil Rights movement. Taken together, the concerns of the students and the Civil Rights movement as a whole wanted far more than desegregation, seeing desegregation only as a means for other more impactful changes. The primary documents and book show the system of oppression as it was related to education, economy, and society. The primary focus of the Civil Rights movement in Atlanta was the improvement of education and economic opportunity, detailing the form of education desired, why that form of education was desired, and the hopes of what that form of education would achieve.
        After slavery ended, a new form of servitude rose into dominance called “peonage.” According to Beyond Atlanta, a reporter wrote that “the only difference between the two systems [of peonage and slavery] is that in Gone in the Wind days the Negro was bound to the landlord by a legalized property system, while today it is the simple device of being in debt that binds him to the soil” (10). Additionally, the book recounts a tale wherein “A tenant, John Turner, along with his son, was shot by a white mob merely for being a “hardworking farmer who had amassed some degree of success from the sweat of his brow”” (12). The situation of African-Americans before and during the Civil Rights movement was largely that of manual labor, and those who managed to overcome the limitations of perpetual labor and raise themselves out of poverty were punished for doing so. Activists during the Civil Rights Movement would have seen education as an important part of the means to overcome this binding to the soil and victory over fear of success and consequent retribution. 
        A Mr. Gather at a meeting of Atlanta students presented complaints and proposed solutions of the student body pertaining to the manner in which this particular college treated its students of color. His comments give great insight into the issue of education as a whole and its effect on the African American population that reached the level of college education. “We question whether some of the paternalistic inventions that governed our conduct here do not hinder the free use of the intellect and thereby hinder our own development and hinder the development of our education. [...] We are forced to take the position that when a student comes to an institution and surrenders his right to make simple choices like whether he feels like putting on a tie or not that there is something here said about his intellect. About his ability to survey his own situation. In other words, we don’t think this is a grammar school.” Mr. Gather’s comments demonstrate the underlying mentality towards the education of African Americans. In these comments can be seen the idea that any power gained by blacks must be power meted out by the established system, a system controlled largely if not entirely by whites. In this meting out of power is the message that blacks in whatever position in society are unquestionably under the power of the whites, and that whites maintain the right to dictate what blacks are and are not allowed to do. This sentiment was incredibly demotivating, and reinforced the psychological factor of oppression experienced so strongly by blacks at this time. If these were the feelings of those receiving a college education, how much worse could it have been for those with less education?
        At the lower, more comprehensive levels of society, citizens of Atlanta approached the Atlanta Board of Education with criticisms and requests regarding the state of primary and secondary schooling for black children. Their statement was clear and simple to understand, “Atlanta, Georgia has failed in its responsibility to offer equal educational opportunity to Negro Children.” Most of the primary documents available from Atlanta concern education in some form or another. Beyond Atlanta explains why this was so, “The economic and institutional development of the city’s black community also provided the opportunity for the sheer scale of the student movement. [...] At the beginning of the 1960s, Atlanta had a larger and more concentrated black college population than any other city in the South.” The book also shares a telling quote from Lonnie King, “In really analyzing it, the only people in the black community at that time who were free to take on the Establishment were college kids.” (118) It’s highly probable that the “citizens of Atlanta” in the document were college students. They write, “Be it further resolved that the Atlanta board of Education immediately remove from Atlanta the scar of segregation and inadequate school facilities for Negro and white children.” The stress upon desegregation was motivated primarily by a desire for the improved education of the black population. Whites generally received a better education and the college students probably believed that desegregation would lead to better population for blacks at the very least as a result of reluctance to lower the quality of education for white children. With desegregated schools, blacks could no longer be pushed aside or ignored, and the same level education would apply to all, regardless of race.
        In King’s speech to the public during the Tenth Anniversary Convention of the S.C.L.C. in Atlanta, Georgia on August 16, 1967, he gives eloquent turn to the perspectives of the college students who so desired these reforms in education. “Psychological freedom, a firm sense of self-esteem, is the most powerful weapon against the long night of physical slavery. [...] From  old plantations of the South to newer ghettos of the North, the Negro has been confined to a life of voicelessness and powerlessness. Stripped of the right to make decisions concerning his life and destiny he has been subject to the authoritarian and sometimes whimsical decisions of this white power structure.” King’s words bear marked similarity to Mr. Gather’s when he sums up his point at the Atlanta Student meeting, and connects the importance of educational reform to the Civil Rights movement as a whole. “To be educated,” Mr. Gather said, “is to be able to make choices; to decide. [...] The central point is that you don’t have an education if you can’t choose.” To the students so actively involved in the movement, education was not only the ability to choose, which at the time was severely limited, but the ability to know and understand the choices that could be made. Psychological freedom in this sense is knowing one’s own abilities and potential. The students understood that if someone doesn’t perceive themselves as capable of achieving a goal, it is not a choice to them, but fate. The consequent resignation to this fate resulted in a sense of powerlessness. 
        Another document that reflects these sentiments of this pressing need for good education was the SNCC request for student records. Morace Julian Bond, the author of this letter, politely requests the following information on the public schools in Atlanta: “Teacher per pupil comparisons in white and Negro schools; the number of classrooms per pupil in white and Negro schools; Funds expended by state and local government in white and Negro schools; Teacher preparation in white and Negro schools; Post-high school achievement of white and Negro high school graduates; Dropouts in white and Negro schools.” Bond’s purpose in obtaining these records was to learn and draw attention to the disparities between education for whites and education for blacks and the results of those disparities. This data would also provide a groundwork from which progress could be made and tracked. The data, especially that regarding “Post-high school achievement of white and Negro high school graduates,” would inevitably find a link between quality of education and quality of life. “For black Georgians, it was not so much segregation that was resented as economic suppression and insults” (14). As Dr. King said with a complimentary sentiment in his Atlanta speech, “Now we realize that dislocations in the market operations of our economy and the prevalence of discrimination thrust people into idleness and bind them in constant or frequent unemployment against their will.” Again, desegregation was viewed only as a part of the grander goal of better education, which would lead to greater economic equality.
        These documents present the true motive for the Civil Rights Movement–the need for better education. However, those who fought for the rights to quality education for African Americans understood that quality education was needed for all. Mr. Gather said, “We think that it’s a part of education to be able to survey your situation, to appraise it and to criticize it. We think that it’s also part of your education to be able to choose on the basis of your own thinking.” Mr. Gather means that the ability to perceive accurately one’s situation is crucial to understanding the road to freedom from psychological servitude and oppression. Of course, this includes the accurate perception of others’ situations as well. In the document presented to the Atlanta Board of Education, the authors write, “In the semi-segregated and over-crowded schools of Atlanta, Negro children as well as whites do not learn of the important Freedom struggle now being waged in the United States, and the historical role the Negro has played in the development of this nation.” By keeping whites ignorant of the situation and history of African Americans, the oppression is reinforced, and through the reinforcement of oppression against blacks, a subtle oppression against whites of lower economic class persists as well, by encouraging the divide between the races. 
The early examples of desegregation experienced in Atlanta “had been token victories at best” (114). Dr. King was dissatisfied by the superficial desegregation that had occurred in the city, as “a tranquilizing drug to lull us to sleep, and dull our sensitivity to the continued existence of segregation” (119). Segregation, to Dr. King and the college students who made up the bulk of the movement in Atlanta, was not about swimming pools, movie theaters, or drinking fountains–such could be integrated without impacting anything of true importance–, but education above all else. They knew that good education for blacks would lead to greater economic advancement and self-empowerment, and that good education for whites that was accurate and sympathetic to the African American history and cause would further heal the relations between blacks and whites and that good education as whole would further the equality of the races. These documents and the context provided by Beyond Atlanta and Dr. King’s speech show that the goal of the Civil Rights Movement was never desegregation for the sake of desegregation, but desegregation for the hope of better education.
