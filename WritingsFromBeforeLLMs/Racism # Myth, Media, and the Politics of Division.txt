Racial Myth, the Media, and the Politics of Division

Introduction


        Despite slavery having been abolished for well over a hundred years, racial tensions and discrimination are at an all time high. Activists for people of color lash out at whites and blame them for the many injustices that blacks suffer. However, only a vast minority of whites before the Civil War ever owned slaves, and the minority that owned the greatest percentage of slaves was in the wealthy upper class. The victorious perception in modern public dialogue is nevertheless that all whites, and particularly remnants of Confederate thought, are to blame for the systemic racism that occurs in the US. Two stories written before and after the Civil War, “The Gold Bug” and The Sound and the Fury, offer evidence that the Confederate correlation is not entirely true. Regardless, the truth of this perception is irrelevant in consideration of the true base cause for continued racism in the US. The base cause of continued racism in the US consists solely in a combination of government policy and media portrayal.


The Red Herring of the South


        Edgar Allan Poe’s short story “The Gold Bug” and William Faulkner’s novel The Sound and the Fury show in conjuction that racism’s correlation with an admiration of the Confederacy is a weak positive at best. While the Confederacy has become a symbol for both blacks and whites for many different things, positive and negative, these symbols have almost nothing to do with the current state of racial tensions in the US, and are altogether distractions from the true roots. These two stories show acceptable viewpoints in regards to the treatment of blacks during the Antebellum period and the Roaring Twenties, respectively. Examining these two works cast obscurity over the often cited cause for modern racism being rooted in Confederate admiration. The purpose of exampling these two works is to show that Examination of the South in relation to racism is an exercise in futility.
        “The Gold Bug” was first published in June of 1843 in Pennsylvania, a state with fewer than one thousand slaves at the time.(14) Edgar Allan Poe bases the story near Charleston, a city in the Deep South, a city he loved and in which he spent a great deal of time. Edgar Allan Poe was opposed to slavery, but not to the Confederate/Southern lifestyle. The story involves three main characters: the unnamed white narrator, a white named Legrand, and a Freedman named Jupiter.
                Jupiter, the Freedman, is introduced in the following passage, establishing his status as a Freedman and why he continues to act as a guardian or servant to Legrand:
 
“In these excursions he [Legrand] was usually accompanied by an old negro, called Jupiter, who had been manumitted before the reverses of the family, but who could be induced, neither by threats nor by promises, to abandon what he considered his right of attendance upon the footsteps of his young “Massa Will.” It is not improbable that the relatives of Legrand, conceiving him to be somewhat unsettled in intellect, had contrived to instill this obstinacy into Jupiter, with a view to the supervision and guardianship of the wanderer.”
 
Throughout the story Legrand and the narrator interact with Jupiter in a kindly way, calling him “Jup” and listening to his concerns. The following interaction is the only one in which Legrand or the unnamed narrator treat Jupiter poorly:  
 
“You infernal scoundrel!” cried Legrand, apparently much relieved, “what do you mean by telling me such nonsense as that? As sure as you drop that beetle I’ll break your neck. Look here, Jupiter, do you hear me?”
               “Yes, massa, needn’t hollo at poor nigger dat style.”
               “Well! now listen!–if you will venture out on the limb as far as you think safe, and not let go the beetle, I’ll make you a present of a silver dollar as soon as you get down.”
 
In this passage, Jupiter, in a tree on account of his being the best tree climber, chastises Legrand for speaking to him poorly, clearly establishing to the reader a sense of mutual respect between Jupiter and Legrand, whom Jupiter perceives to be under his care. Legrand, in turn, concedes to Jupiter. In other parts of the short story, while it can be assumed common practice for whites in the South during the time to view blacks as having no voice, Jupiter is vocal throughout the story and both Legrand and the narrator consider what he has to say. 
        The other work, William Faulkner’s The Sound of the Fury, depicts a series of interactions between Jason Compson and the blacks he encounters on April 6, 1928. The novel, first published in 1929, takes place during the end of the Roaring Twenties, one of the first generations when none under the age of 60 experienced the era of slavery first-hand, whether white or black, and when none under the age of 70 had any clear memories of it. Though none of the blacks Jason interacts with are Freedmen, and Jason himself never experienced the era of Slavery, he views blacks as worthless and lazy, thinks that they should be enslaved, and refers to them exclusively by the term “nigger”.
                Jason often bemoans his responsibility to, in paraphrase, “feed a kitchen-full of niggers.” His opening narration on April 6, 1928, proceeds shortly:
 
“I says she ought to be down there in that kitchen right now, instead of up there in her room, gobbing paint on her face and waiting for six niggers that cant even stand up out of a chair unless they’ve got a pan full of bread and meat to balance them, to fix breakfast for her.”
 
This sentiment infects the entire third part of The Sound and the Fury. While the term “nigger” is commonly used throughout the novel by members of the Compson family and the black Dilsey, of all of them, Jason most clearly uses the word spitefully. He says to his mother when talking about Miss Quentin: “When people act like niggers, no matter who they are the only thing to do is treat them like a nigger.” To Jason, all blacks are “niggers” and non-blacks who don’t meet his standards, whatever those are, are denigrated to the status of “nigger.” Jason is so racist that he uses the term as a derogatory insult to blacks and whites alike.
                Jason is a one-count card, repeating his anti-black sentiment throughout his narration, most often talking about his kitchen. He says once to his mother, “I feed a whole dam kitchen full of niggers to follow around after him [Benjy], but if I want an automobile tire changed, I have to do it myself.” Later on, he says to Job, an old black man who is working too slowly for Jason’s liking, “You ought to be working for me. Every other no-count nigger in town eats in my kitchen.” He says to Dilsey in regards to Miss Quentin, “I cant keep a kitchen full of niggers to wait on her pleasure, much as I’d like to.” Jason is terribly hung-up about his kitchen. His baseline argument for his racism is that blacks are worthless and do nothing but consume resources. In his mind, that justifies his racism. He bemoans not being able to treat them like slaves and force them to work.
                While interactions in these two depictions of the Antebellum and Roaring Twenties South may not be representative, both offered a perspective realistic to people in each era. More recently, a study based on internet search terms shows that racism in America is widespread not only in the geographical south but almost the entire Eastern part of the United States.(15)
                Taking into account the depictions of race relations in Poe’s “The Gold Bug”, Faulkner’s The Sound and the Fury, and the current distribution of racism in the United States, racism likely took on a form independent from slavery and the Southern culture and has progressed into the current era by other means and perspectives not directly related to or dependent on the South or Antebellum slavery. While examining stories such as Poe’s “The Gold Bug” and Faulkner’s The Sound and the Fury can begin to shed light on the independence of racism in regards to slavery and the South, it is only the start to understanding the modern-day roots of widespread racist sentiment.


The Prison System and the Drug War


        The trend of racism in the United States from post Civil War and onwards is complicated, but not opaque. The 13th Amendment of the United States Constitution states, “Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction.” In the most simple view of the situation, this amendment is the root cause of racism in the modern day. The reason being is that the portion that says “except as a punishment for crime” has been ruthlessly exploited from the very first day. After the Civil War, free blacks were arrested for petty charges such as loitering or being unable to present proof of employment. This system was in many ways better than chattel slavery, since now taxpayer money, instead of slaveowner money, would be used to feed, house, and clothe the “slaves” while the employers continued to benefit from free labor. (8)
        This system continues today, and is one of a triad of prime causes for racism in the United States. Prisons operate as funnels for taxpayer money into private pockets and doubly benefit from renting out prison labor to private companies. The prisons are paid for this labor, but the prisoners are either not paid, or paid almost nothing. The second of the triad has changed throughout the years. Before, it was the Jim Crow laws and segregation, but after the Civil Rights movement ended those, the second of the triad became what is known as the “drug war”. Noam Chomsky, a noted intellectual, describes this system of continued criminalization, “[The criminalization of Black life] is called the drug war, and it’s a racist war. Ronald Reagan was an extreme racist – though he denied it – but the whole drug war is designed, from policing to eventual release from prison, to make it impossible for black men and, increasingly women, to be part of [American] society.” and “They have been re-criminalized and turned into a slave labor force–that’s prison labor.”(6) Having a criminal record makes living normally in society much more difficult. The third of the triad is the media, and the media portrayal of blacks and black/white relations. This triad is not separable, but together constitutes a vicious machine that results in the disenfranchisement of an entire race.
        The prison system in the US gives incentive to have high numbers of criminals. This in turn gives incentive to pass laws or police in such a way to create these high numbers of criminals. Private prisons, a growing trend in the US, even makes use of “lockup quotas”. Lockup quotas describe in the contract between the state and the prison that a certain percentage occupancy must be held, most commonly 90%, or the difference must be paid by the state. Keep in mind that this is taxpayer money. The most egregious examples are when prisons are undergoing security upgrades, and thus are unable to house prisoners, but still demand to be paid as per the lockup quota agreement. (5) Prisons in the US are a business. Even if they are not privately owned, they are still privately supplied. The prison system in the US is systemically corrupt and constitutes the demand for which the drug war supplies.
        The drug war may be the single most deplorable system of policies the US has ever enforced. To not even get into the damage it causes in relation to prescription medications and opioid abuse, the drug war disproportionately affects blacks and minorities. Jay Z, the rapper, recently narrated a short film about the drug war, a portion of which follows:


        “The media ignored actual data to this day; crack, it’s still talked about as a Black problem. 
The NYPD raided our Brooklyn neighborhoods while Manhattan bankers openly used coke with impunity. The war on drugs exploded the US prison population disproportionately locking away Blacks and Latinos; our prison populations grew more than 900%. When the war on drugs began in 1971, our prison population was 200 thousand. Today it is over 2 million.”(4)


The numbers speak for themselves. The incarceration rates for non-drug crimes have remained consistent throughout the years, but increased pressure from higher up in the government has resulted in increased hunting of black males. The reasons for this are two-fold. Because of the systemic racism/prison relationship that has existed since the Civil War ended, blacks have statistically higher crime rates. The second reason is that much of the black population is also in the lower income scale, which means they have less ability to defend themselves, or afford good lawyers. 
The whole system is cyclical, so it is difficult to describe any one part of it. Blacks are in the low income bracket because they have been systematically targeted by law enforcement, this leads to higher crime statistics, which partly leads to more targeting; a strong correlation exists between lower income regardless of race and crime rates, with lower income resulting in more crimes. This is probably also due to selective policing, as police are less likely to go after richer targets, due to the difficulty of prosecution, the possibility of personal threat, and public backlash. However, statistics are not entirely (artificially) weighted against blacks. The ACLU reports that, “African-Americans do not use drugs more than white people; whites and blacks use drugs at almost exactly the same rates. And since there are five times as many whites as blacks in the United States, it follows that the overwhelming majority of drug users are white.”(3) But despite the evidence that police are as likely if not more so to find drugs on a white person than a black person, “We are incarcerating African-American men at a rate of approximately four times the rate of incarceration of black men in South Africa under apartheid.” and “African-Americans are admitted to state prisons at a rate that is 13.4 times greater than whites.”(3) The cycle perpetuates itself, as police can justify their racial targeting by looking at past crime statistics, which in turn leads to a more disenfranchised population.


Education and the Media


Jay Z made a salient point in the quote above–“The media ignored actual data to this day.” The media is better considered the combination of media, education, and public discourse. The media would not have power over the populace, if the populace were not susceptible. Racism in the US starts in elementary school. Chomsky, in his book What Kind of Creatures Are We? quotes Alexander Humboldt, “The man whose life is spent in performing a few simple operations … generally becomes as stupid and ignorant as it is possible to be.”(2) The current education system is heavily centered around testing and authoritarianism. This is another complicated issue that shall be dealt with tentatively. Max Horkheimer and Theodor Adorno, prominent philosophers and sociologists of the 20th century, in their book Dialectic of Enlightenment, write that “Totalitarian society does not abolish the suffering of its members, but registers and plans it; mass culture does the same with tragedy.”(1) The education system in the US prepares and indoctrinates the public into a state of unquestioning obedience. Eight hours are spent in the school, after which point they go home mentally exhausted, often with homework, and commonly turn on the tv when they are not working–repeat for 13 years. The schools teach to the test, thus limiting the possibility of individual curiosity and independent learning. Teachers remain under constant threat to teach according to the criteria and guidelines. If the student is kept busy learning the concrete facts needed to make a good grade, they spend no time exploring other schools of thought, or really anything at all. Through the US education system, large swathes of the US population are habituated into a system of unquestioning obedience to authority. 
Chomsky sums up the human condition, “Humans are social beings, and the kind of creatures we become depends crucially on the social, cultural, and institutional circumstances of our lives.”(2) The institutional aspect is composed by the school, and the cultural aspect is largely composed by the media, and the social aspect is composed of others who are also victims of the school and the media. The question here ultimately boils down to how much control does the US citizen have over his environment and, by extension, his perception of the world.
The vast majority of media is owned by five corporations: Time Warner, Disney, Murdoch’s News Corporation, Berelsmann of Germany, and Viacom.(13) Chomsky quotes John Dewey, “power today resides in control of the means of production, exchange, publicity, transportation, and communication, Whoever owns them rules the life of the country.”(2) Adorno and Horkheimer write that “All mass culture under monopoly is identical” and that “Culture today is infecting everything with sameness. Film, radio, and magazines form a system.” and that “In reality, a cycle of manipulation and retroactive need is unifying the system ever more tightly.”(1) According to Chomsky, who references a Harvard/Princeton study, “About 70%, at the lower end of the economic scale, have no influence on policy.”(2) Thus, US citizens are faced with the fact that the majority of their information exposure is controlled by a select few, very rich and powerful entities, who also have significant impact on US policy through lobbying, NGOs, and bribery. The interest of these entities can be assumed to be money and power, and a docile, unthinking population is the best way to achieve and ensure that end. 
The media is the primary decider of public perception in the US. Adorno and Horkheimer write that, “The [radio] democratically makes everyone into listeners, in order to expose them in authoritarian fashion to the same programs put out by different stations.”(1) The programs, or the narrative that the media pushes in the US is one of racial division. The media selectively reports certain going-ons while entirely ignoring others. The most recent example is Black Lives Matter. The Black Lives Matter (BLM) movement and the public debate surrounding it are perfect examples of Adorno and Horkheimer’s assessment of culture at large, that “differences are hammered home and propagated” and that “the advantages and disadvantages debated… serve only to perpetuate the appearance of competition and choice.”(1) By confining the discourse within certain parameters, that of “racial equality” and “police brutality”, the media succeeds in a few things. Firstly, they succeed in calling attention away from the true propagator of racial inequality, the drug war and government policies, and instead make it seem like an proportionally insignificant issue, though still atrocious, has a large impact on the sociocultural whole; police brutality, while an issue, has laughably little effect on the state of the black people in US society. Secondly, by showing the violence of BLM, the media succeeds in dividing and polarizing the populace between those who do not condone violence, and those who support the “justice” of the cause; the result tends toward members of the populace growing more antagonistic towards other members of the populace, subtle manipulation by the media on the perceptions of different colors of skin, rather than being antagonistic toward the truly evil overseers of racial inequality. Thirdly, by escalating the debate, any attempt at addressing the real roots of racial inequality are met with incredulity or misunderstanding; as Adorno and Horkheimer write, “The spectator must need no thoughts of his own. The product prescribes the reaction” and “Anyone who does not conform is condemned.”(1)
In writing about Anarchists that Chomsky admires, he says, “This broad tendency [toward freedom/autonomy] in human development seeks to identify structures of hierarchy, authority, and domination that constrain human development, and then to subject them to a very reasonable challenge: justify yourself.”(2) However, due to the media, people are divided against each other, and have misidentified the structures of hierarchy. As a result, bad times prolong for us all. Chomsky writes, “We would know the details very well if the crimes could be attached to an official enemy.”(2) The media has successfully convinced the US populace that the problems of racism lie in the symbol of the Confederate flag, the white male, and the residual spirit of the South. These are certainly aspects, but when compared to the media manipulation and the government policies that perpetuate the disenfranchisement of the black race, they are ludicrously small, and not even worth considering. Any change concerning the Confederate flag, the white male, or the residual spirit of the South will do nothing to affect the policies of the drug war or prison labor or prison strikes against prison slave labor, which are almost never mentioned by the media.(9) By setting up insubstantial enemies, the media, and those who control the media, succeeds in distracting from the real issues and allowing the public to feel as if they are accomplishing something, and making a difference, when they are in fact playing into the hands of the elite.(7) George Orwell’s 1984 comes to mind, when every week the populace would be brought into a film room to vent their anger and frustration at a fabricated enemy.
