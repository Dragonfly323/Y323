The ancient way of thinking reflects caution, prioritizes safety, and seeks to maintain the status quo. The modern way of thinking challenges accepted beliefs and established norms of man-made society by emphasizing raw human emotion and looking for parallels and metaphors in nature to understand the true place of humanity in the realms of existence. The ancient thinker does not entertain lofty dreams and is a realist; he strives to find contentment in his own place in life and honors the contemporary social order even if his is not the best lot. the modern thinker seeks to advance the condition of man as a whole and is an idealist; he believes in equality and is prepared to take the risk associated with change in order to alter the current political world, social structure, or popular way of thinking; he finds great beauty in nature and thus sees natural human emotions as the most beautiful human element. The ancient thinker is grounded always while the modern thinker allows his thoughts to ascend upwards. Architecture in the classical world reflects the ancient way of thinking. The Parthenon and Pantheon were both symmetrically designed to be in accordance with the contemporary view of beauty and divinity. Modern architecture, from the Gothic masterpieces of the late 16th century to the experiments of Frank Lloyd Wright, elevates the idea of transcendence and imitates the chaotic order of nature; Modern buildings feature prominent spires and asymmetry.
        Descartes is an interesting example of the transition of ancient thought into modern thought. Descartes viewed order as sublime, as evidenced by his allegory of the city built by a single master architect, as opposed to growing up over time to become composed of different ideas of different builders from different times. However, despite his appreciation of orderly beauty, which might be viewed as a commitment to ancient thought, Descartes rebelled against the ancient thought being taught him in school. While his views of aesthetic beauty and, also, social order, remained rooted in the hierarchical thought of the ancient values, his pursuit of truth stemmed from a desire of self-realization, an attempt to understand truth as an individual, of his own striving. Despite desiring to be an original thinker of his own means, he remained true to the ancient methodology, most closely related to Aristotelian thought, of using science, logic, and reason to arrive at deducible truths. If he were a true romantic modern thinker, he may have at once thrown himself into chaotic darkness, trusting in the laws of nature to bring him out of murky philosophical swamps. While he did travel for nine years at one point, the core of his philosophy was derived from inward reflections and meditations that he had while sitting in a heated room, isolated from nature. The ancient thinker feared nature, for at the time of the ancient thinker, nature had not been sufficiently conquered. The modern thinker, however, befriends nature, seeing it as a refuge from the corruption of humanity and the isolation of civilization. As such is the case, had Descartes been a truly modern thinker, he may have gone out into the wilderness to seek parallels between nature and himself. Indeed, the only instance of Descartes discussing nature in his Discourse on Method was to expound upon a preconceived notion that animals were not capable of intelligent thought or speech and derived from this that the distinction of man was this logical thought. Though he had previously mentioned desiring and succeeding in ridding his mind of all preconceived notions save those which were self-evident, a fatal flaw of reasoning in itself, he held onto these preconceived notions of animals instead of opening his mind to the possibility of animal intelligence. Ultimately, Descartes was more of an ancient thinker in that he refused to utterly abandon his identity in the pursuit of truth and instead clinged to comfortable societal and institutional dogma concerning the natural world so as to not risk losing himself in the chaos of unknowing.
        Milton is a product of his time. Convicted in his beliefs, Milton attempted to reconcile the popular perception of God with the problem of pain. In Paradise Lost, Milton’s main allegorical concepts were that Satan took advantage of humanity’s free will and that the sin that was brought about by Eve’s succumbing to Satan’s temptations is the cause for all pain and suffering. The significance of these events is contained within the theme of a hierarchy much in accord with ancient thought. In the vein of Christian syncretism, Milton uses the idea of hierarchy to show that the pain in the world was brought about by the usurpation of the natural order. The specific examples of this concept are found in Satan’s bid for power above his ordained state in Heaven, which may have been the genesis of the realities of “good” and “evil”, and the subsequent temptation of Eve to alter her own status in life, to become “more equal” with Adam. Milton is the abhorer of change. In Paradise Lost, there are few, if any, examples of change that are of a positive nature. Examples of change that are negative are Satan’s birth of Sin, Sin’s birthing of Death as a result of copulation with Satan, the rebellion in Heaven and subsequent fall of the angels, the opening of the gates of Hell- which might be akin to a new frontier of sorts, similar to Columbus’ finding the New World in 1492, which was a significant change in the contemporary times-, the change of perception within Eve that allowed her to eat the fruit, the following Fall of Man, and, in relation, the alteration of mankind’s relationship with God. Verily, the only example of change which is positive is the advent of the Christ into the world, to redeem mankind. This change, however, is a bit of a cop-out as it is meant to return mankind to its original sinless state. It is no great stretch of the imagination to claim that Milton desires for all change to be undone until the time of Eden and then to have time halted, to flow, and thus change, no more. The reasoning in this is dependant upon the concept of the hierarchy, or the Golden Chain, of Neoplatonism. Any substantial change, therefore, is bound to disrupt the perfect original order in which the world was first created by God. 
        Descartes and Milton hold similar respect and adoration for order but their motives are different. Descartes relies solely upon his mind in his quest to find new ideas, philosophies, and truths to understand the world whereas Milton relies upon ancient texts and mythologies to rectify the beliefs of mankind and to understand the world as a student of those long passed, believing that the truth has already been said and must be learned from others, figures of authority, to be realized in one’s self. In this way, Milton and Descartes differ in their personalities. Milton acts in the spirit of the ancient on the basis of the ancient. Descartes acts in the spirit of the ancient but rejects the words of the ancients, creating his own progressive basis in his pursuit of truth. Because of this, Descartes is a more progressive thinker in his belief that there was yet to be discovered, as opposed to Milton’s belief that there is not yet to be discovered as those before have already discovered it. Milton is a student of Scholasticism in that he seeks to understand the thoughts, words, and ways of the past, limiting the chance of original thought within the confines of interpretation. To make use of metaphor, Descartes and Milton both use the same type of ship but Milton scouts the coastline while Descartes sails into the unknown sea.
