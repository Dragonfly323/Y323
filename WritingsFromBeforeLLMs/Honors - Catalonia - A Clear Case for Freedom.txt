

A Clear Case:
Spain’s Authoritarianism, the Ghost of Franco, and Catalonia’s Right to Speak





Catalonia deserves a fair chance under international standards and mediation to decide whether it will stay or leave Spain. Catalonia has born a tenuous relationship with Castille, the major part of Spain, since the French Wars of Succession when Philip V invaded Aragon, whose nobility supported Archduke Charles of Austria, and forced them to sign the Nueva Planta decrees, which made the Kingdom of Aragon obeisant to Castile in a centralized, unified Spain. Catalonia attempted to become an independent state in the early 20th century but was interrupted by the world wars and suppressed by the Francoist regime. In 1978, General Franco died peacefully and Spain transitioned to a Constitutional democracy. Voters from Catalonia gave majority support for the Constitution. Catalonia was probably ascribing to the saying, “any port in a storm”, when they voted ‘yes’. The People’s Party, the current majority party in Madrid, was founded “by seven Franco ministers avowedly to continue the Franco legacy,” and which Mariano Rajoy, the current Prime Minister of Spain, “joined not long after Franco’s death, when to do so was an unabashed declaration of Francoist belief” (Murray). Catalonia has found considerable and justified dissatisfaction in its relationship with Spain, and increasingly more so in the past few years. Catalonia and its citizens deserve the right and ability to voice their opinion and have their grievances addressed.
        A major complaint by not only the Catalan people, but many people in Spain, is the complete absence of any investigation into the Franco regime and the crimes it committed. Craig Murray, former UK ambassador to Uzbekistan, writes, “There has never been any reckoning with the crimes of the dictator or the vast section of Spanish establishment that collaborated willingly with him” (Murray). The United Nations released a statement saying, “Crimes carried out under Franco are crimes against humanity and international conventions on human rights should be applied” (France24). Baltasar Garzon, a Spanish lawyer and former judge who has addressed similar issues of past crimes in Argentina, recently attempted to investigate the Franco Regime. Right-nationalist groups found offense and charged him with abusing his power. The Spanish Court found Garzon guilty and suspended him for 11 years. When speaking about this incident to France24, Garzon says, “ Spanish Justice is very independent as a whole but at the top, that’s less the case, that’s where you seem to find interests at work. In my case, the maneuvers of certain people and the People’s Party for things to happen in a certain way and not in another are very clear. That’s been said publicly, it has been written and proven” (France24). Indeed, Spain’s judicial independence ranks lower than Saudi Arabia, China, and many other Middle Eastern, South American, and African nations, receiving a score of 4.2 out of 7 according to World Economic Forum’s Global Competitiveness Index 2017-2018 (WEForum).
        The people in Spain and Catalonia live with constant reminders of Franco’s legacy. Victims of the regime have found no justice. José Maria Galante, who goes by the name Chato, said to France24, “There’s been absolutely no recognition of what he did.” No victim of Franco’s regime has had a chance to testify in court. Chato, a member of a communist party during Franco’s reign, tells others at a group meeting, “However strong you thought you were, it’s difficult when you find yourself being urinated on, when you urinate blood. I thought I’d never again in my life be able to have sex. I was twenty-two and decided to be strong. I survived thanks to my anger. I survived not because of my political convictions but because of my anger, because at least I was a human being.” Chato knows where his torturer lives, an infamous member of Franco’s regime, Antonio González Pacheco. In 2014, an Argentine judge used the Universal Jurisdiction principle to request Pacheco’s extradition, but was denied by the Spanish Court (France24). The Spanish government passed an amnesty law in 1978 and blocks attempts at investigation, instead ascribing to a type of amnesia, and allying with people like Jaime Alonso, Deputy Chairman of the Francisco Franco Foundation, who told France24, “I guarantee you that the regime did not torture in any way” (France24). Denial is one thing, but refusing any investigation is another.
        Jordi Cuixart, the president of Ómnium Central and one of the first political prisoners of the Spanish state during the Catalan crisis, prepared a video in case of his imprisonment. In the short video, released on Oct 17, 2017, he again evidences the shadow of Franco, “From 1953 to 1967 our head office was closed by the Franco dictatorship and if we got through that you can be sure that we will do so again” (Cuixart). Maggie Chapman, co-convener of the Scottish Green Party, said on Oct 3, even before any political prisoners were taken, that “the Spanish state has reacted in a way that echoes the actions of the fascist dictatorship under General Franco” (Chapman). Around Nov 5 in Sant Cugat, a town near Barcelona, pro-Spain protesters tear down a sign reading “Free Political Prisoners” (Adam), while earlier, on Oct 17, David Alandete, deputy director of El País, responding to a video titled “Help Catalonia”, said, “No, there are no Human Rights abuses in Catalonia. No, there are no political prisoners. Don’t believe this video”(@alandete). 
Spain has arrested and sought the extradition of members of the Catalan government and Catalan journalists and activists with charges of sedition, charges which require violence (@julianassange). The political prisoners have no evidence against them of committing violence, whereas the Spanish state has been disproportionately violent. Irene Baqué comments on a video posted by El País on Sep 26, “Early last week videos appeared of hundreds of people in the south of Spain seeing off police cars leaving for Catalonia to help the central government, shouting ‘Go get them!’, while waving Spanish flags” (Baqué). Over 300 videos show the brutality of the Guardia Civil against overwhelmingly peaceful voters, beating them with clubs, pulling hair, firing rubber bullets, and even jumping from stairs to kick people in the face, people already sitting on the ground (Spanish Police). Over a thousand people were injured, requiring medical attention. While the general stance of the Spanish government is denial and an attempt to discredit the videos, any apologies from the Spanish government attempt to downplay the extent of the injuries, and blame Catalonia for allowing the referendum to go forward (Guardian). The October 1st referendum, declared illegal by the Spanish government, was not necessarily an attempt by Catalonia to leave Spain, but was an attempt to voice an opinion. Baqué, who considered herself Catalan, Spanish, and European before witnessing the brutality on Oct 1 said, “I don’t want to be represented by a government that uses military tactics to deal with a dissatisfied region. I felt like this was no longer about nationalism but about democracy and free speech. This is about having a conversation and opening dialogue” (Baqué).
        On Oct 6, referring to the last declaration of Catalan Independence by Lluís Companys, 123rd President of the Generalitat de Catalunya, Pablo Casado Blanco, a representative of the People’s Party, said, “I believe that history shouldn’t be repeated and let’s hope tomorrow that nothing is declared because maybe the one who declares it will end as the one who declared it 83 years ago” (@julianassange). Companys, an incumbent, democratically elected president, was executed by the Franco Regime. Despite these half-hidden threats, Carles Puigdemont declared independence on Oct 7. He immediately suspended its effects, choosing instead to approach Spain with requests for dialogue (Live: Puigdemont). Spain responded by refusing to talk until Puigdemont put an end to all attempts of independence. Nationwide protests filled Catalonia and Spain; protesters wore white without carrying any national flags, demonstrating their desire that the Spanish government and Catalan government enter a dialogue and resolve the conflict peacefully, diplomatically, and democratically. Assange hypothesizes on October 9, “By refusing all mediation or dialog, Rajoy leaves only [Unilateral Declaration of Independence], which will unite Castilians behind [People’s Party] (@julianassange). Instead of communicating, Spain gave Puigdemont an ultimatum and deadline, and after that deadline passed, chose to implement section 155 of their constitution, which reads in full: 
1. If a Self-governing Community does not fulfil the obligations imposed upon it by the Constitution or other laws, or acts in a way that is seriously prejudicial to the general interest of Spain, the Government, after having lodged a complaint with the President of the Self-governing Community and failed to receive satisfaction therefore, may, following approval granted by the overall majority of the Senate, take all measures necessary to compel the Community to meet said obligations, or to protect the above-mentioned general interest.
2. With a view to implementing the measures provided for in the foregoing paragraph, the Government may issue instructions to all the authorities of the Self-governing Communities. (La Moncloa)
On Oct 26, Carles Puigdemont responded: 
“It is my duty and my responsibility to exhaust all, absolutely all, of the ways toward a negotiated and agreed solution to a conflict that is political and of a democratic nature…. I do not accept these measures because they are unjust and because they hide, almost without concealing it, the vindictive intention of a State that was defeated on October 1st… No one will be able to reproach the will of the Catalan side to engage in dialogue and politics. No one can say that we have not been willing to make sacrifices to ensure all the efforts were made to open dialogue… It is necessary that in the coming hours our commitment to peace and civility remains stronger than ever as this is the only way, I underscore, the only way that we can end in victory” (Puigdemont).
The relationship between Catalonia and Spain described in the Spanish Constitution is found in Section 2:
“The Constitution is based on the indissoluble unity of the Spanish Nation, the common and indivisible homeland of all Spaniards; it recognizes and guarantees the right to self-government of the nationalities and regions of which it is composed and the solidarity among them all” (La Moncloa)
Spain, and those who argue for a united Spain, say Catalonia is violating the Constitution by wanting to leave. The Catalan crisis is also referred to as the Spanish constitutional crisis. Spain wishes to enforce and maintain its own version of the rule of law. Craig Murray writes, 
“The Lisbon Treaty specifically incorporated the European Charter of Fundamental Rights into basic European Union law. There is no doubt whatsoever that the Spanish Guardia Civil on Sunday contravened the following articles:
1. The right to Human Dignity.  
        6. The right to liberty or security of person.
        11. Freedom of expression and association.
        54. Prohibition of Abuse of Rights” (Murray).
Murray also draws from Article 1(2) of the Charter of the United Nations, which states: 
“To develop friendly relations among nations based on respect for the principle of equal rights and self-determination of peoples, and to take other appropriate measures to strengthen universal peace” (qtd. in Murray).
Murray expounds on the disharmony of the Spanish Constitution with the laws of the UN and EU, to which Spain belongs and by which Spain ought to abide. 
“The rule of law is not everything. Apartheid was legally enforced in South Africa. Actually all dictators are very big on ‘the rule of law’. What Spain is attempting now to impose on Catalonia is rule of law without democracy. We are seeing a whipping up of hatred by a central government against a national and linguistic minority and a suppression of its freedoms and institutions. The rule of law within the EU has to stem from democracy, and to respect human rights. Neither is true in Rajoy’s Spain” (Murray).


Murray also compares the Catalan question with the EU’s response to the Kosovans, drawing the corollary that if the Spanish Constitution admits the existence of different “peoples” in its country, it cannot, under EU and UN laws and guidelines, deny those peoples’ right of self-determination.  By declaring the referendum illegal and sending national police to confiscate ballots, Spain prevented the Catalans from even the ability to express themselves. Murray writes, “It is not enough for Spain to say it is implementing law when the law itself is illegitimate” (Murray). Spain clearly loses appeals to any form of authority beyond its own brute strength, upon which it has relied during these events.
Beyond this, according to La Vanguardia citing Spanish Foreign Ministry spokesperson Tilman Renz, “Spain has rejected Swiss, and indeed, all, mediation over its dispute with Catalonia” (@julianassange). On Oct 26, Puigdemont approached Spain with an offer of a December 20th election, which Spain refused. Craig Murray notes, “The Spanish government’s refusal yesterday of the offer of a 20 December election in Catalonia vividly highlights that the thing they are most scared of is any kind of free and fair vote” (Murray). 


Julian Assange, paying close attention to the possible election developments, tweeted on Oct 25, 
“Spain’s plan to annex Catalonia is extremely disturbing. Arrest elected government, replace with viceroy. Emasculate parliament. Seize media. Seize police. Seize embassies. Seize funds. Ban demonstrations. Modify education system to make pro-Spain. Seize Internet” (@julianassange).


Murray echoes this assessment, “[The Spanish government wishes] to take over all the Catalan institutions and media, and institute a judicial ban on pro-Independence campaigning, before they allow any election – that is why they prefer a six month delay” (Murray). Albert Rivera, leader of Rajoy’s coalition partners Ciudadanos, supports these actions, saying, “I want Spain as a State to guarantee the results of those elections” (@julianassange). Spain, remarkably, has made no effort to conceal its authoritarian disposition, while it simultaneously pretends to care for democracy.

In conjunction with the heavy bias of the Spanish newspapers, the People’s Party “requested that Catalan TV be banned from broadcasting [that] evening’s massive demonstration” (@julianassange) Xavier García Albiol, the leader of the People’s Party in Catalonia, said that if Catalan public TV broadcast the rally, it would be “another disloyalty from this channel, which would favor an ideology that aims to break Catalonia from Spain” (ACN). In 1943, George Orwell, who fought in the Spanish Civil War, wrote in his essay, “Looking back on the Spanish War,” “In Spain, for the first time, I saw newspaper reports which did not bear any relation to the facts, not even the relationship which is implied in an ordinary lie… I saw, in fact, history being written not in terms of what happened but of what ought to have happened according to various ‘party lines’” (qtd. in Lattier) Spain will not even allow the investigation of the Franco Regime. Decades available to consolidate media power. What chance that anything has changed? 
David Alandete, the deputy director of El País, bemoans the ability of the global internet to circumvent Spain’s information monopolies, “Political leaders and traditional institutions have lost weight in influencing public opinion. Its place has been occupied by other actors, much more difficult to follow and control, and who are no longer confined to the political and media sphere of a single country” (Alandete). Amazingly, the People’s Party condones and publicizes the busing in of non-Catalan protesters to buffer pro-union demonstrations (@julianassange). The NNGG Spain, the New Generations of the People’s Party, posted on their twitter on Oct 7 a picture of Spaniards in a bus with the quote, “We leave from Madrid with mates. Today no Catalan is going to feel alone” (@NNGG_es). Spanish sympathizers claim that the majority of Catalans do not want independence from Spain, but “at the last Catalan parliamentary election the explicitly pro-Independence parties gained 48% and explicitly anti-independence parties gained 39%” (Murray). Spain shows a remarkable impunity in openly displaying authoritarian tendencies and unashamedly using heavily-biased and poor journalism and other unsavory tactics to reinforce their stance and control.
        The international and psycho-social-historical factors surrounding the Catalan crisis provide much overwhelming support for Catalan independence, and also the inevitable collapse of fake democracies. However, these vantages would take many more pages to detail, and even without them, the clear evidence of authoritarianism, overt intimidation, misuse of law, blatant propaganda and denial, and oppression of peaceful citizens show Spain to defy, without exaggeration, all known democratic principles in its reaction to and dealings with Catalonia. “Smaller states are not a great danger to anyone,” Murray writes on Oct 27, “The crushing of democracy in Catalonia, the fascist salutes on the streets, and the unabashed return of Francoist doctrine, is the real danger” (Murray). Whether or not Catalonia leaves Spain, its citizens deserve at the absolute least the ability to peacefully express their voice and opinions, without interference from the Spanish State’s authoritarian actions, of which I have only skimmed the surface, whose intent to subjugate a minority population within its own borders threatens democracy everywhere.
