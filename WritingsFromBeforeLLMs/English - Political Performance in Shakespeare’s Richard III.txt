


        Richard III details the complexity of maneuvering in the political world. The titular character, the Duke of Gloucester, decides to become king. However, to do so he must kill all those in the line of succession before him, and he also does this in a short space of time. Killing those before him is not difficult, and he has enough power to do it easily. The difficulty resides in doing it in such a way that not only is he not suspected, but his rule is also welcomed and seen as legitimate. The Duke of Gloucester accomplishes this through a system of political rumor and public display, focusing on his immediate family and the nobility, but he ultimately fails due to his betrayal of Buckingham. 
        The Duke of Gloucester’s modus operandi consists of tricking those around him into supporting his ascendancy and disposing of those who do not fall for his tricks. However, each time he kills someone critical or unaccepting of him, or otherwise in the way of his ascendancy, he must perform more convincingly for those that remain. As a result of this cycle, as the play progresses he finds himself in increasingly tighter and tighter situations, digging himself into a hole that ultimately results in him losing critical support from Buckingham and others and dying a pathetic death on the battlefield.
        The play begins with Gloucester plotting his first trick of political performance. Gloucester says in soliloquy, “Plots have I laid, inductions dangerous, / By drunken prophecies, libels and dreams, / To set my brother Clarence and the king / In deadly hate the one against the other” (1.1 32-35). This details the ‘how’ of his plan, but the most important part remains that he must appear innocent during all of these happenings. Gloucester does so by implying to Clarence that Queen Elizabeth has set King Edward IV against him, and solidifies the idea that they two, Gloucester and Clarence, are united against these injustices: “this deep disgrace in brotherhood / Touches me deeper than you can imagine” (1.1.111-112). He takes such care to have Clarence think that they are allied so that all with whom Clarence talks will hear from Clarence that Gloucester is his greatest friend, and to prevent Clarence from pleading to the king in the truth of matters. Gloucester says aside after Clarence leaves, “Simple, plain Clarence! I do love thee so, / That I will shortly send thy soul to heaven,” (1.1.118-119) revealing his lying nature. Later, Gloucester’s performance ensures one final failsafe in Clarence’s death. Clarence says to the murderers, “I will send you to my brother Gloucester, / Who shall reward you better for my life / Than Edward will for tidings of my death” (1.4.42-44). Clarence, who thinks that Edward hates him, and that Gloucester loves him, is, due to shock, bereft of his sole defenses of bribery and entreatment against the mercenary murderers when he finds that Gloucester is his killer.
        Gloucester makes a wise move in killing Clarence before the king dies, as this order of affairs obscures his plot to gain the crown. Additionally, he still must avoid any critical gaze from members of the court and to this end uses Queen Elizabeth as a scapegoat. Gloucester tells the news of Clarence’s death to the assembled court by acting outraged when Queen Elizabeth asks King Edward to call Clarence. Gloucester says, “Why, madam, have I offer'd love for this / To be so bouted in this royal presence? / Who knows not that the noble duke is dead? / You do him injury to scorn his corse” (2.1.79-82). By acting upset, dismayed, and outraged, he reinforces the perception that he and Clarence were close. Then, after the king and queen depart, he says to those who remain, “Mark'd you not / How that the guilty kindred of the queen / Look'd pale when they did hear of Clarence' death?” (2.1.135-137) In truth, Gloucester initially set the King against Clarence, but Gloucester is ultimately at fault for his death. Through his performance in front of the court, however, he assuages any suspicion against him. Since the king is poisoned before Clarence is jailed, and dies after Clarence dies, the court is left at most with the perception that Clarence died by the king’s rash actions and the king dies in turn from grief and regret. Gloucester, at this point, is free of suspicion, save from Queen Elizabeth, whom he blames.
        Continuing this general vein of assassination and political maneuver, Gloucester does away with all those against him and in the line of succession until only the young Prince of Wales and Duke of York remain. Conspiring with Buckingham, he proceeds to put on a performance that will embed his righteousness into the minds of the nobility, and thus his worth as king. Though Gloucester allays and prevents suspicion against himself, he must win favor for himself as well. He plans ahead of time to appear reluctant to take up the charge of kingship while at the same time having Buckingham descry the illegitimacy of the Prince of Wales. Buckingham brings the nobility to the preplanned place and says to Gloucester, who puts on airs of being bothered by this all, “You say that Edward is your brother's son: / So say we too, but not by Edward's wife” (3.7.176-177). and then, “Yet whether you accept our suit or no, / Your brother's son shall never reign our king” (3.7.213-214). Gloucester responds by saying, 


“Since you will buckle fortune on my back, / … / I must have patience to endure the load: 
/ But if black scandal or foul-faced reproach / Attend the sequel of your imposition, / Your mere enforcement shall acquittance me / From all the impure blots and stains thereof; / For God he knows, and you may partly see, / How far I am from the desire thereof” (3.7.226, 228-234). 


Through this combined performance of Buckingham and Gloucester in front of all the nobility, Gloucester further dissuades any suspicious thoughts to arise within their minds. His feigned protest and reluctant acceptance of the kingship, aided by Buckingham’s threats of civil unrest and usurpation, helps to convince the nobility that he is innocent of conspiring against the previous king and line of succession.
        Richard III cleverly shows the complex relationship between performance and political maneuver. The Duke of Gloucester has three main objectives in his performances. The first is to rid suspicion against himself in regards to the many deaths occurring. The second is to win the support of his family. The third is to win the support of the nobility. The only type of performance required is the type that works. For Gloucester, this type of performance is one of displaying love for his family, justified anger, and pious righteousness, all of which elevate him in others’ eyes. Though he ultimately fails by betraying Buckingham, Gloucester succeeds through his performance in discarding suspicion against himself, dividing the nobility against the ruling line and in favor of himself, and gaining what would appear to be a legitimate kingship. Due to the tragic end of Richard III, the former Duke of Gloucester, the play Richard III hints that the most “beneficial” performance is one of moderation and loyalty, for had Richard III not excessively conspired to kill the Prince of Wales and Duke of York and betrayed the Duke of Buckingham, he would likely have remained in power, having already convinced the nobility of his righteousness and legitimacy.
