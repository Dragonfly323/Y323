The following are two versions of the same story. The first version uses two verb tenses to separate the past and the present. The second version uses the same verb tense for both the past and the present. I wrote these for my ENGL 426: Creative Writing class. Unfortunately, it may be that you can only experience the story once, so you must choose in which manner you would first like to experience it. Please enjoy :)

        Margaret Sage watches the rain soak through the cement hole in the wall. The wall, she imagines, ought to feel her dislike of it. Her appreciation, too, she supposes –but mostly her dislike. She remembers wooden floors, but those were long gone, both in time and place. She watches the gray sky through the cement hole in the wall. 
        “Margie!”
Margaret jumped as Anne Walker startled her. She was not used to her neighbors visiting unannounced. The clack of Anne’s shoes disappeared on the muffling carpet, “Margie, dear, do say that you’ll be coming with us to the Platz later on!”
        Margaret gently placed her tea on the side table. “I was not aware you were going, Annie. You do mean much later on, don’t you?”
        “Margie, we’ll always wait for you,” Anne replied, sitting on the lay couch. A servant came to pour the visitor tea as Anne continued on.“William will be there, I know how you must think of him.”
        “Oh Annie, don’t you dare, you do know how I feel about that entire situation, and you’re not making me want to go,” Margaret replied, picking up her tea in a somewhat irritated manner.
        She wraps the coarse shawl tighter about her as a stray wind blows through the cement hole in the wall. She wonders if she should make a fire, keep it hidden. Days like these are when she most misses tea. She glances lethargically around the bare room, hovel, more like it, wondering what to do as she waits for William’s return.
        “We always go through this and you always say you don’t want to go and then you always go and you always tell me later endlessly what a good time you had,” Anne said, laying down in mock exasperation.
        Margaret exhaled shortly, looking at Anne. “Alright then, I’ll go, but we’re not staying on till midnight like last time. I mean it!”
        The sky darkens as Margaret sits on the cot in the cold cement room. She stares vacantly at a broken piano, in pieces, in a corner. How does a piano wind up in a place like this, she wonders.
        Margaret, Anne, and William sat at the café, enjoying the weather, talking, laughing, eating little cakes with their coffee. They first noticed the people in the courtyard pointing to the sky, looks of terror, or bewilderment, painting their faces. They three sat under an umbrella, unthinking, in shock, perhaps, as they watched the people begin to run and scream. Some others, like they, stayed still, staring mouth agape to the heavens while the mob flowed by like a raging river. Maybe it didn’t last so long as Margaret remembered, that initial moment of terror. Anne was lost in the crowd; William alone gripped her hand like iron and led her to shelter.
        The darkness of the sky seems to reach through that little cement hole in the wall, covering Margaret with the icy fingers of an approaching winter. She begins to relinquish hope that anything warmer will embrace her. She hears the cries through that little cement hole in the wall, the useless gunfire, the heavy, slow footsteps. She does not welcome the interruption of that lonely silence.














































































Margaret Sage sat in the old cot and remembered wooden floors. Persian rugs covered the centers, but she could still hear in her mind the short space of time when the shoes clacked between rooms. Shoes would always clack here, and she was glad in a depressing way that she had no hard shoes to bother with the constant noise. She watched a little pool spread from below the cement hole in wall.
        She gazed at the charred marks where they built fires from time to time, nothing like her glass fronted fireplace at home. Well, she thought, I suppose this is home now. I even have a piano, she added, glancing at their dwindling fuel supply in the corner, wires sprouting like an infestation of weeds. She laughed to herself and stopped when her eyes fell on the door.
        Looking out the window, she saw how nicely the sun glistened on the wet cobble, and the meat pie smelled so good. Unfortunately, she already told Anne she’d go with her and William to the Platz; the meat pie had to wait. She saw a cockroach near her foot and for a disgusting moment considered picking it off the cement floor and eating it. Fortunately for the little bug, she did not fall so low. She distracted herself by thinking of better things, better food. 
Her throat ached for the hot aroma of coffee, an often felt sensation when remembering that day. She never looked at the sky afterwards, not till much later, not till they had all gone. Running, a lot of running, throngs pressing them, incoherent voices, a ringing in her ear, all memories of a faded past. A faded yesterday plagued her like some ill-gotten disease, for though fifteen years passed, she knew that day as yesterday, always yesterday for her, the day her life ended. Every day in the room with the cement hole in the wall was only another day of dying.
William remained outside, somewhere, somewhere she did not know. Sometimes he was gone for days, leaving her alone, saving her yesterday only to leave her alone. He smiled at her over coffee and cakes while Anne pretended to be engrossed in a novella. He grabbed her hand, not Anne’s, as they three ran through the crowd. Anne never crossed his mind. 
Margaret heard gunfire through the little portal to the world, the cement hole in the wall. Her face drooped. She knew how useless guns were. She waited. She waited. She heard a terrible cry, a familiar cry, suddenly cut off. The wind blew into the room through the little cement hole in the wall, carrying the faintest odor of gunpowder. She waited. The silence that followed was a lonely silence, a forlorn quietness echoing around the gray walls. She resigned herself to its interruption, a heavy, dragging, thump.
