On Being Ill
“There is a virgin forest in each; a snowfield where even the print of birds’ feet is unknown. Here we go alone, and like it better so. Always to have sympathy, always to be accompanied, always to be understood would be intolerable. But in health the genial pretence must be kept up and the effort renewed–to communicate, to civilise, to share, to cultivate the desert, educate the native, to work together by day and by night to sport. In illness this make-believe ceases.”
“Divinely beautiful it is also divinely heartless.”
“In illness words seem to possess a mystic quality.”
“In health meaning has encroached upon sound. Our intelligence domineers over our senses. But in illness, with the police off duty… if at last we grasp the meaning, it is all the richer for having come to us sensually first, by way of the palate and the nostrils, like some queer odour.” (Tao: wisdom of heart over learning of head. Jung: collective unconscious. Kant: transcendental Schema.)

Modernity allowed virginia woolf to greater capture her thoughts, or the tao, just as the tao will always give birth to and seize upon everything. It is only natural that someone knowing the tao would seize upon the contemporary patterns to express it. So tao is always expressed. Woolf saw in modernity the essence of tao and fed it to herself, hoping that the newness of abstraction in literature might make her perception more clear, by stripping away the rigidity of logical thought, as her perception was not founded in logic, but in the inexpressible abstraction so typical of tao. She was hoping that if people would allow their consciousness to flow as her book does, they might become more aware of how all of reality flows, instead of being perpetually bound to endless thought loops of recurrent redundance. She uses the inanities and logic of the characters to show that all of the thoughts and self-propulsion and people reading and analysis and various types of knowledge all serve at missing the point, which underlies the entirety of the going ons, such as at the dinner.


On Being ill


Woolf uses a thene of patterns and daily going ons that pass unnoticed to the lay observer. However, due to their recurrent nature, the patterns have a habit of impressing upon everything a sort of constant hum, or song, which plays in every little aspect of life. People, as well, are trapped in this song, unwitting pawns to a seeming chaos that whirls and hurls itself at them, bringing them back again and again to places they know well but have somehow never quite learned. In the back of their mind, a ceaseless knocking, often ignored, grows louder, as the recurrent nature of life and patterns hint toward some greater truth, which, when casually grasped at, fades immediately from sight, leaving behind a confused onlooker, standing shaken by the buffeting winds.


We are always speaking in silence; it is only when we say nothing else that it is easier to hear.


With To the Lighthouse, Virginia Woolf crafts a window peering into a darkened room. The window is fragmented and dirty, but as one working with only the barest of elements, words upon paper, lacking the tone of the voice, the sway of the body, and the gaze of the eyes, one could hardly expect anything more. The book is only an image, a shadow of the truth, but in every word she asks us to look deeper, to find the answers she has so long and fruitlessly sought herself. If we peer carefully into this novel, and into ourselves, the fog upon the window may come to fade, the cracks repair, the grime vanish, until we find that we are inside that darkened room, and see the window for its true self: a mirror.


Examining a wide variety of seemingly disparate philosphers, we  learn a greater understanding of the limitation of language, as so perfectly exampled in To the Lighthouse. Through these philosophers, so often considered at odds, a unity emerges that speaks to a different truth beneath the words, on the margins of the page, hidden in the very binding of the mind. A nihilist, an existentialist, a stoic, an epicurean, a logician, a man thousands of years dead, and two graphic novel authors from Brazil, will all bear testament to the conflict of words as facts and words as images, determing in the end that experience alone can speak, and words are but the echoes of that utterance.
