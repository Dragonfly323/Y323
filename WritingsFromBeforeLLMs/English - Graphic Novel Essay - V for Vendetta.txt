Freedom is relative. What is true Freedom? Is it real or only a concept? Can it be secured by words or a constitution? Can Freedom and security coexist? Does Freedom exist through laws designed to protect it or is Freedom entirely dependent on the mind? Does Freedom include the freedom to take away your own Freedom? Alan Moore’s V for Vendetta’s main philosophical query deals with the true nature of Freedom and it’s relation to fear.
        In the beginning of the book Eevee, a young girl living in a dystopian fascist society, decides to sell her body for money so that she can live a better life. In the bottom left panel of the first page, we are shown her face through a mirror, symbolizing even before she wears a mask of makeup on the next page the false nature of her actions, as they are incongruous with her conscience, and thus not a reflection of her true self. This evidences a lack of freedom within her own mind, for whether by societal conditioning or inherent conscience or man’s law, she perceives her actions as wrong, and thus is a slave to the law, being unable to act in a true freedom in which the will expresses itself unadulterated. Despite it being her own body, or so she might believe, the subsequent scenes reveal that she does not really own it. The man she propositions reveals himself as a cop and accosts her. Fearing at first the badge of the fingerman, as lawbreakers are severely punished, she soon comes to fear the realization of her lack of ownership over her body, for the punishment for her offence gives the fingermen the freedom to do as they please, which is, in this case, to rape her. 
        The Fingermen in V for Vendetta exemplify the concept of the complete lack of individual freedom in terms of physical property. They have the power and authority to do as they please, all under the guise of protecting the nation from those who seek to tear it apart and disturb the peace and tranquility of England. Against such unopposable power, individual freedom and individual right ceases to exist. The first few pages of V for Vendetta confirm this reality.
        It has been shown that one cannot hold property save by the mercy of those in power. Where, then, does freedom lie, if at all? Can one have freedom of the mind? Or can the right conditions destroy the freedom of the mind, as well? The titular character V is the embodiment of freedom and exhibits a divine sort of freedom not often seen in any society. With his great power of freedom, he can take freedom away. The essence of freedom is courage, for with fear can nothing be free. V holds the courage to defy the state and wage war against it. His first individual victim in his war is the voice of Fate, Prothero. V manipulates the fears of Prothero, that his precious dolls might be harmed, to the extent of breaking his mind and rendering him catatonic, in essence destroying his freedom, if at least only temporarily.
        If the freedom of an individual can be manipulated and taken away, can freedom of the masses be taken away? The better question is whether it can be returned. Around page 112, V takes over the television station to broadcast anti-propaganda. When V begins to reach out to the public, he has but one purpose, which is to give the masses courage sufficient to overcome their fear. Through the constant surveillance, severe punishments for petty crimes, and general paranoia in the police state, the people have been subdued by fear: “What are they [the children] to make of your bullying, your despair, your cowardice and all your fondly nurtured bigotries?”(116) Fear is the only enemy of freedom. What is the significance of the belief that fear is the only enemy of freedom? The implications are far reaching and impactful. Take, for instance, a man unafraid of death, unafraid of the greatest miseries and tortures. What limits this man? Fear is the only limiting factor. V’s plan, as such, is to eliminate the aura of fear permeating Britain.         
        V, through the traumatic events of his past, learns that freedom, the right of the individual, outshines all other lights within man. V tastes the bitterness of imprisonment in Larkhill Resettlement Camp and, thanks to a neighboring prison mate, a former actress, learns that true freedom is found within the mind. He learns that through willpower and courage, one may hold the freedom of his mind, the last proverbial inch, as the actress called it. Such freedom is a resoluteness of the soul, and does not often find abode in the hearts of the average joe, admittedly, such freedom is rare, and V understands this. If this resoluteness of the soul were not rare, but common, V, a master of psychological play, as evidenced by his dealings with the Voice of Fate, could feasibly bring about the freedom within the leaders of the nation, who fear rejection, and thus cling and are addicted to their power, and change the nation that way. 
        V understands that not everyone has the ability to be truly free, so he does not seek to make everyone free, for he cannot do so. V’s objective in giving the masses courage is not to give them freedom, but to provide the conditions for freedom. Inherent within freedom is the ability to choose to be free. The current conditions are not conducive to free choice. However, due to the overwhelming fear of the communities, and with freedom being abstract, V must maneuver his agenda in such a way that the people make the choice to be free. Essentially, the only way the people can be free is by choosing to be so, and V gives them a taste of freedom through the revolution of fascist England.
        Freedom is an abstract. V demonstrates this by freeing Eevee from the prison of her mind. The process culminates on page 172 where the lower right panel shows Eevee as a white silhouette on the pinnacle of a building, bringing to mind the transfiguration of Jesus Christ on Mount of Olives, even as V says the panel before, telling Eevee to “become transfixed… become transfigured.” This is the hidden theme of the book. True freedom is equivalent to a complete and utter lack of fear and ego, or rather, a total filling of peace, which is opposite of fear. Being filled with peace is for all the mystical understanding of the state of heaven. Peace and fear can be represented simply as the difference between spirituality and religion. Religion, represented by the fascist state, frightens believers into obedience and oppression of the natural way of life. Spirituality, symbolized by V’s anarchy, is the shedding of fear and enrobing of freedom or peace, putting off the old man to put on the new.
        Also in Alan Moore’s V for Vendetta is the Passion of Christ, or in this case, Eevee, on pages 170 and 171. These pages show the death of Eevee’s ego and desire, as Eevee says “I can’t feel anything! There’s nothing to feel!” The upper left panel on page 171, where V tells Eevee, “They offered you a choice between the death of your principles and the death of your body” brings to mind the saying attributed to Jesus: “For whosoever will save his life shall lose it: and whosoever will lose his life for my sake shall find it.”(Matt. 16:25) On page 171, the very next panel, the middle top, is the perfect image of Michelangelo’s Pieta. Another Biblical reference is found in the last panel on page 171. When V says “All the blindfolds are gone”, the reader may recall the words attributed to Jesus referring to those who are under the bondage of fear, “they seeing see not; and hearing they hear not, neither do they understand.”(Matt. 13:13)
One aspect intertwined with the concept of putting off fear is the concept of individual choice. The fascist ideology of the state contrasts this aspect to such an extent it threatens to overshadow it. Yet where the opportunity to choose lies the ability lies. Alluding to the often debated idea that “the kingdom of heaven is within you”(Luke 17:21), on page 215, in the middle three panels, metaphorically, Eric Finch looks at the “mad pattern” of life, wondering “Who imprisoned me here? Who keeps me here? Who can release me? Who’s controlling and constraining my life, except.. … me?” In the middle third panel, the explosion produced from his mixing of powders, symbolic of alchemy, which actually is an allegory for transmuting the soul, represents the light of epiphany, or enlightenment, as Eric Finch also finds heaven, shouting “Freeeeee!” in the last panel on page 215, running into a great light.
        The metaphor continues on to show what the enlightenment entails. He sheds his clothes, symbolic of the constraints of civilized society, he thinks in his heart, “Vaulting, veering, vomiting up the values that victimized me, feeling vast, feeling virginal… was this how he felt? this verve, this vitality… …this vision. La Voie… La Vérité… La Vie.” The values that victimized him are the moral, ethical, and legal codes under which he lived in the fascist nation, which is symbolic of religion. Accompanying his enlightenment is a sudden clarity of thought, peace, and acceptance of life, or la vie. This closely reflects many philosophies on the subject of enlightenment, which are in general agreeance that enlightenment is essentially acceptance of everything in the utmost and purest sense.
        After V dies, the significance of his abstract actions is revealed. On page 252, in the top right panel, in which not coincidentally hangs a cross, Eric Finch tells Dominic, “People need symbols, Dominic. He understood that. We’ve forgotten it.” Through his manipulations, V helps the population to genuinely seek freedom, which is of the utmost necessity if it is to be realized. V for Vendetta shows not only what true freedom means, the death of the ego and fear, but how freedom can be found. As freedom is in the mind, and, in some ways, the perception, enlightenment requires an alteration of the perception. This usually entails a shedding of false realities, all of which are necessarily polluted with fear, until the soul has no filter through which it sees reality, being able to see for the first time that which is directly present in the here and now. V for Vendetta answers the question of the true nature of freedom in extensive detail and warns the reader of the dangers of fear and that fear exists solely in the mind and so can always be overcome, thus resulting in enlightenment, or salvation.
