Maximilien Robespierre excited the passions of the mob through the confident espousement of his perception of the French aristocracy. The strength of movement lies solely in the level of force in comparison to the weight of that which is to be moved. At the pinnacle of the French Revolution, Maximilien Robespierre announced to the people the way to salvation. The godhead, in his mind, “created men to help each other, to love each other mutually, and to attain to happiness by the way of virtue.” [11] It seems, at first, sufficiently reasonable to the rational mind to back such statements of peace and love but Robespierre goes on to say, “it is He who impels the just man to hate the evil one,” and, later on, “evil belongs to the depraved man who oppresses his fellow man or suffers him to be oppressed.”[11] Essentially, according to Robespierre, the godhead desires for all men to love each other and to be virtuous, but, in order to achieve this state of cohabitation, anyone who disagrees with Robespierre’s view of things must be destroyed as enemies of liberty and to refrain from doing so is to deny the duty “to purify the earth which they have soiled, and to recall to it the justice that they have banished!” [11] Robespierre preaches broad tenets upon which everyone is able to agree only to intermingle violent charges to the susceptible and fervid minds of the throngs. The cries of justice form the irony of every revolution by impassioning a persuaded morality to decapitate the head of reason. Maximilien Robespierre’s use of emotional manipulation caused the Terror of the French Revolution.
        In speaking of justice, what constitutes morality? For is not justice the paying to morality of its deserved dues, to bring balance to the equation of right and wrong where hitherto it has been tipped unfairly? Robespierre called the people of France to be the enactors of justice, to reign divine retribution upon the dissenters and oppressors of mankind. A difficulty arises, however, in the relation of justice to morality, and indeed in justice and morality themselves alone. The senses of morality and justice are emotional ones, and thus subjective, and ably persuaded. Anthony Ashley-Cooper, 3rd Earl of Shaftesbury claimed, however, 


“that, in addition to the senses enumerated by Locke, men were equipped with an ‘inward eye’ that enabled them to perceive the morally good. Moral perceptions became available to the mind via inborn sentiments. Feelings of benevolence, pity, love, and gratitude gave shape to moral judgment and rendered moral action pleasurable.”[9]


Let it be assumed that the Earl of Shaftesbury thinks correctly in his assumption that there was an inborn morality which gave way to positive actions. How might this inborn morality manifest itself, when so often the moral stances of one differ from those of another? For instance, there might some be who would spare the nobility and dissenters of the Revolution the death penalty, acting in obedience to what they believe to be their inborn morality of peace or at least practicality, working on logic of better end. Others, not so lenient, such as Robespierre, would enact strict and merciless justice on the enemies of liberty, in obedience to what they believed to be inborn morality, an inborn morality that allowed for any means to an ideal end. With the use of these examples the surface, and thus acting, morality proves itself to be in persuasion of emotions and perspective. Therefore, what is truly right and wrong bears no consequence in the events of the French Revolution. All that bears consequence is what the people believe to be right and wrong.[9]
 The people are subject to the persuasion of emotion, and credibility, and logic. Robespierre had two of these in abundance and could fake the third. Robespierre had credibility due to his position on the States-General and his powerful use of language easily aroused emotion, whether in support or defiance, for his ideas. Did Robespierre have the ability to rouse his people to their part in the ongoing revolution? 


        “Psychologist Daniel Wegner’s research into what he calls the ‘ironic process of mental control’ suggests that attempts to remove a thought from attention and attempts to retain a thought in attention are both likely to produce increased recurrence of the thought. Thus, by extension, to say to oneself or to another ‘I am angry’ or ‘I am not angry’ are both likely to bring forward and enhance awareness of angry feelings and their triggers.”[9]


Quite simply, yes. Through repetition of emotionally charged words, Robespierre caused his audience to reflect upon the meaning of those words and consequently aroused stronger manifestations of the emotions associated with them. For many, the Revolution was a chaotic foray of strife and contention. Robespierre answered the ‘why’ of the revolution in its plethoras of violence and united his people under the words of “liberty, equality, fraternity.”
Still, one who does not feel wronged will feel no desire for justice. How was it that the people came to feel wronged? Even Robespierre with all his skill in oration could not conjure a feeling of wrongness within a contented people. Admittedly, 


“With famine and disorder came a financial crisis. The heavy cost of aiding the colonists in the American Revolution, the lavish spending of Queen Marie Antoinette and her favorites, the bizarre tax system which exempted the feudal nobility and the church had brought the realm to the verge of bankruptcy.” [8]


The French people as a whole may have required food, and might possibly have understood the power that they held, despite not being of an aristocratic class[6]. Indeed, “the central ideas of the Enlightenment are identified and are found to correspond to the essential characteristics of the market economy which produced the bourgeoisie.”[1] The Enlightenment allowed for the financial growth of the non-nobility but upon finding the growth to be inherently limited, effects of the Enlightenment caused them to become aware of the limits of their success[3]. As a result, with time, they slowly grew the seed of discontentment and bore the bitter fruit of the cocoa bean. The factor of emotional discontentment that allowed for Robespierre to inflame the passions of the people has its origins in the effects of the Enlightenment period in France. 
How did they become aware of the non-necessity of their state, as a slave might become aware that no universal law requires him to be a slave? P.J.B. Gerbier, a contemporary of the Revolution, wrote in June of 1789, “Where does so much mad agitation come from? From a crowd of minor clerks and lawyers, from unknown writers, starving scribblers who go about rabble-rousing in clubs and cafes.”[3] In the beginning of the Enlightenment age, in some sense the aristocracy supported the newcomers of the age but all the nobility had their chosen writers to support; new thinkers continued coming into existence in society, but found no one to support them. This is the view espoused by Robert Darnton, a lead researcher into the intellectual state of pre-revolutionary France:


“Late arrivals found most benefits taken and their way to success blocked. Imbued with the enlightenment belief that merit would yield rewards, they became indignant at their indifferent reception. Frustration, reinforced by their inability to earn a living, led them to a “Grub-Street” existence and to the development of a powerful indictment of the aristocracy. In a nutshell, they came to write and believe that the top layer of French society was rotten and was destroying the society.”[3]


A key factor of the phenomenon of discontentment within French society is due to these spurned intellectuals, or philosophes, who spread their views through an illegal book trade operating within pre-revolutionary France. In pre-revolutionary France, anything seditious or in poor opinion of the king, clergy, church, or government constituted illegality. As such, not being able to publish books openly, liberal writers sent their manuscripts out of the country, had them printed, and then had them smuggled back into France to be sold on the literary black market. In this way, the discontentment of this class of authors began to infect the public whose minds their works influenced. Their works helped the public come to terms with a feeling of “wrongness” and the candid manner of the fictitious writings gave the people the courage and persuasion to question the purported competency of the monarchy, aristocracy, and clergy.[2] 
Historians often argue about the role of the Enlightenment and Enlightenment thinking in the French Revolution. The Enlightenment may not have directly caused the French Revolution but it did play an integral part in the birth of a revolutionary mindset by undermining the authority of the church and the monarchy. Both the church and the monarchy at the time claimed their power by divine right, that their rule and authority were god-given and ordained. However, once the Enlightenment came, it “was the end of a spiritual world ruled by angels and demons, cherubim and seraphim, Beelzebub and Satan.”[4] At one time, religion was able to claim that god ordained this and that particular moral rule was to be followed and this was the correct interpretation of God’s word and that particular thing made you worthy of salvation or worthy of damnation and so on ad infinitum. Monarchies, as well, being closely tied to some religion, could possess their own versions of divine authority to quiet and control those under their rule. Thanks to the direct efforts of philosophes and their contribution to the advent of the popular use of reason, the bulk of the populace was given allowance in contemporary society to doubt and eventually rebel against the churches and monarchies assertions.[12] No longer would one need fear the alienation of his peers if he were to express views incongruous with the church. While there have always been fearless or uncaring individuals who did not mind others opinions in regard to their own, it was not until the Enlightenment era that the bulk of the French people, the conforming majority, were allowed the ability to express a contrary opinion of the ruling bodies, both of society, that of the church, and the law, that of the government, all while remaining on the effortless wave of assimilation.[12]
This pervasive quality of the Enlightenment age, among other things, on French society examples itself when


“immediately after the fall of the Bastille, young men and women, among them children, formed political associations and military bodies, gathered in assemblies, and sent addresses and delegations to Paris to ensure the members of the National Constituent Assembly of their entire support.”[5]


Within contemporary French society, the ideals and spirit of the Enlightenment, in its “desacralization” of the culture and society and its support of the arts and sciences, provided a tool that the people could use to combat their circumstances.[12]
        The more abstract Enlightenment ideals pertain to self-efficacy, the ability of man to reason, the virtue of the mind and of the human passion, and the potential of the individual to be free. As such, a slight irony abounds when the people exchange in their conversations with each other the authority of the church with the authority of Enlightenment thinkers such as 
Voltaire and Rousseau, as they have only succeeded in abandoning one master for another. However, despite this irony, this exchanging of masters has powerful effects. When the majority of France ascribed itself to a master other than the officially established one, an imbalance appeared. France underwent drastic changes before, during, and after the Revolution. Before the Enlightenment, within France lay an undercurrent of religious authority, the claim of power on that which by rule cannot be known, and the resorting to ancient texts to provide a foundation of rule. The Enlightenment was a tide of rationality and science rising in usurpation of the views of the right to power held at the time. Now, power was claimed by that which can and must be known, no longer believing for the sake of believing but proving what things were and were not true. This current proved stronger than the existing one. The king’s and church’s boats, which had sailed on the current of religious authority under the auspices of telling the people that it was neither possible nor morally right to rule themselves, suddenly found themselves overwhelmed by a deluge of contrary thought, which had previously only ever been at most an easily dealt with annoyance of an eddie or some unruly wind.
        So, the French Revolution began, in it’s myriad fronts and purposes. As the historic reality of the French Revolution was a series of revolutions, very different in their aims and therefore in any theoretical affiliations they may have had,[4] only one claim is examined here, that the Enlightenment presented a sufficiently alternate and substantial viewpoint capable of standing against the established doctrine and regime. The primary aspect of the French Revolution focused upon here is the influence of Robespierre on the movement. Thanks to the effects of the Enlightenment on the public psyche, Robespierre had a firm groundwork with which he might persuade the people to action. In accordance to Enlightenment ideals and in ease provided by their effects, “it was not difficult, when the leaders of the tiers etat made their bid for power, to envisage their aim as the transference of the sovereignty from the monarchy to the people.”[4]
        Robespierre used and abused the enlightenment ideals to further his own vision of how the world should be. In regards to the Robespierre movement, the Revolution 


“strayed from the primrose path of enlightened happiness to the strait and narrow road of Jacobin virtue, from the principle of representative and constitutional government to the rule of an authoritarian elite, from the philosophes’ ideal of peace to the revolutionaries’ crusading war and the Napoleonic dream of conquest.” [4] 


Robespierre subtly spun the ideals of the Enlightenment with which the majority agreed and presented a call to violence and retribution in the interests of the liberty of the formerly oppressed, and the protection of natural rights, completely deluding the people into forgetting that, according to the original spirit of the Enlightenment, liberty and natural rights were just as much the heritage of the criminal as the free man. Additionally, in regards to criminality, in reference to the spirit of the Enlightenment, as well to the subjectivity of morality, could anyone actually be guilty or innocent? The point being that, despite the supposed rationality purported by those who claimed to be adherents to the Enlightenment ideals, some lead figures, Robespierre in particular, adulterated certain aspects of the Enlightenment to suit their own desires. This is not to say that Robespierre did not believe the things he preached but rather that he too was a victim of his own emotional persuasion, abandoning the pure logic found inherent within the spirit of the Enlightenment. Robespierre fell helpless to the fervor of the mob he inspired, the two elements wrapping each other into a cocoon of fiery madness.[7] 
        Maximilien Robespierre used language to victimize the mob, make enemies out of those opposed to the Revolution, and to pervert Enlightenment ideals to the point where they urged violence and retribution, a mockery of their original intent. Maximilien Robespierre, in his speech “Justification of the Use of Terror”, states:


        “This great purity of the French revolution’s basis, the very sublimity of its objective, is precisely what causes both our strength and our weakness. Our strength, because it gives to us truth’s ascendancy over imposture, and the rights of the public interest over private interests; our weakness, because it rallies all vicious men against us, all those who in their hearts contemplated despoiling the people and all those who intend to let it be despoiled with impunity, both those who have rejected freedom as a personal calamity and those who have embraced the revolution as a career and the Republic as prey.”[10]


Robespierre appeals to senses of justice and virtue, noble aspirations as they are, by using words like “sublimity,” “strength,” “truth’s ascendancy,” and “rejected freedom.” The words “sublimity of its (the Revolution’s) objective”, or, for accuracy, their French equivalent, saddled the minds of the audience with a sense of rightness, a sense of justice, a sense of strength from doing what is positive, approved, supported, and accepted. At this point, the audience is more inclined to accept what follows, being in support of what has already been said. When Robespierre says “our strength and our weakness”, he introduces an opposition to the sublime objective of the Revolution. He reinforces this idea of an enemy by providing easily acceptable and emotionally charged dichotomies such as “truth’s ascendancy over imposture” and “the rights of the public interest over private interests”.  He thus unites his audience against a common enemy, in his implication, the enemy of truth and the rights of man. His words seem logical and it can be assumed that the majority agree with it up to this point. Being in such a state of agreeance, and in the fervor of the moment, surrounded by a supportive mob, the normally skeptic listener would have had greater difficulty rejecting what came next. Robespierre presents to the audience the face of the enemy, continuing to use emotionally charged words, saying that the great purity “rallies all vicious men against us,” and accuses them of seeking to “despoil” the people and even marks the neutral as enemies saying “all those who intend to let it be despoiled”.  In one short section of a speech which multiplies these examples, Robespierre has used a myriad of tricks of emotional manipulation, first affecting within the audience a strong sense of justice, an antagonism against the enemy of that justice, and adds a face to that enemy of justice, which must in turn be crushed, ending with saying, in effect, that any one bordering on neutrality is either with the cause of justice or against it, thus simultaneously providing the audience motivation to enact justice and fear if they do not.
        Robespierre’s emotionally charged and manipulative speeches had a direct effect in causing the mass hysteria which allowed for the Terror to occur during the French Revolution. While more moderate groups like the Girondists wished to spare the King, if only to use as a bargaining chip with foreign countries, the radicalism of Robespierre and the Jacobins caused mind-boggling amounts of violence and bloodshed to the point of sickening excess, executing civilians without trial, and for charges so trivial as vague suspicion. Robespierre created a monster of paranoia and death in rousing the nation to the Terror only to fall victim to his own inventions when others understood that he was perhaps the greatest enemy France would face in order to achieve peace.
