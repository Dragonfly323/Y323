The Debt of Time










        “We’ll all join the war!” Jean-Charles raised his wine to his friends.
“Here!” They responded in unison, downing their drinks. They four had spent the night drinking up courage, talking of the broken tension and the world war that now surrounded them.
        “What land have we, save the land where we drink?” Jean-Charles spoke in liquored words. “Yet we fall under threat of the English and their slaves. We are beset on all sides, brothers, and shall we stand down? No! Who can love their country, and not give his life for it? No death could be more glorious than a death for the land we love!”
        Jacques and Pierre sounded enthusiastically but Lamont hesitated.
“Lamont, Louis Lamont, my friend, have you no love for France?” Jean-Charles said, noticing his silence.
        “Now, Charles, I do love France, but I have spent time in England, and I have many friends there; they are not loyalists. Yet when we fight these wars, whom do we fight? Do we fight others who hate us, or do we fight their king, and their policies? I have seen beauty in their land and in ours. Cannot I hope for some better way than war?”
        “Lamont,” Jacques slurred. “You are drunk.”
“No, no.” Jean-Charles waved his hand lazily through the air. “Let us give him the courteous reply deserving of our friendship. Lamont, need I remind you the countless atrocities and displeasures caused us by the English and their allies? They have stolen what is rightly ours and today demand more! Do you believe we ought simply to hand all of the territories to them? You would bring us to ruin! What sane man thinks that the hostile English would not jump to take us when we have no money and no defence? That is as stupid as the peace.”
        “I went to England during the peace!” Lamont responded passionately. “I know it! I know their people and their thoughts and they are the same as yours! I do not know the answer, perhaps there is none. But war? Must we have war, again? They think as well as we that the treaty was unfair. I do not disagree, but we must find peace, or else this war will never end.”
        “We found a peace,” Jacques pointed at Lamont with his wine cup. “And look where it’s got us. I’ll tell you. Nowhere. And that’s where this is going too. Nowhere. Idealism may find soil in a- a true peace, but not now, and not here.”
        A short silence followed. Pierre spoke. “Well, friends, perhaps tomorrow we shall all think it through and come to an agreement. I am all for a pact of brothers, to fight for France, but I am disturbed by Lamont’s words, and they confuse me, though perhaps it is the wine. And with that,” Pierre downed his drink and placed pay on the table. “I head home.”
        “Agreed.” Jacques studied his half cup of wine for a moment before emptying it.
“We’re all drunk, I suppose.” Lamont finished his drink as well.
        “Oh, so we’re off, then? To tomorrow!” Jean-Charles toasted and breathed deep before downing his own.
        “Will you walk home with us, Charles?” Lamont asked, putting on his coat.
“No, no, head on, head on.” Jean-Charles stretched back in his seat and yawned. “I’ll make my own way in time.”
        “Very well, till tomorrow.”
“Till tomorrow, all.” And the three left Jean-Charles in the dingy tavern to ponder. He declined the barmaid’s offer of another drink, and listened to the conversations around the tavern. Before long, another voice interrupted his thought.
        “You’d do well to listen to your friend.” Jean-Charles hadn’t noticed the grimy, cloaked figure sitting in the corner. 
        “And you say, why?”
“War’s glorious, isn’t it? Of course, you’ve got to think that. You’ve got to make it something beautiful and honorable. But it isn’t beautiful and honorable. Not at all.”
        Jean-Charles studied the face beneath the hood. The man was young, maybe late 20’s at most. “You were in the war?”
        “Yes. I was in the war. I was in many wars.”
“How can you not feel, then, that it was never finished? That the treaty was useless? That they are our pure enemies, as they have always been?” Jean-Charles displayed confidence, but it faltered. 
        “You don’t listen, boy.” The man gruffed.
“I am hardly younger than you.”
        The man stayed silent for a moment. “I fought under Maurice de Saxe.” Jean-Charles’ eyes widened. “At the Siege of Maastricht.”
        He bowed his head to the man. “You were at that battle? Certainly you must feel,” he hesitated. “Certainly you must feel that, that, well, some anger, some indignation for that all you fought, all for which Maurice de Saxe fought, was taken away at the stroke of a pen!”
        “I’m not here to talk politics with you.” The man sipped his beer.
“What-” Jean-Charles flustered. “What-, I don’t understand. What are you saying?”
        “I’m not saying anything.” The man sighed. “ I’m just an old man, born into silence. And you’d never believe the truth.” Then the man half added to himself, “though I suppose I’ve nothing to lose in telling you.”
        “The truth?”
“I am 300 years old. Well, almost. And here you are ready to die.”
        Jean-Charles laughed. “You’re 300 years old? You expect me to believe-” The man interrupted him with a few words in a strange dialect. Jean-Charles gave him an odd look. “What? I can hardly understand you.”
        “That.” The man replied. “Is what the French spoke when I was a boy, when I was your age. You need not believe me.”
        Jean-Charles thought for a moment, studying the man’s demeanor. “Why are you telling me this? Are you taking me for a fool?”
        The man laughed softly. “No, not a fool. Not a fool in this. You can be a fool without me. I tell you because you are so ready to die, and I want to give you a choice.”
        “Alright, I’ll humor you. What kind of choice?”
“I fought at the Siege of Maastricht in 1673. I lived to lay siege to it again only 8 years ago, in 1748. When the peace came, I took off my armor, and left it where it lay. For my life, I have lived in war. I was born in war, and I have known war for most of my days. I know full well what enemies the English are of France, even before there was a France. The Caroline War was my mother, and the Lancastrian War was my father. I developed a taste for it. I was good at it. You’d imagine, were you so old. But taking the same city twice showed me the endless cycle.” He paused.
“I’ve not stayed for a sermon, old man, if you are so old. Tell me your choice or I shall lose my patience.” 
“How impertinent. Very well.” The man leaned forward. “Are you willing to die?”
“For my country, yes.” Jean-Charles broadened his chest.
        The man chuckled. “So, are you willing to live?”
Jean-Charles started. He glanced about, unsure. “What do you mean?”
        “Would you like to live,” he spoke softly. “Like me, for hundreds of years? Ever ready to fight? Ever ready but never needing to die? What better way to truly serve your country?”
        Jean-Charles fidgeted slightly.
        “You will die eventually, but not for a very long time. What do you think?”
Jean-Charles remained silent. “Why?” He finally answered. “Why would you offer it to me? There are nobler men, stronger men, men with more influence and honor than I. Why me?”
        The man’s laugh echoed through the tavern. “No reason. Although, you do have to be willing to die in order to live. Aside from that, no, nothing special about you. Mark it to my whimsical nature. Do you know Doctor Faustus?”
        “The- the play? The English one?”
“Yes, the English one. I’m surprised you know it.”
        “I have only heard of it, one of many English abominations.”
“Now do you really think that or do you speak for another? Tell me, you know of the story?”
        Jean-Charles did not answer immediately. “Do you mean to say that you are the devil? And that I am to pay some horrid price for what you offer?”
“No! No, not at all. The devil is much older than I. I do offer a devil’s bargain, however, but only the one thing will I give you. Some blessings come with their own curse.”
“Eternal life? What curse comes with that?”
“Dear God, boy, you think I’d give you eternal life? No man could commit such a crime. I think I will go, if you want such a thing. I revoke my offer and I’ll leave you in peace to die for your country.” The man stood up to leave.
“No! Wait.” Jean-Charles thrust his hand forward. “What do you mean it would be a crime? Please, tell me more. I did not mean to offend.”
“My whimsical nature knows its limits. I think the wine has gotten to my head. I ought never to have mentioned any of this to you.” The man walked out of the tavern.
Jean-Charles ran out of the tavern. Few people roamed the streets that time of night so he easily spotted the man bustling down the lane. He jogged after him. “Wait!”
The man turned to address him. “Go away, boy!” Then ducked into a narrow side street.
Jean-Charles, sprinting now, carefully navigated the cobble. The effects of the wine lingered enough to cause him concern. Turning the side street, he saw the man’s back disappear at the top of some stairs.
        “Please, sir!” He shouted, crawling up the steps. When he reached the top he saw the man waiting for him at the crossroads of two side streets. 
“It was a lie.” He said. “It was all a lie. Drunken ramblings! I’m a mad man, don’t you see? I’ll not have you following me all the night! Go home!”
        Breathless from the sprint and the stairs, resting his hands on his knees, Jean-Charles panted, “I don’t believe you. I don’t know all what I do believe but I know you’re as old as you say you are.”
        The man laughed incredulously. “You’re drunk! It was a rag! I’m scarce older than you!”
“My grandmother.” Jean-Charles straightened up, breathing a few deep breaths, regaining his composure. “Sang me a lullaby when I was a child. She said it was hundreds of years old, from the time of Saint Louis. I can’t remember it well, but what you said tonight called it to memory. The sounds were the same.”
        The man stood staring at Jean-Charles. At last, he turned to leave. “Go away. I made those words up. You’re memory is wrong, or it was a coincidence.”
        “You didn’t.” Jean Charles said. “Maybe you did. But it doesn’t matter. If you are telling the truth, I would give anything for what you offer.”
        The man stopped walking. He made a false-start as if unsure, but then stood still. “Boy, do you have a mother?”
“Yes, yes I do.”
“A sister?”
“No. No sister, but two brothers, older than I, who live in the Americas and already fight in the war.”
        “And a lover?” The man turned his head toward Jean-Charles. “Do you have a lover?”
        “No, no lover. None I could call my own.”
“And what of your friends? Your friends you swore this very night to die by?”
        “Yes, they are my friends, but why are you asking me this?” He waved his arms.
“I told you there was no price. But there is.” The man gazed at the stars now.
        “What- what is it? Please, tell me!” 
“You’d really give anything, huh.”
        “Anything of my own, yes. But what are you implying? Speak your mind, man!”
“I won’t take anything from you. I’d like this moment to disappear. To stop myself from giving you this curse. To save you from your own blindness.”
        Jean-Charles squinted his eyes and shook his head. “I don’t care the cost.”
The man laughed softly, and, smiling, looked at Jean-Charles. “Why do you humans seek so avidly to escape death. What keeps you so ignorant, so in the dark of it’s meaning and purpose that you would give anything to forego it?”
        “Please, sir. I would give my life.” He croaked
“Oh no, you will give much more than that, but I see that wisdom and reason won’t reach your ears.” The man put his hand into his pocket, hesitated, and then reached into his other pocket and pulled out a rather plain looking vial of amber liquid. 
Jean-Charles stood speechless. The man walked up to him and held his hand out. He reached out for it, only for the man to close his fist and pull it back. “Are you sure?” 
The youth nodded slowly in reply, his eyes glued to the vial. 
“Here you go.” The man handed him the vial.
Jean-Charles stood studying the vial, holding it up to the light of the moon, questioning its contents.
“Mix it with burnt coffee before you drink it. But you must not give it to anyone else, just as will be yours alone all the bitter draughts that follow.”
Jean-Charles grasped the vial tightly and looked at the man. “I will put your gift to good use. Now tell me, what must I give?”
“Don’t you understand? I won’t take anything from you. I am not the debt collector. Time will name your price.”
“Time alone?” Jean-Charles pondered the words. He looked up at the stars, hopeful of the long life granted to him. “You never told me your name.” He looked back to the man, but the man was gone.

1886
        Jean-Charles sighed as he took his jacket and cap off. He made his way slowly down the hall and stepped into his library. A soft chuckle escaped his brown beard when he saw the old man sitting there, looking out the window at the changing colors of early Autumn.
        “After all these years,” Jean-Charles sat down in the armchair opposite the man. “I don’t know what to say.”
        The man gazed silently at the maples and firs swaying in the wind. “Your house,” he turned to Jean-Charles. “Is quite beautiful. You hate the English a bit less, then?”
        Jean-Charles smiled morosely, “The gothic revival began in England around the time I was born. It reminds me of their lives, the lives of all the ones I didn’t consider, the ones I didn’t value.”
        “War too, then,” the old man returned his gaze to the wind dancing leaves outside, “has lost savor for you.”
        Jean-Charles nodded, “Too late, I fear. My immortality blinded me, even to the deaths of my friends. They only spurned me to vengeance. After the Revolution here, which was rather tame, now that I think of it, I traveled back to France, and you well know what happened there.”
        “The Terror.” The old man closed his eyes and lay a hand over his face.
        Jean-Charles looked down at the rug. His breath fought the tightness in his chest. He regained his composure after a while and looked back at the man with face obscured. Jean-Charles smiled, “That was quite enough for me. More than enough. I- I- yes, I am done now. Done with that.”
        The old man took a deep breath and looked into Jean-Charles eyes. “When I met you all those years ago, you wanted to live for war. Everyone you knew is gone now. Have you found another reason for life?”
        “I haven’t a choice do I? I am as you, doomed to live forever. I have resigned myself, and live in a world of books.”
        “We are not the same. The elixir I gave you was diluted. It had only one drop mixed with water. You will live some 30 years or so more. The wrinkles show on your eyes. Jean-Charles, I offer you the choice anew, and you may live forever.”
        Jean-Charles stared blankly at the old man. Tears silently welled in his eyes and overflowed to stream down his still face. “You mean,” he said weakly. “I can die?”
        “Yes, of course.” The old man reached in his ragged coat pocket and pulled out a vial of ethereal golden liquid. It shone like fire. “But you may live as well. Travel with me, there is much to see, much that we can do. I first had to give you the taste of time, but there are mysteries I have found in the world in the years that I have lived, mysterious privy only to those immune to death.” He placed his hand on Jean-Charles’ shoulder. “Come with me. There is joy beyond the pain. You have lived a normal life, and so can now live an extraordinary one. You and I will travel through the ages. We will see civilizations rise and fall. We will be gods among men, wise as serpents, creatures of a greater existence.”
        “I- I hardly know you.” Jean-Charles shuddered, powerful emotions rippling their way through his body.
        “I know,” the old man sighed. “But it must be now that you decide. I ask that you trust me on account that I did not at first give you the straight draught.”
        Jean-Charles crumpled, his chest heaving in great sobs. “I- I-” He struggled to speak through his tears. “What a gift death would be… what is immortality alone?”
        The old man’s lip trembled. “What indeed.” He stood up and patted Jean-Charles assuredly on the back. “You do not need to be alone. I will walk with you. We can give others the draught as well.”
        Jean-Charles shook with rage and screamed at the man, “Where were you when she died? Where were you?” He fell to the ground and wrapped his arms around the man, crying into the cloth of his pants. His voice sank to a grieved whisper. “Why could you not have saved me that one pain? Do you know the pain of that?”
        The old man inhaled sharply, his voice affected by unsteady breath. “I know my friend, I know.” He looked down, hesitating. “You must tell me. Will you travel with me? Will you drink more?” 
        Jean-Charles breathed deeply, recovered himself, and sat once again in the chair. He buried his face in his hand in thought. The occasional wave of grief threatened to bring a renewal of tears. At last, he looked to the man, who remained standing, and then to the winds outside. “No more,” he said quietly. “No more.”
        The old man nodded, and walked to the door. He turned. “Forgive me, Jean-Charles.” Then he left.






































        Jean-Charles laughed, “You know well that war is not so easily evaded. I lived in Barcelona after that. I met a girl. We moved here. Well not here, but America, New York, actually. Napoleon’s success at times tempted me to return, but I always remembered. I always remembered. The girl and I, we lived together some time. Consumption took her, a symptom of the crowded city, I suspect, back in ‘28. I moved here after that. I’ve been alone since then.”
        The old man leaned over and patted Jean-Charles on the shoulder, “There, my boy, it is alright. I thought you’d have traveled the world but I see you’d rather travel through books.” He gestured toward the full shelves. He paused, words hesitating on his lips. He leaned back into his chair and continued. “I’ve not come for a casual visit. You know a world now that others can never understand. But look, look here in this mirror.” He pulled out a small glass from his pocket. “You see, look at your eyes.”
        Jean-Charles looked at his eyes. “I don’t understand. What do you want me to see?”
“You’ve got wrinkles.” The man whispered.
        Jean-Charles stared blankly, and then his eyes widened in realization. “You don’t mean?”
“Yes, yes, and I’ll tell you why. I only gave you a drop. It’ll give you about 150 years.”
        
        






























        Jean-Charles sat reading in an armchair with a cup of tea. He looked up to see a poorly shodden man standing in the doorway of his library. He set his tea down and stared mouth agape. Gradually, his surprise settled into recognition and resignation. “So you have come.” He said.
        The man walked in and sat down in the armchair beside Jean-Charles. “Yes, I have come. To see how you are doing.”
        “And that is all? My hair begins to turn grey, and I see wrinkles encroach my face. I have not lived so long as you deemed.”
        “I compromised.” The man said, and then sighed. “Is not 200 years long enough? You have tasted only the beginning, and your sorrows will only grow with time.”
        Jean-Charles looked away. His breathing shuddered. “I found a love, and I watched her grow old and die. I watched my children and grand children die from old age. Everyone I knew from my days of youth are gone. I am all alone.”
        “Yes?” The man encouraged him.
“I thought I drowned once, but found that I would not, and suffered agonizing pain till I walked to the shore from the murky depths of the ocean. I have foregone food for years, hoping to die, but only wasted away and continued on, a mere skeleton of being. I have been stabbed, shot, burned, and decapitated, but nothing would pass me on. I had never known what a curse it was to go on living.”
        “It must not have all been bad.” The man placed his hand on Jean-Charles shoulder.
“Everything I have ever known has passed away before my eyes. I have seen man achieve flight and technology, and terrible tools of war, and fast has the world become incomprehensible to me.” Jean-Charles shook. “I can love never again, having seen all I did die! I am cursed to be alone the remainder of my days, wandering this earth a stranger in my own land! And what use? What use was it all? What have I gained? I have only lost.” He buried his face in his hands and sank from his chair to the floor in tears.
The man stood up. “I compromised, as I saw this. I underwent the same. I gave you but a drop of the elixir. It will soon run its course.” Jean-Charles clung to the man’s dirty leg, crying softly into it. “But I drank a whole cup. And now I must ask you, you can have more, and you can travel with me, and we will be strangers together. So tell me.” The man reached into his pocket and pulled out a vial of brilliant, golden liquid. “Do you want more?”
Jean-Charles sank to the man’s feet, shaking, trembling, struggling to speak through his grief. “No more.” He said. “No more.”

Notes:


Hundred Years War: Last two of the three wars were the Caroline War and the Lancastrian War


Franco Dutch War: Siege of Maastricht 1673


War of the Austrian Succession: antecedent of French and Indian War/Seven Years War.


The Peace referred to is the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle of 1748


“As stupid as the peace.” Was a French saying referring to the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle


Tavern scene takes place in 1756, during the first full year of French and Indian War/Seven Years War.


French and Indian War/Seven Years War was referred to as a  world war at the time.
