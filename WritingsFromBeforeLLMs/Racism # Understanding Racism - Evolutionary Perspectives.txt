
Understanding Racism: It’s only natural.


        In the making of the International African American Museum, everyone involved has some idea of what they want it to achieve. For some, the question revolves around identity and a reclamation of history and origin. For others, the focus centers upon worth, a statement of powerful, metaphysical presence. Whatever the aims, the International African American Museum will impress upon all who see it a firm acknowledgment: here are a people with history, culture, pain, joy, hope, and most of all, humanity. All human. That may seem so obvious it hardly bears mentioning; yet, in reality, humanity falls to the side for so many in their consideration. Any type of bigotry or hate will sacrifice the perception of humanity in others, but most relevant to the IAAM is the form of dehumanization known as racism.
        What is racism? Another well-known word of obvious meaning? Hating people because they’re different? Maybe so. But racism is poorly understood. It is not, as many think, a blind hatred. It is not, however you might argue it, something to be fought and abolished through force. Those who are racist ought not to be shut away or hated. Or punched. Racism is just one manifestation of a universal human tendency. The current attitude toward racism, the attempt to drive it away through antagonism and volume, is no different than a doctor treating the symptoms rather than the cause. The intention is good but the disease persists, even if the symptoms fade. To overcome racism, it must be accepted. To heal the racists, they must be accepted. We all share the trait, to some degree, of this universal human tendency, and those who return hate for hate and meet racism with antagonism succumb to the same vice. Still, in all this, the racists are justified, as are those who hate the racists. Justified, yes, but only through what they know through the formation of their perception. All hate for others resides in this justified perception. The typical American racist hates a black man not for the fact that he is black, but on account of the racist’s perception of blackness.
        Hatred against races, and hatred of racists as well, arose in our evolution. Every single human can hate, and we all hate. Racists just happen to hate according to a particular racial group. However, racists are no different in their humanity than any of those who hate according to a different group, such as political party, culture, gender, age. And all are justified, but only in what they know. What they know, literally in their DNA, is the divisive schema of us and them. People external to the individual fall within the us group or within the them group, and the groups remain malleable. Racism, like all forms of hatred, stems from this separation of us and them.
        The distinction of us and them had a significant evolutionary advantage in our path as a species. When humans lived in a more sparsely populated world, sorting humans as friend for those familiar and enemy as those who were unfamiliar served a critical purpose in the conservation and protection of resources. Scarcely ten thousand years ago, a blink in evolutionary time, humans lived in close-knit communities with little exposure to other peoples. When encountering a stranger at that time in evolutionary history, natural selection found it fit to develop suspicion and distrust in the individual encountering the stranger. The individual naturally enters a defensive state against the unknown. In the evolutionary timeline, those who tended to hesitate or doubt rather than be on guard and interpret the unknown as a threat were gradually bred out of the population on account of strangers seeking resources and willing to kill to obtain them. That scenario was then, and it served communities well, but the world has changed more quickly than evolution can compensate.
        During the great breadth of our evolutionary development, individuals crafted their perceptions entirely from direct experience. They learned from their immediate environment, were able to categorize and pass down through generations the knowledge of threats based on proximity, familiarity, and experience. As such, short of oral tradition (the evolutionary tendency of differentiating us and them far predates language if we followed the other great ape species, who exhibit the same tribal behaviors without a “developed” language), the individual perception was limited, small, and was suited and advantageous to the environment; generally, since perception was formed so heavily from experience, the individual had a great degree of perceptual truth relative to their situation and surroundings.
        Humans developed a warring tendency that made use of the differentiation of us and them early in the evolutionary timeline. While it served a purpose then to obtain and protect resources (which allowed greater reproduction), the situation of the modern world differs beyond measure, with resources in abundance and no need for tribes to go to war over painstakingly obtained necessities like water or herds. Unfortunately, DNA changes slowly and humanity retains the tribal tendencies of its recent evolutionary past. As a matter of habit and genes, the typical human unconsciously sorts everyone into an us group, which are those the individual perceives as allied to the self and/or the group(s) with which the individual identifies, and a them group, which are those of known threat and unknown threat, with “unknown” always leaning toward “threat” as a matter of evolutionary course.
        Ten thousand years does not nearly suffice to change the evolutionary form of tribal communities and perception based on direct experience, to a new form suited for large cities, arbitrary laws, and abundant media of information both out of place and out of time relative to an individual’s immediate physical surroundings and awareness. In large cities, when ten thousand years ago, the individual would seldom encounter strangers, the individual encounters a mass of strangers every day; unable to properly, accurately categorize based on direct experience the character of the strangers met by the individual, the individual compensates by making broader assumptions based on the entirety of knowledge gained to that point. The assumptive habit comes directly from humanity’s evolutionary biology, but the knowledge gained to that point comes from other individuals through the form of word-of-mouth, written word, graphics, and other forms of communication. While at the core, the individual still crafts perception on experience, in the modern world a great deal of an individual’s experience comes in the form of others’ opinions and thoughts, whether in a three hundred year old book, like Hobbes’ Leviathan, which still bears marked influence on the perceptions of the West as whole, or from the news station on the television. Every image, word, and tone encountered by the individual affects that individuals’ perception unconsciously in ways and magnitude the individual can neither control nor comprehend. At most, the individual can choose to consciously attach a truth value to the information presented. Typically, much of the modern person’s perception has been crafted unconsciously from the totality of all that the individual has experienced up to that point, and all the experiences fit into a weaved network of reality unique to that individual.
        In this way, regardless of how close it approaches actual reality, every individual’s perception is entirely legitimate and justified. The individual’s worldview is reality to them; it is the way the world is. To the individual, their own perception and opinions have the greatest truth value and highest degree of compatibility with daily experience.
        The goal for each individual, the objective at which the perception aims, is survival and reproduction. So, connecting the concepts, the individual believes on account of their evolutionary biology that their own perception is the interpretation of the world most suited to survival and reproduction. The major portion, if not the entirety, of the formation of the perception held as truth by an individual occurs at a subconscious, instinctual level. In other words, individuals do not really know how they came to be as they are. Without a firm technical foundation, the truth value of an individual’s perception (and identity) refers itself largely to emotion. These emotions rarely have an accurate, consciously identifiable backing and they do not easily change. An individual’s perception requires sufficient justification to change; such justification in this case would offer something of greater value in terms of survival and reproduction–which the individual determines mostly through natural instinct, while unconsciously comparing with all previous experiences and current perception. The individual will naturally interpret any attempt to change their perception without proper justification as a threat and respond defiantly, while relying on an emotionally abstract justification to buffer their own perception. The justification is emotional and abstract because the individual unconsciously refers to the entirety of their experience, the voice of which primarily exhibits itself as an emotion or mixture of emotions. The individual interprets these unjustified attempts as threats because, to them, their current worldview holds greater value in terms of survival and reproduction than whatever value strange information newly presented offers in exchange. 
Again, the individual naturally and mostly unconsciously bases their perception on personal experience. This personal experience could have any number of influences, of varying accuracy regarding the greater world, but the individual cannot accurately determine the truth value of most communications and experiences due to a lack of relevant information. Importantly, humans have evolved with the tendency to interpret the unknown as threats and maintain awareness of previously perceived threats. For example, if an individual has a negative encounter, the mind automatically notes everything within the experience, from visuals to feelings, and stores them in the instinctual reservoir of the unconscious. While the individual can go on to have many positive experiences, negative experiences persist more readily within the instincts and the perception on account of negative experiences bearing greater critical importance to survival. A positive experience may or may not greatly aid or inhibit survival or reproduction, though it certainly has an effect, but a negative experience could quickly become dangerous. It takes months and years to have a child, but only a moment to die.
In short, the individual perception predisposes itself to the identification of threats, negative experiences (even if they are only a visual representation, like videos of rioting, or verbal communication, like a disparaging remark about someone or conveyance of a possible threat) persist over positive ones, and our modern society has arisen from changes too rapid to allow evolutionary process sufficient opportunity to adapt the species. From these conditions arise racism and every other form of collective hatred or antagonism.
Through individual experience, racists are formed from family and community environment, ubiquitous media, and confirmation bias with a proclivity toward the identification of threats. The potential for these conditions to exist serves true for all people at the core, but racism bears some of the greatest relevance today in our multicultural world. The individual unconsciously makes increasingly broad categorizations relative to the expanding awareness of the individual through indirect experience in the form of verbal communication and media. Racists happen to confer the majority of their threat perception to a particular race. In an evolutionary effort to increase their chance of survival and reproduction, they unconsciously add to their perception the justification of that threat perception by accepting those direct and indirect experiences that confirm the negative appraisal and discarding the neutral and positive occurrences. 
The perception of a threat also increases the tendency toward antagonism and violence, as attacking first confers an evolutionary advantage. This tendency toward antagonism and violence increases the occurrence of negative experiences and conflict, leading to an increased solidification of the original perception. Whether from news of Denmark Vesey’s attempted rebellion, footage of Ferguson riots, or violence-soaked gangsta rap—despite this media only showing a contextually-lacking, partial portion of reality, evolutionary biology nudges the individual’s perception toward a negative interpretation. To generalize and simplify, a racist would take such experiences as confirmation of African Americans as a threat, while someone without a previously negative perception of African Americans would be confronted with doubt, hesitation, or fear, and would need to compare the negative experiences with all other experiences. If, in this largely unconscious process, the individual finds greater value regarding survival in their original (positive) perception of African Americans, the negative experiences may still be considered but eventually given a lower truth value, amounting to little importance.
These bio-psychological processes happen in many contexts, of course, such as when the media emphasizes police killings of African Americans, effectively elevating tensions between African Americans and police everywhere. In any example, despite usually only involving a statistically small portion of whatever population or categorization of people, the individual often interprets such information in a much larger context perceptually. The lesser involvement and contact an individual has with whatever categorization of peoples, the greater perceptual weight limited information will have. For example, someone watching tv in their room receives information from the media, and not direct experience. Instead of experiencing whatever category of people in close proximity to the individual, the people with whom the individual would have most real world contact, the information the individual receives at the time represents a statistically small, probably distant portion of that same category of people. Due to the relative amount and intensity of experience and the proclivity toward negative interpretation, these selective experiences through media have a disproportionately strong impact on the individual’s perception.
In the evolutionary perspective, racism or any other form of collective hatred is a biologically justified mechanism designed to protect the individual from threats. Unfortunately, these broad categorizations with a proclivity toward negative interpretation do not often capture the subtle, complex, and more accurate reality. The typical American racist unconsciously perceives African Americans as a threat to their own survival. In our resource-rich modern society, the evolutionary tendency toward antagonism that originates and maintains racism no longer confers crucial evolutionary benefit. No longer necessary for survival, the biology and habits remain to cause grief and lead to a plethora of negative outcomes, like Jim Crow, or disproportionate incarceration rates of minority populations. As an example, common to the living perspective of racism is denying opportunity, which, in the historical and evolutionary context would confer greater reproductive chance for racist’s them, while conversely lessening the reproductive chance for the racist’s us. 
Though our evolutionary past justifies this behavior, the perception from which it arises now causes greater harm and lesser benefit. In modern society, the arbitrary categorization of us and them based on appearance no longer bears great impact on an individual’s chance of survival and reproduction, as it may well have done thousands of years ago when tribes warred often and portrayed tribal affiliation through physical appearance or costume. The factors which determine perception and categorization are complex, certainly, but the impact of media in all its forms and an individual’s culture’s oral traditions (conveyance of perception through words) tower over the impact of direct, interpersonal experience, concurrent with mundane life. In modern society, the individual unaware of the evolutionary history and subsequent biological tendencies of humankind, of which they are a part, are especially vulnerable to unconsciously developing and maintaining an us/them perception and subsequent hatred of them. As a reminder, the hatred comes from an evolutionary trait tending toward negative interpretations as a safety mechanism, sometimes involving instigations of antagonism and violence. Racists ascribe to racial categorizations of us and them whereas non-racists ascribe to near infinite categorizations, inspired by acceptance. The racist’s dichotomic categorizations serve to dehumanize them and allow greater focus on us. The us requires greater focus from an evolutionary perspective since reproduction takes such a great deal of prolonged effort compared to the lesser mental capacity needed to more directly deal with them through violence or other mechanisms.
To explain more simply what has necessarily been highly technical up until this point: humans generally have an us and them categorization schema, a schema that arose in an evolutionary situation wherein it aided survival and reproduction but now no longer confers the same benefit; the schema has become arbitrary and impractical in modern society owing to the disproportional influence of indirect experience over individual perception, the rate at which strangers are encountered, which leads to broader categorizations, categorizations which bear decreasing relevance to interpersonal reality proportional to increased generalization, i.e. stereotypes, and the schema has become dangerous and harmful on account of its proclivity toward negative interpretation and instigation of antagonism as a defensive mechanism. The presence and effects of this schema are universal among humans. In other words, everyone is a type of ‘racist’, varying in degree, insofar as they divide life into us and them. 
Today, racism is unique, however, in its strong perceptual permanence, due largely to the proliferation and reproducibility of media and that media’s disproportionate impact on the individual’s perception. In the United States, subtle semantic cues, omissions, half-truths, and inaccurate portrayals in educational material and popular media convey the racism of those who created it. The national attention on racism has also obfuscated the issue in such a way that the fundamental us/them schema disappears, replaced by an inescapable societal view of racism based not on hateful intention but on ignorance. Everyone bears a measure of ignorance, and while the concept of ignorance has a great overall effect on perception, intention claims the most crucial importance in regards to ending racism. 
Others do not often accurately ascertain an individual’s intention in an act. Again, there is a proclivity toward negativity in the interpretation of intention, as one’s intention is part of the overall meaning and experience to another. If an individual has good intention and others interpret the act as offensive, the individual is met with doubt, shame, uncertainty, or any manner of negative emotions, ultimately adding a negative experience to the reservoir of unconscious perception; this does not directly aid intergroup harmony or the deconstruction of the us/them schema, especially if negative interpretations bolster the sense of a divide. Alternatively, if the individual has poor intention and the act is greeted with hostility by others, the individual unconsciously interprets that as justification of the original perception; as the original perception was negative, and the individual was greeted with negativity, the perception is once again justified in that event. If others respond with positivity to an act committed by an individual, if they respond with love, good humor, and acceptance of the individual as a human, regardless of how the individual’s act appears on the surface, the individual will have a positive experience; if the individual had good intentions, the experience will further bolster intergroup harmony and the dissolution of the perception of separation and antagonism; if the individual has poor intentions and receives positivity instead of antagonism, the original motivation for his poor-intentioned act will face challenges to its justification, and that individual will store the experience in the reservoir of unconscious perception. If responded to negatively, regardless of act or intention, negativity increases. If responded to positively, positivity increases, lessening conflict and pain.
The human mind, evolution, and biology carry incredibly complex systems, history, and processes. What people believe and perceive, they believe and perceive for a justified reason. The justification does not need to be, and rarely is, based on objective reality or facts and is especially never based on other individuals’ justifications; these justifications are ultimately abstract and emotional in practice, being born from a vast magnitude of experiences and information stored and networked through the mind and body. Justifications cannot be argued. They cannot be punched. They are individuals’ realities, uniquely true and justified. 
Due to evolutionary adaptations, humans have a tendency toward negatively interpreting events of unknown threat, a tendency which once aided human survival but no longer serves the same purpose. Our blossomed, populous society has forced humans to cope with environments for which natural selection has not sufficiently adapted us. Surrounded by strangers, people must reinterpret their relationship with those around them, as well as the possible threats they pose. Bowing to the habits of biology, humans unconsciously make increasingly broad categorizations in order to cope with a vast, scattered perception brought about by an overflowing deluge of information. Individuals living only a few thousand years prior to modern times crafted their worldview predominantly from their immediate environment, and even only two hundred years ago individuals still predominantly relied on their surroundings, barring a few pigeons and hand-delivered letters here and there. But due to this same evolutionary context, a context rapidly changed through these past two hundred years, the limited but incessant information a modern individual receives from the torrent of information has a heavier impact on the individual’s perception than it merits in reality. Compound such effects with a desire, born from self-preservation, to spread knowledge to us of perceived threats from them, and such a pathology as modern racism finds fertile ground.
Hatred, in all forms, whether of races or racists or commies or nazis, only perpetuates violence, conflict, and negativity. All hatred is justified on account of the individual perception and the drive to survive and reproduce. However, for truth and benefit, no hatred is justified. The divisive schema of us and them gives birth to and is birthed from this drive to survive and reproduce. All humans want those same things. Individuals hate them as a defensive mechanism designed to protect us. This division of us and them is an illusion born on a perception of competition. In simple metaphor, two men with plenty to eat can convince themselves they must kill the other to survive. In spirit, all, if not merely enough, succumb to this tendency to cause great societal harm. On this world, there is enough. Everyone at their core wants only to live. Competition, conflict, and negativity are illusions in the sense that they no longer benefit humanity. The final resolution of hatred, of racism, is found through understanding the self and evolutionary biology and the realization of the individual that there is enough for the self without need for violent competition. 
But this is all theory. In practice, the end to racism comes not through any form of violence, antagonism, or conflict, as such behavior only perpetuates division, but rather through love, compassion, and forbearance. We must deny our natural proclivity toward negative interpretations. The human tendency desires swift, hard justice or retribution and revenge, but such tendency also arises from a drive to preserve the self and survive. Such tendency would once have served to eliminate threats, but now serves only to perpetuate conflict and division. Every person has a right to defend themselves, and they should; otherwise, the way to peace is through peace. The IAAM will serve well to combat the informational misconceptions of America’s racism, breaking down the overly-broad homogenization of African Americans through intimate experiences with art and history, but in order to achieve the greatest benefit, it must welcome all, and all involved must act with love.
