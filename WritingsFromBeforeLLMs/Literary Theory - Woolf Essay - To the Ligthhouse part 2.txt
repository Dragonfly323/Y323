The words of those critics who reviewed Woolf’s To the Lighthouse demonstrate the spark she attempted to light. In the modern and postmodern movements, artists and writers moved into the abstract, all that which defied any evaluation by contemporary logic. As such, those who interpreted and found meaning in Woolf’s work had to do so without the linear thinking that western society had so heavily relied upon up to that point. To the Lighthouse in particular did not align with traditional paradigms of literary expression. To the Lighthouse could not easily be understood in terms of character development, story progression, or conflict and resolution. Still, something was occurring in To the Lighthouse, and though critics were largely unsure of exactly what was going on in the novel, they nonetheless felt that a communication had occurred, an indirect communication of feelings and emotions that could not quite be pointed to in any particular movement or phrase, feelings and emotions which many would rarely have experienced otherwise. In accomplishing this feat, Virginia Woolf overcomes the limitations of her society and, while not single-handedly demolishing the limitations of Western thinking entirely, served as one of the brave pioneers in deconstructing the framework of a religiously skeptical society.
        One critic, Rachel A. Taylor, writing a review for Spectator in 1927, beautifully demonstrates the crux of the crisis. She writes of Mrs. Ramsay: “She bewitches you. She is sorrowful for something lost out of Time; something that, found, would illuminate eternity” (Majumdar). These words capture the essence of Woolf’s impact on society, for through her words she offered a glimpse of common humanity, that we all long for that something, we know not what. Common practice in Western society is to simply ignore any moments of longing for the eternal, that place out of time that all conscious beings seem to know. Woolf’s writings did not awaken the West, and ought not to be expected to perform such a conquest as reversing centuries of illusory thinking, but they did offer cause to, even for a moment, focus one’s attention on something real, yet ephemeral, something felt in the essence of being, but unknown in words beyond that. That small impact alone, a drop in the ocean, but what is the ocean if not a collection of drops? While the West shunned and feared the unknown, the abyss of totality that Woolf experienced in her bouts of “madness,” she crafted a window into the sense of beauty and being that the unknown offers. She could never speak aloud of her thoughts and feelings plainly, being a mystically-inclined woman in a rationalistic, male-dominated society, but she could transfer those feelings through the quiet examination of common moments, to express the feeling of being eternally present in a chaotic, time-paced world.
        This forcing of thought upon the nameless is not all that Woolf accomplishes in her novel; she draws attention to the eternal present. She attacks the falsities and illusions of the modern world. Some critics interpret the characters in To the Lighthouse as having little substance, but they fail to realize that this is exactly Virginia Woolf’s intention. As Louis Kronenberger says in a 1925 review, “Those who reject To the Lighthouse as inferior to Mrs. Dalloway because it offers no one with half the memorable lucidity of Clarissa Dalloway must fail to perceive its larger and, artistically, more difficult aims” (Kronenberger). Woolf intends to demonstrate through her characters, especially Mr. Ramsay, the microcosms of different aspects of society. Mr. Ramsay encapsulates the staunch intellectual who searches for meaning and truth in every place but the present, always striving for the next goal in an endless pursuit of… he knows not what, perhaps the letter z? Though, he cannot reach it. 
Kronenberger writes that Woolf uses a method of characterization “not based on observation or personal experience, but purely synthetic, purely creational.” He writes further that the characters, with the exception of Mrs. Ramsay, “are not fully alive because they are not whole enough. Most of them are one-dimensional fragments that have been created with great insight but insufficient vitality. They have minds, moods, emotions–but they get all three through creative intellect.” While appreciating the work as a whole, Louis Kronenberger fails to realize, especially in saying that Virginia Woolf has no gift for passion–“her people never invade the field of elementary emotions: they are hardly animal at all,”–that Woolf views society in this way, synthetic and creational, never invading the field of elementary emotions, of real being. The one-dimensionality of these characters are Woolf’s own critiques of society’s lack of spirit and life. 
        To Woolf, who could find no one who understood her experiences of “moments of being,” the society in which she lived lacked something crucially fundamental. While she could never capture the crux of her experience, her piercing intellect crafted a thousand critiques of those around her, critiques demonstrating all the habits and perspectives that kept them from sharing in the beauty that she experienced. This perspective of Virginia Woolf’s clearly demonstrates itself in the empty intellectualism of Mr. Ramsay, the brash egotism of Charles Tansley, who was not satisfied “until he had turned the whole thing round and made it somehow reflect himself and disparage them” (Lighthouse 16), and the opium using Mr. Carmichael. These three characters in particular embody the tendencies of Woolf’s contemporary society to over-intellectualize life instead of experiencing it, to become so self-centered and dependent on others’ opinions as to lose sight of everything else, and to escape into a thousand different fantasies, illusions, or hazes. 
Virginia Woolf writes from experience. In the Jungian perspective, all of her characters are manifestations either of her own unconscious, which would be linked to the collective unconscious and thus the foundation of human nature, or of the archetypes in her environment. Compelled to write this novel, she searches for the meaning of these patterns in the people around her, how they make them different from her, and whether there is any hope that they can change, whether there is any hope she can at least understand the difference. She did not write this novel for others, but for herself, and doubtless she could not have considered it more than a message in a bottle for anyone else who was capable of understanding to find. Bottles can float for a long time, in this case outliving the sender, and whether in the field of physics or art or spirituality, Virginia Woolf’s perspective is now finding a common kindred spirit; we can only hope she knows.
Maybe, however, it was only a matter of communication, the barriers of time and culture and language. Maybe Virginia Woolf was not so alone after all. Would she have had the same inclinations to mysticism and spells of madness that she called “moments of being” in any life and environment? Was Virginia Woolf wrong? If these feelings did not arise spontaneously, if Tao did not arise suddenly without warning within individuals, how could society ever change? Many critics felt that Virginia Woolf was saying something in her writing, especially in To the Lighthouse, and of that novel they give the greatest praise to the second part, “Time Passes”, which most perfectly captures Woolf’s perspective of time and the unspeakable beauty of decay and small moments that usually pass unnoticed. One critic, Arnold Bennet, writing a review in June 1927 for Evening Standard, disagrees, entirely missing Woolf’s intention when he says, “the middle part, entitled ‘Time Passes’, shows a novel device to give the reader the impression of the passing of time. In my opinion it does not succeed” (Bennet). Admittedly, Woolf’s intention in “Time Passes” does not easily fit into words. However, Conrad Aiken, writing in July 1927 for Dial, comes closer to her intention when he writes, “We live in that delicious house with them. Nothing happens, in this houseful of odd nice people, and yet all of life happens. The tragic futility, the absurdity, the pathetic beauty, of life–we experience all of this in our sharing of seven hours of Mrs. Ramsay’s wasted or not wasted existence. We have seen through her, the world” (Aiken). 
Aiken, at least, understands the question, the same question Woolf herself cannot quite put into words. It is not a question alone–were it one it might be phrased “what’s the point?” No, Woolf asks a question that holds the semblance of an answer in itself. By describing these scenes of no importance, but describing them beautifully, she challenges the reader to feel something beautiful and desirable in an otherwise objectively pointless story. She writes life unfiltered with all the boundaries of ego and time while overcoming those same boundaries, boundaries that prevent us from discovering the beauty in our own lives. She calls into conscious perception the flaws in others such as Mr. Ramsay, Charles Tansley, and Mr. Carmichael, that we might find in ourselves, and the same aspirations of beauty in Mrs. Ramsay and Lily Briscoe that we might discover if only we searched for them. Most importantly, she stresses the importance of feeling over thought. To the Lighthouse is really Mrs. Woolf’s assault upon the rigid, quantitative, secular thinking of her time. It proves through its foundation of realism and examination of individual thought and consciousness that the unspeakable and the unknowable are not only real, but far more important in life than the philosophical theories, discourses or examinations of what we can see and feel that dominates Western society. To Virginia Woolf, the act of feeling itself carries the utmost importance. Woolf’s argument is that the world, life, and every single moment of being contains unfathomable mystery and that even without solving the mystery, one can feel the love and beauty of it.
        Sadly, her message went largely unheard. “Dear Mrs. Woolf,” begins J. E. Blanche in August of 1927 for Les Nouvelles Litteraires, “do you wish to create an atmosphere? Is there a hidden meaning there? You assume that your readers are as intelligent as you and as accustomed to seeing into the obscurity and resolving mysteries” (Blanche). Virginia Woolf does not expect to be understood, though she may desire such, for she understands that some mysteries, especially the greatest mysteries, cannot be conveyed, only experienced. Mrs. Ramsay found the greatest joy and truth in feeling understood by her husband that she loved him without words, “For she had triumphed again. She had not said it: yet he knew” (Lighthouse 184). This bears particular importance when considering Mr. Ramsay’s character and need for verbal validation. To Woolf, it represents the victory of emotion over intellect or the limited expression of the surface that might be found in words meant to represent feelings. To feel directly, from one to another, carries vastly more importance and meaning than to say anything. To Woolf, the most important things do not need to be said; rather, they are understood directly. This holds true for To the Lighthouse as well. It says nothing directly of the truth and meaning that Woolf intends to capture and convey, and finds its greatest impact in what is understood beyond the words. 
This idea of communication without communication marks in part the postmodern movement to which Woolf belonged, and perhaps primed some select few with a greater capability of understanding her intention. Jean-Jacques Mayoux, writing a review in June 1928 for Revue Anglo-Americaine, understands the rarity of those who can know the heights of Woolf’s expression. “It is unfortunately only to the happy few that Virginia Woolf has just given a definitive work which contains all her vision of the world, in which all the delicate beauty of her art is to be found” (Mayoux). Here, at last, amongst all those critiques and lay readers is one who can mirror Woolf’s mind. He correctly intuits the essence of her novel, “To the Lighthouse is a long contemplation, a harmonious unwinding of images and emotions, of sentiments and thoughts in an interior world… Virginia Woolf makes us feel the parallel permanence of the outer and inner worlds.” Mayoux so accurately perceives Woolf’s intent and execution that he merits an outstanding remark. 
The aspect of the novel so few others understood, the meaning and importance of the characters, he describes without a second breath, “Woolf’s characters live an astonishingly normal life for themselves, and not for our benefit, and do not even have hidden turpitudes to reveal to us; they do not liberate enough of themselves in order to embrace things entirely.” Woolf intends this entirely, but who could know without already knowing? He also understands Woolf’s style in writing: “Everything takes place between brackets, with a sentence for each happening. This work which has no apparent articulations, we have found to be totally coherent, a combination of equilibrium and subtle correspondences.” Woolf her whole life was plagued by a society and people around her who obsessed over the details, the scientific, the proof and action and cause, but here we find one who sees beyond the details towards the whole that Woolf intended.
