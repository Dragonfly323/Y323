The Norse “Wulf and Eadwacer” brings to heart the hopelessness of doomed longings. The poem details the disassociation of one girl’s wishes and desires from the perspective of her family, and the dark results of that disconnect. The unnamed narrator speaks of her emotions during the ordeal, and her own mixed state of mind. However, in the end, she leaves the poem with an unfinished air, simultaneously in resignation and defiance, knowing that her place by her husband’s side cannot change, but also that her passion lives beyond her own life. “Wulf and Eadwacer” recounts in succinct form how emotions can live despite enemies of society and circumstance, and how one’s heart can never be unwillfully owned.
The narrator mourns her powerlessness. The poem begins, “For my people it is as if someone might give them a game.” This line signifies the apathy her family has for her emotions and desires, delineating her to a pawn in a play, one whose own desires bear no significance to the outcome. The poem continues, “They will want to take him if he comes into their band, / it is not the same for us.” Her family wishes to capture her lover, and, less likely, adopt him as their own, or more likely, execute him or harm him in some way, but the narrator and her lover wish to be rid of everyone, that they might be alone and free from others, abandoning the politics of the families.
The next stanza describes in greater detail the difficult situation of the narrator and her lover. The first line states, “Wolf is on one isle, I am on another.” The two lovers are separated, whether physically, or politically, or both, and can be understood as living on two different worlds, in metaphor. The next line details more, “That island is secure, enclosed by marsh,” symbolizing to greater extent the inability of the narrator to be with her lover, describing the island as unreachable, secure. “There are blood-thirsty savage men there on that isle; / They will want to take him if he comes into their band, / it is not the same for us.” The last three lines of the stanza detail the nature of the narrator’s lover’s family, who with equal fervor wish to keep them separated. The repetition of the lines, “They will want to take him if he comes into their band, / it is not the same for us.” signifies the similar, or dissimilar interests of her family and her lover’s family. The shared antagonism toward the narrator’s lover by both families points to the likely interpretation that Wolf is either an outlaw, a dishonored member of the family, a younger brother, a servant, or someone of similar low social standing. Once again, “it is not the same for us.” symbolizes the lovers’ wish to be rid entirely of the families.
The next stanza takes place after a possible marriage, during a time of intense emotional turmoil. “I availed the wide-ranging expectations of my Wolf:” the narrator recounts. This line speaks of the narrator’s conflicted heart in seeking to keep her lover, Wolf, despite the recent events that try to drive them apart. “When it was rainy weather and I sat weeping,” she goes on to say, “Then the battle-swift one laid his boughs about me;”. These lines speak of the narrator’s sorrow in respect to Wolf, and her emotional overspill into sex with her husband. Of the sex, she says, “there was pleasure for me in this, / yet it was also loathsome.” These mixed emotions make it likely not only that she loves both men, but also that this poem is a personal account, and not fanfiction. However, most likely, her emotions of love for Wolf merely misdirected themselves towards the “battle-swift”, and she does not actually love him.
Further support for the claim that she loves only Wolf resides in the next stanza. The narrator mourns her absent lover, “Wolf, my Wolf, expectations of you / Have made me ill,” Her words bring to mind those times when one says they will be somewhere and do not show up, leaving the one who remains waiting with feelings of rejection and confusion. The narrator continues, “your rare visits, / A grieving heart, not at all the lack of food.” These lines tell the reader that the man she has married has wealth, and that she is well taken care of, but that she suffers from starvation of the heart, and pines away. The narrator grows more love sick with each passing day, and the focus on Wolf in the stanza signify that she has little feeling left for her husband. 
The final stanza seems to have skipped some period of time. The penultimate stanza recounts her awaiting her lover, Wolf, while this last stanza indicates that she has had a child with Wolf and has given the child to Wolf to raise. “Do you hear, Wealth-Watcher? A wolf is carrying / our cursed whelp to the wood.” She calls her husband “Wealth Watcher”, possibly meaning that she also comes from a rich family, which coincides with their rejection of Wolf as her lover, and that her husband married her more as a business arrangement than for any amount of love. Despite her marriages lack of love, however, she finds consolation in her affair with Wolf, by whom she has borne an illegitimate child. The lines have a mocking and sarcastic tone about them, embodying the narrator’s spite towards her cold and uncaring husband. Continuing to address her husband, she says, “That which was never composed is easily torn in two, / our passion together.” She completely rejects further advances made by her husband, denying that there ever existed any form of attraction between them.
“Wulf and Eadwacer” can easily, pleasurably, and accurately be read as the following: A woman from a wealthy family loves a younger brother or servant of another family. However, due to the servant status of her lover, or that an elder brother would have first choice, she must marry one whom she does not love. She implores her lover to remain with her, even if in secrecy, but it is not so easy for her lover to do so. Both families disapprove of Wolf, and he must carefully make his visits, which he can do only rarely, out of caution, to see her. She makes herself entirely available to Wolf, but lacks the power or opportunity to see him. So she waits for him. In her loneliest moments, she finds a bitter comfort in the embrace of her husband, though she sees their relations as ultimately repulsive. She is well provided for, which, as she mentions that aspect in her home, might imply that to be with Wolf would be to choose a much more difficult life. Despite the difficulties in their relationship, she manages to conceive by Wolf, and bears his child. In the end, she finds comfort that her love has borne fruit, and rejects her husband from then on entirely.
“Wulf and Eadwacer” has suffered innumerable interpretations, ranging from bestiality, literally wolves having sex, or as a companion poem to the Saga of the Volsungs. Not honoring the interpretations of bestiality or wolves having sex, the immediate counter to Wulf and Eadwacer as a companion poem to Saga of the Volsungs lies in lines 13-15 of the original Old English text. Translated to modern English, the lines read not that the narrator, often interpreted as Signy, visits Wolf, but rather that Wolf visits the narrator, which is inconsistent with the Saga of the Volsungs, where Signy visits Sigmund, and only once, in the guise of a sorceress. To further discount the fanfiction hypothesis, the mixture of emotions in lines 12a and 12b, coupled with the first person narration, indicate that the poem is a personal narrative, written from experience.
“Wulf and Eadwacer” reads as an early Romeo and Juliet style tale. It recounts a woman’s defiance at society and familial politics in pursuit of emotion. From a Romantic, or Transcendentalist perspective, the poem captures perfectly the unconquerable nature of the heart in the face of adversity. The poem ends in victory, with her love bearing fruit where her marriage does not, defying the expectation, and maintaining a strong heart and pride in independence despite oppressive circumstances.
