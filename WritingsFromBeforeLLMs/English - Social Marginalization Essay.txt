Social marginalization affects individuals even when removed from the oppressive society. Individuals oppressed under social marginalization, whether through physical abuse, denial of potential, or negative views, often begin to identify within the paradigm of their socially mandated place in society. Internalized oppression, this self-identifying, a sort of viewing of one’s self through the eyes through which one believes the surrounding society perceives them, often has an incredibly subtle nature and prolonged residual effects. Individuals suffering from internalized oppression also sometimes choose to remain oppressed, even with alternatives available. Such behavior signifies the fear that comes with rebelling against societal norms and represents the deep roots this subconscious habituation can spread.  Brave New World by Aldous Huxley, The God of Small Things, by Arundhati Roy, and Disgrace by J. M. Coetzee all offer telling examples of the phenomena of internalized oppression and allow the reader to better understand the great burden that comes with accepted, even to some degree desired, social marginalization.
        Brave New World shows starkly the extent to which individuals lack awareness of oppression and even desire the tools of their oppression. Linda, John Savage’s mother, a Beta in the World State of Brave New World lives for years away from the society that has systematically and genetically suppressed her individuality and ability to live freely. The repeated cultural clashes she experiences in the savage reserve while living among the natives, such as the difficulty she experiences as a result of her promiscuity, shows she lacks the ability to consider different ways of thinking; she lacks the ability to overcome her conditioned control. Even after living in the native culture long enough to raise a child nearly to adulthood, she remains enslaved to the societal standards of the World State. When seeing Bernard and Leena, Linda cries “If you knew how glad–after all these years! A civilized face. Yes, and civilized clothes. Because I thought I should never see a piece of real acetate silk again.”(Huxley 119) What constitutes a pleasurable life has been so ingrained and conditioned within Linda’s perception that she has, after all these years, been unable to change her perspective on the world and find an acceptable place, in her view, in the different way of life among the natives. 
While Brave New World constitutes an extreme example of conditioning, starting in the development of the fetus, the novel nonetheless mirrors the ability for conditioning to ingrain itself within the populace and within the individual. The Work Group for Community Health and Development at the University of Kansas writes that “[Those suffering from internalized oppression] begin to feel that the stereotypes and misinformation that society communicates are true and they act as if they were true.”[1] John Savage experiences this notion of conditioning having truth when he finds himself combatting a rising tide in challenging the lack of free, or noble, thought within the society of the World State. When John tries to free the Deltas from the enslavement of soma, the Deltas react accordingly: “A great shout suddenly went up from the mob; a wave of movement drove it menacingly towards the Savage.”(213) Though the Deltas embody an extreme example of conditioning, Lucy, in Disgrace, exhibits the same behavior when she fights David about staying at the farm, even though she has to live “like a dog”(Coetzee 205). This lack of an ability to alter one’s perceptions in the face of oppression, the result of heavy conditioning, or even aligning one’s perceptions with the oppressor, the result of societal or environmental pressure, renders the individual incapable of finding security, in some sense of the word, in any other paradigm aside from the one into which the individual has been conditioned or pressed into accepting.
        The God of the Small Things shows the tragedy of individuals suffering from internalized oppression reinforcing the oppression of their own group. According to a postcolonial analysis of God of the Small Things, “the identity of the colonized is marginalized, and the natives are alienated to their reality” and, as a result, “the internalization of inferiority occurs and the servitude creeps in.”[2] While the caste system existed long before the British colonization of India, colonization can, in a sense, occur within the native populace, exactly as occurred with the formation of the caste system. Vellya Paapen, who lived during the time of street-sweeping, when the untouchables could not allow touchables to step in their footprints, embodies the extreme to which individuals, as an instinct of survival, will reinforce the oppression of their own group. Vellya Paapen firmly believes that untouchables must keep their place and obey the societal laws. Vellya subconsciously does this on account of an instinct of survival. He subconsciously views it as in the best interest of the group of untouchables as a whole to not upset the societal order. Just as the monkeys in the ladder experiment would, for reasons unknown to them, beat monkeys who climbed the ladder[3], so too does Vellya Paapen suffer from information cascade, the reinforcement of environmental behaviors, when he sits “for hours at the door of their hut, still drunk, his glass eye and the edge of his ax glittering in the lamplight, waiting for Velutha to return.”(Roy 268) Vellya Paapen does not once think of any alternative to the situation, and thinks only that he must right the wrong that his son has committed by killing him. Tragically, Vellya Paapen bases these feelings of right and wrong on the perceptions of an oppressive society that have bled into his subconscious through years of conditioning and environmental reinforcement.
        Disgrace constitutes a remarkable novel by expounding upon the negative effects of oppression not only upon the oppressed, but also upon the oppressors. A writer by the name of Narasimha Rao Kedari says well that, “Lurie is broadly representative of an older social order and the officially defunct South Afrikaner dominance, statutory racial oppression and the uneasy pleasures of the white privilege.”[3] In a society overcoming the systemized oppression of Apartheid, the lingering perception of white superiority and black submissiveness permeates the culture. David Lurie has little to no ability to see from the eyes of those that “enriched” him (Coetzee 192) on account of his privileged position as a white male in a society dominated by white males. He takes for granted the submissive nature of others and expects his desires to be fulfilled. He seems vaguely aware of the imbalance of power, such as during one particular encounter with Melanie when he thinks, “Not rape, not quite that, but undesired nevertheless, undesired to the core.”(Coetzee 25) but makes no attempt to correct it, and rarely, if ever, considers non sexual dynamics within the relationships he has; after he makes love to Bev Shaw, he thinks, “This is what I will have to get used to, this and even less than this.”(Coetzee 150) Because David Laurie operates from a position of dominance, and receiving, he lacks the ability to form a stable relationship based on mutual respect, equality, and companionship. David Laurie, as the result of his privileged position, never learns how to love a woman in a way that benefits either of them. Though he may think it benefits him, or enriches him, in truth, such unstable relationships only serve to bolster David’s own self-centeredness, keep him confined in oppressive social paradigms, and excludes him from experiencing the more beautiful depths of human nature.
        Conditioning within both the oppressed and the oppressors of a society prolongs an unequal and detrimental state of affairs. From the perspective of those oppressed, such as the majority of the inhabitants of the World State, Vellya Paapen, and Melanie Isaacs, adherence to social norm reinforces itself as means of a subconscious instinct to survive and/or avoid potential pain. From the perspective of the oppressors, the instincts of oppression remain much the same, those of survival and avoidance of pain. Those in societal positions of power or dominance, such as Mustafa Mond or David Lurie, reinforce the societal standards of population stratification into the oppressed system structures out of an instinct of self-preservation and stability. From Mustafa Mond’s perspective, the oppression of individuality poses the greatest chance of preserving the life of the populace, a small price to pay in his mind. From David Lurie’s perspective, the position of social dominance as a white male allows for the greatest safety on his part and for the propagation of his seed, his biological instinct, with greater ease and therefore facilitates his own inherent instinct of self-preservation in regards to his genes. Any society that oppresses any people oppresses not only the submissive but the dominant in that it undermines the potential for equality to arise in that society. The internalized oppression, the subconscious continuation of learned behaviors on both the part of the oppressed and the oppressors, severely limits the ability for relationships based on equality to grow, thus limiting the ability for talents, intelligence, and skill, such as Velutha’s carpentry skill, to freely operate within that society. This inability to realize the greatest potential of every individual systematically debilitates the growth of the society as a whole and the good of every member of that society, regardless of position, income, race, or religion. Brave New World, The God of the Small Things, and Disgrace all show the negative effects of oppressive structures and an inherent malnourishment of liberty and growth that affects every single member of that society.
