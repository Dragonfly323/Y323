
The Beauty of Pantheism
How an Old Philosophy Never Aged




        The perfect balance of the universe and the existence of life poses a difficult question to the human race. Religion can easily answer this question by appealing to some form of authority, or perhaps some poor form of logic, and operates under the pretense that that authority can never be completely understood, deeming the human mind, upon no basis whatsoever, inadequate to comprehend the universe. The answer provided by religion does not satisfy the avid seeker of truth. Avid seekers of truth such as Jesus, Meister Eckhart, Benedictus de Spinoza, Erasmus Darwin, and Percy Bysshe Shelley, for the sake of truth, have historically denied appeals to concrete authority and accepted truths if they do not adequately answer this question. The question is the state of man, and whether man and the material universe are separated from the creator god, the cause of the universe. While religion traditionally says that man is separated from God, and that man must follow certain set rules in order to reconcile with God, pantheism supposes that the universe is the creator, and that all in existence is part of God, and cannot be separated from God. Pantheism does not allow for blind obedience, and requires reason dictate the best action. Pantheism as a philosophy supports universal education, universal freedom, and universal harmony for all creatures of the world, and serves to empowers individuals to rule themselves. Pantheism achieves this all through perceiving the universe as one infinite and singular whole. 
        The unity, or singular nature of the Universe, has manifested itself through scientific means in present times and spiritually in many diverse traditions from around the world. Laozi, the founder of Taoism, speaks of an eternal reason, or tao, which one can behold effortlessly and the manifestations of which one may see if they wish to.[13] According to Jewish Kabbalah, the Universe came about by G-d’s tzimtzum, or contraction. In Jewish Kabbalah, G-d is an infinite being that, because He is infinite, cannot manifest anything outside of Himself, as to do so would place a limit on His infinite nature. So to create the Universe, He had to contract Himself and burst forth into creation.[5] Hinduism calls ultimate, singular reality “Brahman”, a world soul.[3] The Stoics used reason to determine that a single material composed everything in existence. The equating of the material world with an infinite divine arises in mystic, though often perceived as heretical, branches of Judaism, Islam, and Christianity. The shadow of Pantheism has never lingered far from the core foundations of religion and spirituality.
        The Bible as well as additional texts support this dotting of history with the idea of the oneness of nature, and consequently, the oneness of man and the divinity of existence. In John 10:30 Jesus purportedly says “I and my Father are one.”[2] Tradition considers this relationship between Jesus and the Divine an exception, as, according to traditional interpretation, Jesus, through virgin birth, forewent the sin nature passed through the male seed. However, Biblical support for this doctrine is scarce, arising only in the two gospel infancy narratives and a prophecy in a chapter of Isaiah fulfilled in that same chapter of Isaiah a few verses later. In a contemporary cultural context, the doctrine of the virgin birth of Jesus likely results from the early Christian church’s belief system’s competition with the predominant Greco-Roman mythology, which features miraculous births like that of Perseus, whose mother, Danaë, was impregnated by Zeus, who transformed himself into golden rain and fell on her belly, as her father had locked her in a brass urn, and Zeus could not get in otherwise. The interpretation of Jesus as fundamentally different from the rest of humanity on account of a virgin birth beggars belief and serves only to distract from his core message, that of pantheism. Without the strained interpretation passed down by tradition, and man-made law, the most appropriate understanding of Jesus and his concept of the Father is a pantheistic one.
        Jesus explicitly includes his fellow man in his divine birthright, attempting to convey that the Son of God was a state of being. He says in John 14:20 “At that day ye shall know that I am in my Father, and ye in me, and I in you.”[2] While somewhat evident throughout the Gospel of John, the Gospel of Thomas, a coptic script not included in the New Testament canon, more explicitly portrays Jesus as speaking in an abstract first person from the point of view of the Tao, or, in Christian terminology, the Logos. Jesus purportedly says in verse 77 of the Gospel of Thomas, “I am the light that is over all things. I am all: from me all came forth, and to me all attained. Split a piece of wood; I am there. Lift up the stone, and you will find me there.”[1] Meister Eckhart, a christian mystic living in the 13th and 14th centuries, echoes these words, saying “Whoever possesses God in their being has Him in a divine manner, and He shines out to them in all things; for them all things taste of God and in all things it is God’s image that they see.”[8] In another part of his sermons, Meister Eckhart, on the threshold of heresy in denying the Church’s place as mediator between God and man and possibly denying the doctrine of separation on account of Original Sin says, “Nobody at any time is cut off from God.”[8] 
As time advanced through the centuries, the Church, both Catholic and Protestant, and Christianity as a whole, established securely within doctrine and set interpretation that the material world was evil, that this world is not home, and that we must deny our human nature on account of its imperfectness and subsequent evil. However, in a pantheistic perspective, the more mystical and more convalescent meaning of Jesus’ words appears with ease. Jesus does not call his followers to forsake the material world, but rather the enslaving herd mindset of the masses at large. In the parable of the lost sheep, the praise befalls the sheep who wanders from the herd, breaking free of social constraints and limited perspectives. The Jews of Jesus’s time accused him of heresy for equating himself with God. Ironically, were it not for the entirely unlikely virgin birth, modern Christians would do the same. In the past 2000 years, the charges of heresy have remained the same, for Jesus, Meister Eckhart, Benedictus Spinoza, Giordano Bruno and anyone who dare say similar today. The Father about which Jesus talks is not the Tetragrammaton of the Old Testament, but the Universe itself.
        If the Universe to so many spiritually influential figures, too innumerable to mention, is God, where lies the logical justification to say that the Universe and God are two names for the same entity? Benedictus Spinoza, born Baruch de Spinoza, proved definitively that not only is it logical for God and the Universe to be the same, it is impossible for them to not be, or rather, impossible that there exists anything without the infinite substance that he called God. While Spinoza’s logic escapes simplification, and any partial quote would hardly make any point, an attempted summarization follows: Spinoza reasoned that for anything to exist, it must have the attribute of existence, and that all things which exist must necessarily share that attribute. He further reasoned that as all things that exist share the attribute of existence, that existence must therefore be the primary cause of all things which share that attribute. Much in line with Kabbalistic mythology, because no substance besides God can be granted or conceived, as God is infinite and, by consequence, can have nothing exist outside of his infinite nature without contradiction, Spinoza also reasons that “Whatsoever is, is in God, and without God nothing can be, or be conceived.”[12] 
        Spinoza lived in the 17th century and used reason alone to determine the nature of the Universe. With the advent of the widespread use of the scientific method, however, the observation and prediction of recordable and consistent data provided a firm foundation for his logic. Though science has not completely simplified the nature of the Universe to the one singular fundamental substance that Spinoza predicted, the standard model of physics simplifies the Universe to condensed energy, or matter, and the four fundamental forces that operate on that matter. Simplifying only as far as protons, neutrons, and electrons allows for every combination of matter and every species of life humanity has ever encountered. This scientific truth of complexity arising from simplicity echoes in Spinoza’s ninth proposition: “The more reality or being a thing has, the greater the number of its attributes.”[12] Understanding Spinoza’s words “reality or being” as “quantity” perfectly coincides with the scientific discoveries of the modern day.
        A great defender and antagonist of religion lives under the name of Pain. On the one hand, in those religions that hold God as benevolent, the problem of pain causes many to wonder why a good God would let bad things happen. On the other hand, many religions use the problem of pain as a source of power, telling people that pain exists in the world on account of human insufficiency, and that for them to become sufficient, they must follow the tenets of the stated religion. However, to the pantheist, and to scientific knowledge, the problem of pain is no problem at all. Pain, from a scientific viewpoint, keeps us alive. The pantheist understands this as well, and accepts pain without suffering. The great flaw that many religions foster, that objective science and pantheism rejects, is the perception that the world is not perfect, or is incomplete in some way, largely on account of the phenomena of pain. The scientific viewpoint sees pain as an evolutionary adaptation that warns the organism of danger. The pantheist, in his understanding of the infinite nature of the Universe, sees pain as temporary, and of no great concern. The pantheist and the Christian both experience pain in their lives. While the pantheist tends to accept pain just as they accept all of the universe, the Christian, by nature of the religion’s inherent doctrine of the imperfection of mankind, tends to dwell on it, perhaps even perceiving it as punishment for some wrongdoing they have committed. Any perception that seeks to avoid or deny the necessity of pain creates the problem of pain where no problem originally lay.
        Pantheism as a religion holds no objection to science and is not only congruent with but necessary for any concept of God in which God is infinite, all-powerful, or all-knowing. A god cannot be infinite without composing the material world. If the material world were separate from God, it must be self-sustaining, of an independent first cause than that of God. Returning to Spinoza’s logic, two things may not interact without sharing a common attribute. God could not interact with an independent Universe, which it must necessarily be if God is considered separate from that Universe. God could only interact with a largely independent Universe if God and that Universe shared a common ground, which, according to mathematical logic, would be greater than either God or the Universe separately and must therefore be the true God. Therefore, pantheism does not negate certain fundamental tenets of world religions, namely, that God is infinite and omnipresent. 
The inherent nature of religion, however, has historically caused harm to the state of man. Just as the Pharaohs of Egypt and the kings of France and the kings of England and the Emperors of China and the emperors of Japan once justified their rule by means of Divine Right, so too did the Church once rule with an iron fist under the supposed authority of God. Admittedly, in a historical perspective, the appeal to ultimate authority by rulers of nations benefited the people greatly by lessening strife and fostering unity under a sense of purpose but the Church in the Dark Ages claimed monopoly on religion, acting as sole intermediary between God and Man, appointing those monarchs who could then claim divine right. Such times have ended, but the malevolent nature of religion continues to this day, manifesting in as diverse places as politics, cults, scams, schools, and entire cultures. Religion operates through an intermediary between the divine and man, whether be it an Imam, a Pope, a Guru, a Pastor, a Cult leader, or any other appointed head. The head represents the ultimate authority on matters of Ultimate Authority. In the objective truth of these situations, however, millions are bowed in blind obedience to religious tenets and rules, obeying without need a long outdated mode of survival. While these structures of blind obedience can result in good outcomes as well as bad, the potential for abuse is high, as evident with various religions’ justifications for Jihad, Crusade, Inquisition, slavery, wars, the systematic oppression of women, and, most relevant to this course, in some cases the utter rejection of science if it threatens belief, science that could potentially advance society, reduce poverty and disease, and prolong and increase the quality of life. 
Pantheism threatens this possibility of blind obedience, and has in all authoritative religions been treated as heresy. Pantheism frees mankind from the shackles of oppressive authority. If people realize that they have as much communication and connection to the divine as the pope, they no longer have need to hold his word as infallible in any way, and are free to use reason and free thought to best live their lives. Pantheism also renders obsolete forms of written authority held to be from God. God, as an infinite being, cannot be contained in any way without limiting the extent of the truth of God’s reality. Limiting the extent of the truth of God’s reality would necessarily propulgate falsehood through absence. Any concept of God, any written word, any statement of truth, any conceptual understanding of an entities reality can only be partly true, as concepts, words, and statements, are all limiting in nature. Any partly true thought, idea, concept, or perception, in relation to infinite truth can hold no greater claim of authority to that infinite truth than any other partly true thought, idea, concept, or perception. This simple but finite truth resulting from equating God to the Universe has historically been condemned as heresy. Pantheism questions and threatens every form of authority and adherents to pantheism often cause great social unrest due to their threat and lack of adherence to established social hierarchy, as Matthew 10:34 purports Jesus to say, “I am not come to bring peace, but a sword.”[2] and verse 16 of the Gospel of Thomas purports Jesus to say, “They do not know that I have come to cast conflicts upon the earth: fire, sword, war.”[1] 
The concept of individuals en masse ruling themselves strikes fear into the heart of the believer and the skeptic alike. A common creed, like Christianity or Islam, provides a standard by which society expects individual of that society to act. Pantheism, with its anarchic tendencies, undermines the rallying call of ultimate authority to which so many religious societies adhere. With a rule book, such as the Quran or the Bible, individuals are predictable and society is stable. Without a rule book, however, chaos can grow and flourish. Unfortunately, the common perception of chaos is negative, and certainly not without cause; in the past when the unpredictable could determine whether one lived or died, societies needed common rules and unquestioning obedience. Societies and religions are apprehensive of pantheism or atheism, essentially the same in their rejection of absolute authority, on account that they render the prospective divine punishment or reward promised by the disobedience or obedience of moral laws obsolete. While pantheism discards the authority of commandments by not blindly obeying them, pantheism itself is the most morally upright of all philosophies.
Giordano Bruno, Erasmus Darwin, and Percy Shelley, all notable thinkers with tendencies, if not outright declarations, of pantheism, lived their lives out of love for every living creature, and desired the unity and liberty of man. In Cause, Principle, and Unity, Giordano Bruno says, “Every soul and spirit has some degree of continuity with the universal spirit, which is recognized to be located not only where  the individual soul lives, and perceives, but also to be spread out everywhere…”[6] Giordano Bruno also says, “Nature is none other than God in things. Whence all of God is in all of things.”[7] By viewing everything as divine, one is naturally inclined to act with gentleness, love, and mercy. Giordano Bruno valued liberty and unity above all else, and despised the division and pain and general lack of free thought that he observed in the masses. Bruno says in On the Infinite Universe and Worlds, “Through her [Unity’s] virtue I envy not those who are bond though free, who grieve in the midst of pleasures, who endure poverty in their wealth, and a living death.”[7] For Bruno, a lack of awareness of the unity of the universe and the brotherhood not only of man with man but of man with all forms of life is equivalent to hardly living at all. Giordano Bruno saw all things, animate and inanimate, as divine and as such held a deep appreciation for all facets of existence. This deep appreciation rendered him incapable of consciously committing evil. As a pantheist, Giordano Bruno upheld the highest moral standards not out of fear of punishment or hope for reward but out of natural tendency.
        Erasmus Darwin, not quite pantheist, but holding the notion that all life came from Mother Earth and Father Time, rid himself of the common, petty divisions that mankind habitually suffers. Described as friendly, generous, and self-ruling, Erasmus Darwin genuinely desired the good of all mankind. Far ahead of the social norm, he actively worked towards the abolition of slavery and for the education of women. A principle effect of the pantheistic worldview manifests in the appreciation of all life. Erasmus Darwin glimpsed the possibility of a world free from social hierarchy and master-slave relationships, in both the metaphorical and literal senses. However, as many others did, he found certain ideas of his concerning the unity of the universe and the origin of life, perceived by the people as equating man to lesser beings, largely rejected.[4]
        Percy Shelley, another philanthropic individual, influenced by the logic of Spinoza and ideas of Erasmus Darwin, also found his revolutionary ideas concerning the nature of universe and societies of man rejected and cause for his social ostracization. Oxford expelled Percy Shelley in 1811 on account of his refusal to renounce the statements in his essay The Necessity of Atheism, which denies the existence of a creator god on logical grounds but allows for the possibility of a pervading Spirit co-eternal with the universe.[9][11] Shelley, seen by society as a brigand and a miscreant due to his outspoken atheism, his form of atheism denying only the existence of a creator god specifically, nonetheless fostered high ideals for society and the state of man. Rebellious against figures and forms of authority, as beautifully portrayed in his poem “Ozymandias”, Shelley desired that people rule themselves and not allow others to decide their lives and control their thoughts. For this reason, he saw the majority of institutionalized religion, with its rules and foundation of fear through threats of punishment should the rules be broken, as inherently tyrannical and an enemy to the liberty and freedom of mankind and the mind. Percy Shelley did not rage solely against religion, but against all forms of tyranny, such as the Peterloo Massacre in 1819, a violent reaction against peaceful pro-democracy demonstrators. Percy wrote in reaction to the event his poem “The Masque of Anarchy”, in which he writes, “Declare with measured words that ye / Are, as God has made ye, free.”[10] Peter Shelley, as a pantheist, sought the singular goal of the liberty of mankind, for mankind to be held accountable only to the authority of existence itself, free to love and think and reason without fear of retribution.
        Though pantheism has existed as a communicable philosophy for at least two thousand years, it has never garnered widespread acceptance. Pantheism offers a worldview grounded in logic, friendly to science, desirous of harmony with the universe, and in accord with a peaceful and loving nature. Adherents to Pantheism answer to no limited form of supposed ultimate authority, understanding that one cannot, as a part of the ultimate authority of the universe, deny the ultimate authority of the universe. In the face of the masses, stratified into social hierarchies, and identifying with beliefs and traditions, the notion of ruling one’s self defies the societal order, and generates feelings of confusion and antagonism from the general populace. Jesus, Spinoza, Bruno, Eckhart, and Shelley all spoke of the same fundamental truth or idea, that the Universe is God and that man has unalterable liberty by virtue of existence. They all faced persecution, sometimes deadly persecution, from the society or ruling class of the time. To say that greed or covetousness on the part of the persecutors drove their denouncement of the pantheistic philosophers does not accurately interpret human nature. The primary defenders of the established norm rally under tradition, conditioning, and fear. While pantheism is superior in all respects as a philosophy to any organized religion, the people en masse deny it because they do not understand it and because they fear the unknown. 
        Were the masses to embrace pantheism the sciences, civilization, ecological preservation, education, and the general well being of mankind would prosper profoundly, for pantheism forbids no knowledge, and no action, save that which causes harm, and allows for reason to dictate what would and would not cause harm. To clarify, the one natural tendency of pantheism that can stand for a rule is to do the most good for the greatest amount. Simple adherence to the equality of all living things, undeniable in the philosophy of pantheism, and motivation from love, not adherence to tradition or rules that may or may not be followed out of altruistic feeling, would bridge any divide that man might encounter. The equation of the divine and material present in pantheism inhibits the ability of tyrants to exploit and oppress the people and fosters individual growth and liberty. When one views the universe through the pantheistic perspective, one loses the ability to fear, and the crippling need to be accepted, as one realizes that just as God is coeternal with the universe, so too are they coeternal with God and eternally belonging with the universe and all that exists therein. Upon this firm foundation, which no force can move, liberty finds fertile soil to grow and individuality of loving and peaceful nature blooms from the pantheistic tree of life.
