I
He had a habit of balls and lines and row dances. It afforded him the greatest joy to dance with others and to see others dancing all at once like leaves upon a tree swaying in the wind. Such harmony, such fluidity, even the drunken fumbling of the less graceful guests entranced him. At dances, he felt wholly present. The insects sounded without the windows in that silent space between a song's end applause. During the day he wandered the cobbled and muddy streets, always the outsider, the onlooker. Only at dances did he find a common ground with those he loved so dearly. He danced that night as he danced any other, till his eyes met hers. 
He found his being far away, but close by to her, she existed only in the etchings of his eyes as he turned in the flow of the dance and wondered whether he should look again for her or wait till by chance the dance would bring her to him. He lost all his knowledge, all learning, in that sacred moment when his eyes met hers. The less educated, that blossoming, dreaming romantic might call it love at first sight but he knew something more, darker, richer, and forceful and as his weight drew upon his heart he found his hand leave for the next partner and realized in an instant he looked once more into her eyes. 
He looked once more into her eyes and she looked into his. That moment lasted. He knew not long, but he liked to think in his own theories of time that it lasted eons, that civilizations lived and died and blossomed and thrived race after race of intelligent life falling and rising in that dance as they ebbed and rose in that simple music made magical in the moment, that here in her culminated all the progress of eternity all the failure of millennia here in her the great magnum opus of the All achieved.
His next partners passed in an unremembered blur and he wondered if he simply exaggerated his emotions or whether he only hoped for what might fill the vacancy of the truth. He grew sick with troubles, unsure whether to fear or to embrace the adoration of fate. He lost all ground, and knew nothing for certain. 
But she had looked to him the same way! She reflected all. Breathlessness stole her as well! Her face sank in a blushed pallor! She-
"Hello"
His heart choked his throat. How he wished to embrace her that moment, to tell her all the wonderful things he had known only at last so that he might have a companion, a brother, a sister, a mother, a friend, a dearest compatriot of-
"Hello, sir. I am Tristan d'Alle" unmistakeable nervousness seasoned her voice, hidden only by pure will and courage.
All he could answer, "I know." She sat down beside him upon the stone window ledge.
"If you know me, I ought to know you." 
He could see the tenseness in every muscle and feature of her body. Oh! But his name! What was his name? How could he have forgotten at this very moment! Thank god for thinking quick upon the feet. If only he did not so often change names. 
"Paul Henri, Tristan." But how he hated lying to her, how wished he to shed all that he wore as skin.
"Paul Henri," she said with the slightest twitch of her lip which betrayed a hidden smile, "what a pleasant name."
He answered quietly, "Yes, I know. Else why would I have chosen it?"
She laughed at this, and grew red.
"Have I embarrassed you?" Her laugh infected him but he restrained it.
"No, so it is your true name, then?" She said still smiling, though remaining reserved.
"Why would I change it after you said you liked it."
"I don't know. Would you? I like it after all."
"Names, they bear no importance, cannot we know each other only in our eyes?" 
She grew again pale. "Would you like to take a walk." She asked, her face unreadable.
"By God, how I would love to take a walk with you." But she had already gathered her dress and started in a fleeting walk for the door, bending through the crowd like a single flowered reed in a river of debris. He forgot as he watched her indescribable form disappear through the crowd. He remembered when he lost sight of her and hurriedly followed the phantasmal trail of parted people closing.
She joined his side as he walked through the door into the garden, the crickets hosting a triumphant racket over the silence of the song's end. Applause, and a new song begins.


II


How he wished to embrace her, and oh he knew how she wished to embrace him! She clung to his arm as they walked the edge of the garden. But he could not be sure and contented himself with the closeness of her beating heart, she, the wonder of the world to him, the goddess deserving a tree of golden apples.
"What do you think the stars are?" He asked her at last.
"I think," her head leaning on his shoulder, so that he walked slowly and carefully, "that they are distant planets, with worlds not unlike our own." 
"You do not think of them as fireflies or jewels cast in a dome?"
"Of course they are jewels," her voice dreamy, "jewels beyond compare. When we die, our eyes will shine with them as well."
"They shall at least reflect them if we die at night."
"Do you fear death?" She asked plainly. 
"What is there to fear of death? We shall all die."
"Yes, I suppose." They walked in silence for a spell. She continued, "when I was little, looking at the tree that grew without my window, I remembered we had buried my cat there some months ago and I saw him in the bark and the leaves."
"Is it so simple? And what of the soul?" 
"Is there a soul." She said to the open air, for she knew he could not answer her. 
They walked on in silence. The humidity and heat of the night brought the thinnest layer of perspiration upon their faces, so they shone with a dim glow from the light of the dampened ballroom. They walked in the silence of their own musings, deaf to the dull hum of the insects and the muffled music and chatter of the dance. The garden lay bare of others and the few flowers hung closed in the night. Ivy crept around trees and stone pillars lining the graveled path. He resigned himself with some effort not to think, for he knew no thought could combat the stillness of the air, and the heaviness of that night.
Parted in his will he wished to speak, but dreaded to break the silence. Further, he had nothing to say with words and so unaccustomed to human speech. As a musician, he had felt something similar before, when he longed to play, when his heart ached to cast the timbre upon the weightless forge, to smooth the fire across the ebony glass of his violin. He stopped walking. She looked at him with wonder, as if awaking from a dream, the soothing monotony of their walk broken. In that moment he thought of ripe delicate fruit, of tilling soil and watching a seed grow to bear flowers and fruit and of a brush pushing inward the thick garment of a virgin canvass. He thought of all these things, of a fire that warms a small room in defiance of harsh winters, of young animals breaking free of their captive womb to breath the succulent air, of all the emotions a human heart can bare and most of all he thought of how beautiful all those things seemed. He thought of these things, and unable to suppress that creative blazing fire within a moment longer, he kissed her. She returned and embraced him as a falling flower would embrace a changing wind. She had felt it too, that blinding creative lust, and between the two of them they began to plant a garden, they chose the colours and the paints and they gathered wood for their fire and they knew they had found that unnameable existence which filled the dreams of philosophers and alchemists and sages and saints. They melted into each other and lifted away breathless. They had not moved from that spot in the garden, but had remained standing there, wide eyed and alive and joyous at the dreams of life.
He wondered then if she needed to know. But how could one doubt the veracity of this love! How could one doubt that she did not love truly him, who understands him in the silence of a night in the slumber of the tongue! How could he doubt! He determined, then, to tell her, to tell her the truth, and the pain of his existence. From then, his heart troubled him, for though the rapture of, it could not be called love, knowing her lifted his aged wings from the ground and his ancient eyes to a beautiful and hopeful sun, he feared she did not love him, for she did not know him.
"Henri?" They walked still about the garden, the previous weight lifted by the release of the kiss, arm in arm, a shuffled embrace.
"Yes, Tristan?" Her peaceful smile broke his concentration.
"Tell me why your brow has been so furrowed late." Her attention startled him.
"I was thinking is all." He said, his brow furrowing slightly yet again.
"You can tell me you know. I want to know more about you." She caused him to melt but he did not break.
"Perhaps one day. I don't know how to say it, now." He looked up at the stars. She stopped walking, and holding both his hands turned to face him. How beautiful the moonlight showed her, and how old she seemed with that smallest of smiles.
"Is it that you love me? Because I already know." She smiled softly, and with serenity and calm, with understanding.
"Why diminish what we already know with that poor communication of words?" And he kissed her again. Her face felt like magnolia blossoms. He would tell her, he had decided, but even had he wanted to, he could not show her here, or now. Her smile allayed his fears for the time, and they stood embracing, each lost in that rare silence where thoughts absent themselves and heart and mind come forward to breathe seldom tasted air.
