I read “Dawkins’ Godless Delusion”, by J. Angelo Corlett and “Is Nature Enough? No”, by John F. Haught. While Corlett’s essay comfortably achieves a pleasant and sensible approach to the struggle between rationalism and faith, Haught’s argument suffers from cherry-picking, selective observance, and a misunderstanding of naturalism. Corlett writes with a loving attitude, apparently desirous of an actual unification of theology and science. Haught uses superfluous reasoning and arguments that simultaneously work against religion, what he defends, and naturalism, what he attacks. Corlett’s essay centers largely around syntax, expounding his view on Richard Dawkins use and misuse of the terms atheist and agnostic. Haught makes a poor argument against naturalism.
            Haught states in his abstract that he wishes to stimulate discussion in a “somewhat provocative manner”, a manner that he certainly achieves. In that Haught desires to evoke discussion, he succeeds in giving plenty against which to argue. His arguments are one sided, working on arguments beyond necessity, selective appeals to authority, and convoluted reasoning dealing with absolute truth. He argues against naturalism, the idea that nature is the entirety of existence, from three points: its spiritual amplitude, its explanatory adequateness, and its representational accuracy as a worldview.
                He splits naturalism into “sunny” and “sober” naturalism. He then proceeds to quote only from “sober” naturalists such as Camus, Russel, Weinberg, and Hicks. He bases the idea that sober naturalism is more accurate than sunny naturalism in the sober naturalists musing, purely by their own feelings about the matter, no logic or rational thought to be found, that naturalism must of necessity, if true, be a depressing reality to the human race. Haught uses these sober naturalists to form his argument that naturalism lacks the spiritual amplitude to fulfill its followers. At the core, his argument is that naturalism is not spiritually fulfilling because it does not give everyone spiritual fulfillment, though he makes no claim that any other form of religion does, and such argument could apply to any worldview, a weakness that incredibly devalues any worth of the argument in terms of the legitimacy naturalism as a worldview. 
                His arguments against naturalism’s explanatory power and representational accuracy suffer ever more fatal flaws. He admits that naturalism has a vast explanatory power in terms of the universe and how it works. He adds, however, through the idea of layered explanation, that God could be the cause of the Universe and how it works without contradicting the tenets of naturalism. Unfortunately, one could say the same of anything. Using the exact same logic, the universe could just as well be in a matrix, the result of a primordial butterfly wing-flap, evil machinations of the god Azathoth, or the sneeze of Saturn. Of course, such logic can go on forever, with God as the cause of the Universe and God himself being stuck in a matrix calculated by extremely intelligent machines or aliens from another dimension. The possibilities are endless, and, as such, the argument holds little validity in way of dismissing the legitimacy of naturalism. In the parameters of Haught’s logic of layered explanations, it is just as likely, and simpler, for the Universe to be the cause of the Universe, than for God, or whatever one might input for x, to be the cause of it.
          His argument for representational accuracy asserts that naturalism can hold no claim to truth because it claims that the mind is a result of natural causes, and as such, cannot access absolute truth. It is an apologetic argument unfounded in logic, completely baseless, and unsubstantiated on any ground. The counter argument from religion is that since the mind can conceive of an absolute authority, God, it therefore holds the capability of receiving by revelation absolute truth from absolute authority. However, the naturalist holds the universe as the absolute authority, so that argument is rendered null, not that there ever were any logical grounds for it.
                Corlett, however, makes the much more modest and reasonable claim that agnosticism is the most logical and fitting perspective. He reasons that as it cannot objectively be known one way or the other whether God exists, the stance with the least room for error is the suspension of belief. He reasons that though one can make the case that the universe is largely self sufficient, one cannot make the same case that the Universe does or does not need “God”. These arguments, as to the necessity or non-necessity of God and the necessity for open-mindedness, contrasts with Haught’s strict rejection of naturalism.
                Corlett makes a strong argument that a completely atheistic worldview, or even any portion aside from an equal suspension of belief on both sides, is not logically coherent in relation to an absolute, which in this case is the presence or absence of God. Corlett rightly denounces Dawkins mostly atheistic worldview as being atheistic only toward a particular definition of God. Unfortunately, a common problem for theological debates, Corlett provides no clear definition for what “God” constitutes exactly; Corlett only mentions that Dawkin’s arguments neglect the “finer” or “more subtle” interpretations of the nature of God. Admittedly, Corlett himself makes not the argument for the existence of this abstraction of divinity but rather for an open and investigative stance toward the possibility that there lays independent of natural law some divine being.
                Haught does a splendid job of providing what comes off as a callous and belligerent tirade against some narrow niche of a pure and founded philosophy while Corlett does a splendid job of providing an environment of warmth and open mindedness, calmly focusing on both logical incoherencies and logical strong points in Dawkin’s arguments, thus evening the divide between faith and disbelief in the supernatural and opening the space for a reasonable and substantiated middle ground, that of mild skepticism and open-mindedness.  Corlett’s writing demonstrates the character of a gentleman and the mind of a critical thinker. His advocacy for open-mindedness shows that he understands that absolute truth is not so easily found, and, as such for lack of knowledge, no side can be reasonably taken upon any solid logical grounds.
