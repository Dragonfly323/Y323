Log


        The cool breeze dampened the discomfort of the tropic humidity. Our feet, some shoed, some booted, some bare upon the deck, made hollow thumping sounds or soft, padded steps. Our clothes from the weeks of voyaging were stained with our and ocean’s sweat. Land in sight and coming close, the azure waters long having shown the sandy bottom of the Caribbean seas, the crew anchored the ship and let down the smaller boats to go ashore. The lands were quiet as we rowed in silence to the ivory sands.
        The Captain Major squinted his sun-speckled eyes and told us lightly armored men to stay a moment before embarking. I watched clouds form and go through the balmy sky as our boats swayed in the soft wind. The waves broke between our boats and the land. After some time, the Captain Major waved us forward, to step foot upon that world. We rode the surf like Polynesian journeymen.
        A cloud covered the sun when I first stepped to the shore, though I never paid heed to omens. The sand crept into my greaves, impatient to kiss me, like some lover left behind in war. As I unloaded supplies, I saw palm trees from the corners of my eyes. They waved hello, hello, hello. What a pleasant wave. I knew then that were there any land where resided the treasure we sought, this was that land, somewhere past the verdant trees. Fresh spring water flowed from somewhere far upstream. We bathed in that river and drank from it with our throats and ears and eyes. The Captain Major looked for gold. Francisco found little black fruits, like coated pearls.
        In the morning, we left our camp, and entered the woods. The dark green of the vegetation filled our vision for hours. We encountered natives, some four or five in number. They shot at us with arrows. By fortune, before they all could flee, we shot one with an arquebus. At this point, having traveled for half a day into the jungle, we turned back to our camp, following our recently cut path. We arrived in the light of a crimson, setting sun.
        We cut off the head, hands, and feet of the native’s body, which we had brought back with us, and slit open his stomach so that his organs spilled out. Fashioning a crude stake from the wood of a harder tree, we impaled the carcass by the edge of the jungle. The limbs we fashioned in an ‘x’ shape and tied with string the hands and feet, which we earlier removed, so that they dangled from their respective limbs. The head of the creature we placed within the cavity of the chest, which we had broken open. With such a display to repel the savages, our party slept more securely through the night.
        The shewel, as the English call it, protected us but soon began to smell terribly. From three days on, the moment our expedition crossed into the jungle, the ocean breeze that protected us from the stench then drove it into our skin. The pleasure we felt when first we saw this new world faded. The jungle lost its splendour to our senses. Its humidity suffocated us. Its rot ate away at our feet and our fingers. Pablo had the appearance of a leopard by the second week of our stay. By the third week, we all suffered from some malady of the jungle.
        By the fourth week, our spirits were low. Our expeditions returned nothing new to us, and the jungle never ended. Our maps showed a half day radius in every direction from our camp, and our cartographers had grown weary of drawing trees. Twenty-six of our party stayed in their beds, riddled with mysterious ailments. Fourteen of those had festering sores and blackened toes. Sixteen had dull yellow eyes. Eleven made no response to words, food, or drink and could do naught but waste slowly and silently away.
        On the 32nd day of landfall, while clouds covered the moon, savages crept past the hanging skeleton of their kin and slaughtered our watchman and four more who slept. The fourth poor soul cried out, alerting us all, and we frightened away the devils with our firearms, killing two. The difficulty of moving the sick prevented our returning to the ship, so we remained through the night.
        In the morning, we inspected the bodies of the savages. Their skin was smooth, with no rashes or sores, and their eyes shone white like the moon, even in death. The Captain Major found this a hopeful sign for our pursuit. He took their healthy appearance as proof of the existence and closeness of the treasure. However, the crew was not so ready. Venturing any further than we had would mean a full night in the jungle. The Captain Major understood this well, but the ineffectiveness of our medicines on the sick meant that the only cure to the ailments of our crew, and, indeed, the only hope of our returning alive, was the treasure.
        We prepare now for one final expedition into that hostile garden of dreams. We six of courageous souls, we who survive unimpeded by death or ailment, we who cannot leave, we with no other way; we are not enough to sail our vessel. In the two days since that night when the savages attacked, two of whom’s decaying remnants now hang, we have moved our sick to the ship, leaving Carlos to tend them, measured our supplies, and steeled our nerves at this last purgatory of life. The Captain Major deemed I write down a record of our travails in this new world, should none of our party survive, and bury it here by the cliff, with the ship’s log. If we do not survive, this place will be our grave and the grave of those who float helpless on the sea. May God be with us all.
                                                Signed,
                                                        Juan De La Fuente, August 29, 1534
