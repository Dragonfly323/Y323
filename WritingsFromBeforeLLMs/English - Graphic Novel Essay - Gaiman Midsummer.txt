Not having read the actual play by Shakespeare, Neil Gaiman’s “A midsummer Night’s Dream” seems to be a dreamy allusion to some forgotten mythology. There are not only strong mythic elements, but numerous embodiments of nature and the forces of natures, or human emotion. To draw from generalities, the depiction of sprites, fairies, and demons both as the audience and the subject matter of the play alludes to the ancient traditions of divine or unknown inspirations into the nature of the universe. Neil Gaiman’s employment of the subject matter being the perceiver of the play gives the impression of a surreal otherworldliness. Throughout the performance, entities such as Peaseblossom, Robin Goodfellow, and King Oberon give their remarks upon the accuracy of the play’s depictions both historically and personally. Based on Gaiman’s version, I wonder if the actual play is itself somewhat beyond the realm of mortal perception. For though the origins of the characters express understanding and enjoyment, however might a foreigner understand the going ons? Is Shakespeare’s play a testament to the utter inability to know things of divine and mystical nature, no matter how one might attempt to pursue them?
        On the other hand, tragedies and comedies know no set plane. In departing from the norms of understood society and culture, while retaining certain familiar elements, such as the royal court, Shakespeare testifies to the universality of human emotion and drama, bridging a chasm of even the most disparate of peoples. Shakespeare’s use of embodiments of fairy folk, nature, and natural aspects serve as comic exaggerations of human personality traits. An otherworldly fairy maiden can be the epitome of beauty and grace while Robin Goodfellow might be the human propensity towards cruel humor. Shakespeare’s concepts within the context of the play are sufficient tools to communicate the message of humanity’s shared aspects with nature and other cultures. 
        Neil Gaiman’s interpretation focuses heavily on the blending of realities. Neil Gaiman causes the reader to wonder for a moment exactly how real mythologies are, and where one draws the line between playful exaggeration of universal aspects and fiction. A common thread in Neil Gaiman’s Sandman series is the divide between the subconscious and the conscious, or the dreaming and the waking world. “A Midsummer Night’s Dream” is no different and it even goes farther, for Neil Gaiman’s version is a story of a troupe who encounter mythological beings, proceed to perform for them a play in which some humes encounter mythological beings and fall asleep only to awake and wonder if it were a dream and fall asleep only to awake and wonder if it were a dream. That is a perfectly proper sentence. 
        The main theme, I think, is not that one must divide the sleeping from the waking but rather to understand how integrally the two intertwine. More than this, Neil Gaiman’s story suggests the necessity of the unreal within the real. For, and this might be said of all the Sandman stories, what is man without dreams? Neil Gaiman’s interpretation of Shakespeare’s play opens to me the idea that there is no real difference in the affectations of humanity whether the phantasmagorical creatures are real or imagined. Regardless, humanity is inspired and such things are often visions of great beauty that otherwise could never be found. Myths need not be factual to be true.
