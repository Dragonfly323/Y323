The Nature of Budding Romanticism:
Rural Virtue’s War Against Urban Vice Through the Lens of Wordsworth and Clare


        To its contemporaries, Romanticism first arose like a violet amidst the paved roads of detested urbanization. Those who swooned under its allure grew disillusioned with the progress of society and saw predominant within the vices of man multiplied. Sickened by the greed, pollution, and poor morals of the cities and their denizens, romanticists fled to find the feathers of nature’s wings sheltering them from the sun of their dissolution. In their new found paradise, some mimicked the style of their muses and wrote in the language of flowers while some wrote plainly of pure human emotion. They burned the wasted papers of bankers and businessmen and planted the seeds of glistening brows, weathered hoes, quiet reflection, and communion with nature. Reminiscent of the parable of the prodigal son, the romanticists abandoned the plague of the city and its poisoned waters and returned to their homeland, their father’s house, the state of man within nature. Their poems capture their feelings on nature and urban society.
        In his preface to “Lyrical Ballads”, William Wordsworth attacks urban society by addressing the illusion of the city. His argument establishes the degrading and convoluting effect fast-paced life city life has on its inhabitants. He explains that, “under the influence of social vanity”, citizens place more importance on outward appearance and status, becoming something akin to the Biblical whited sepulchers, dead within. To Wordsworth, those living in the cities risk themselves dying intellectually and emotionally due to the infertile complexities of human society found therein in their greatest concentrations. Wordsworth praises the virtues of nature and urges that man seek the “low and rustic life” where “the essential passions of the heart find a better soil in which they can attain their maturity, are less under restraint, and speak a plainer and more emphatic language”, and “elementary feelings coexist in a state of greater simplicity.” Wordsworth believes that people living in cities lose awareness of their humanity and that people living in rural areas free from societal distractions and pressures more easily address their inner soul and core human emotions.
        In “The Tables Turned”, Wordsworth focuses on intellectualism, another aspect of city life related to the complexities of society, as a vice. “Up! up! my Friend, and quit your books;” Wordsworth begins, saying that “there’s more of wisdom in [the song of the woodland linnet.]” Wordsworth’s dissatisfaction with intellectualism stems not only from its seeming lack of a purposeful end but from its inability to provide positive change to the extent the effortless appreciation of nature can. Wordsworth says, “Books! ‘tis a dull and endless strife”, expressing his understanding of the allure of intellectualism, and its horrid descent into a craving of more and more, as with most things in the city, that can never be satisfied. This endless craving comprises the idea of “savage torpor” addressed in Wordsworth’s “Preface to Lyrical Ballads.” Through the emphatic language of “The Tables Turned”, Wordsworth implores man to break the dreadful urban cycle and abandon abstract, endless meddlings in haughty intellectualism and to instead submit himself to an appreciation of life, best exemplified in the raw vitality of nature.
        In “Lines Composed upon Westminster Bridge,” Wordsworth writes of the wasted power and presents of nature upon the constructs of industrialized urbanization. He composes his lines in an attempt to capture the splendour of the sun beautifully steeping, of the valley, rock, and hill, and the gliding river. These manifestations of the divinity of nature come together to form the “mighty heart” mentioned in the last line, “And all that mighty heart is lying still!” Wordsworth mourns the lack of response on the city’s part to the gifts of water, sunlight, and terrain that nature provides, knowing that elsewhere they would give birth to majestic forests, rolling hills of grass and flowers, and life abounding on the bounties of the river. Instead, only smoggy air and oil-fluoresced water answer to the longings and grief of Wordsworth, like a cheap whore to a man whose beautiful and impassioned lover has been lost to him. 
        “The Peasant Poet”, a poem by John Clare, another romanticist poet, describes a simple man admiring nature. This poem serves to example an important aspect of romanticism by expounding upon the all-important perspective one must have in order to appreciate nature. Romanticism exists as a mindset. Where one might see in nature only the potential for exploitation and urban development, another sees “the very voice of God”. “The Peasant Poet” focuses on the perspective of the romanticist, “To him the dismal storm appeared / The very voice of God,” and the love he bears for nature: “he loved the brook’s soft sound,” “he loved the daisy-covered ground,” “he loved them for His sake.” The poem takes snapshots of the peasant’s surroundings to accentuate the simplicity of his life: “The swallow swimming by;” “The cloud-bedappled sky” “The insects i’ the brake.” As they do not immediately satisfy the fickle appetite of one troubled and enraptured by the savage torpor of the urban mindset, the miracles of nature are hardly noticed by denizens of cities. The joyous and contented romanticist peasant, however, remains constantly aware.
        These poems praise rurality and deride urbanism. Each in its own way expounds on aspects of nature’s superiority over industrialized society. “The Tables Turned,” exhibits Wordsworth yearning to share the joys of nature with the wasting minds of dead intellectualism. “The Tables Turned” and “Lines composed upon Westminster Bridge” address the absence of life, both in the individual and in the land, discovered in cities. To Wordsworth, the city and all therein consists only of illusion; to him, the city is not “real”. John Clare’s poem shares the appreciation of nature, but does not mention the city. As such, “The Peasant Poet” emulates pure romanticism. Romanticism does not consist of hate for cities but of a love for nature alone. The nature of romanticism necessitates its war against urbanization and industrialization as the expansion of cities encroaches the boundaries of nature.
