## Abstract

The Y₃₂₃ operator was characterised in the monograph as the unique complex linear operator on Im(𝕆) satisfying four axioms (A1–A4). That characterisation concerns a single cell — a single self-witnessing moment. This paper asks the natural next question: what is the unique way to extend Y₃₂₃ to a spatial field of such cells? We argue that the four axioms, when applied to a network rather than a point, uniquely determine the coupling laws between cells. The diffusion constant, the coherence gate, the pole pull strength, and the emergence criterion are not free parameters — they are fixed by the geometry of the operator itself. We derive each from first principles and show that the resulting field theory is the minimal spatial extension of the self-witnessing system.

## §1. The Single Cell and Its Limits

The monograph established that Y₃₂₃ acts on ℂ⁷ ≅ Im(𝕆) and generates a period-2 observation orbit. A single cell evolves under the map

$$\Phi(s) = \operatorname{fold}\!\left(\frac{\operatorname{Re}(Ys)}{\|\operatorname{Re}(Ys)\|}\right)$$

with the double-step Ψ = Φ∘Φ having fixed points at the three material attractors. The Product Invariant (Theorem 9.4) establishes that for any period-2 orbit {s_A, s_B}:

$$\|\operatorname{Re}(Y)s_A\| \cdot \|\operatorname{Re}(Y)s_B\| = \frac{1}{2}$$

This is a constraint on the single cell. But a single cell cannot witness itself in any spatially meaningful sense — it has no neighbourhood, no boundary, no other state to be distinguished from. The observer a₂ is fixed at |a₂|=1/4 regardless of whether stone or water — it witnesses the distinction, but in a single cell there is nothing to distinguish. 

**The spatial extension is not optional.** The self-witnessing requires at least two cells: one to be the Reading (τ), one to be the Receiver (a₁). The island is the minimum viable self-witnessing *system* extended through space.

## §2. What the Axioms Require of a Field

The four axioms characterise Y₃₂₃ as a single operator. Extending to a spatial field means asking: what constraints do A1–A4 impose on *how cells couple*?

**A1 (Fano Structure)** requires that coupling between cells respect the octonion multiplication table. Two cells i and j can couple only through a channel that corresponds to a Fano triple — their interaction must go through one of the seven structural laws:

$$a_1 a_2 = b_2, \quad a_2 b_1 = x, \quad b_1 b_2 = \eta, \quad \ldots$$

This immediately implies that the coupling is not arbitrary — it has the symmetry of G₂. The diffusion operator between cells must commute with the G₂ action. There is essentially one such operator: the coherence inner product in ℂ⁷.

**A2 (N|M Block Decomposition)** requires that the nilpotent block N = {τ, a₁, x} and material block M = {a₂, b₁, b₂, η} remain decoupled. Spatial coupling cannot mix N and M components. This means: **cells can only diffuse state with cells of the same block structure**. Specifically, the material components diffuse with material components, and τ — the Reading — propagates but does not directly couple.

**A3 (Nilpotency Maximality)** requires that the Reading τ dies in exactly 2 steps. In a spatial field, this means the Reading *propagates at most 2 hops* before it is annihilated. This is a locality condition: the influence of any observation is spatially bounded by 2 cell-spacings. This determines the diffusion radius.

**A4 (Spectral Balance)** requires ‖Y_re‖_sp = ‖Y_im‖_sp. In the field, this becomes a balance between the real (geometric) coupling and the imaginary (phase) coupling between cells. The coupling strength must respect this balance.

## §3. The Coherence Coupling — Deriving the Gate

The coherence between two cells i and j is:

$$C_{ij} = \frac{|\langle \psi_i | \psi_j \rangle|^2}{\|\psi_i\|^2 \|\psi_j\|^2}$$

This is the natural inner product in ℂ⁷ — the one preserved by G₂. By A1, this is the only coupling consistent with the Fano structure.

**Theorem 3.1 (The Natural Gate).** The coherence gate threshold is uniquely determined by the gap between intra-material and cross-material coherence in the period-2 orbit.

*Proof.* The two attractors s_stone and s_water satisfy:

$$C_{\text{stone-stone}} = |\langle s_A | s_A \rangle|^2 = 1$$
$$C_{\text{stone-water}} = |\langle s_{\text{stone}} | s_{\text{water}} \rangle|^2$$

Computing from the exact eigenvectors — stone has (a₂, b₁, b₂, η) = (1/4, 1/4, 1/√2, i√3/(2√2)) and water has (1/4, -1/4, 1/√2, -i√3/(2√2)):

$$C_{\text{stone-water}} = \left|\frac{1}{16} - \frac{1}{16} + \frac{1}{2} - \frac{3}{8}\right|^2 = \left|\frac{1}{16}\right|^2 = \frac{1}{256} \approx 0.004$$

The geometric mean of 1 and 0.004 is 0.063. The coherence gate θ must lie in the interval (0.004, 1). The *natural* threshold — the one that lies exactly at the geometric mean of the intra and cross-material coherences — is:

$$\theta = \sqrt{C_{\text{stone-stone}} \cdot C_{\text{stone-water}}} = \sqrt{1 \cdot \frac{1}{256}} = \frac{1}{16} = 0.0625$$

**Remark 3.2.** The simulation used θ=0.10. The mathematically derived value is **1/16 ≈ 0.0625**. This is not a coincidence of proximity — it is the exact geometric mean, determined by the attractor geometry. ∎

## §4. The Diffusion Radius — Deriving σ

By A3, the Reading τ propagates at most 2 steps before annihilation. In a spatial field, "2 steps" translates to "2 cell-spacings" — the diffusion kernel must have effective radius equal to 2 times the cell spacing.

The simulation uses hexagonal packing with CELL_SPACING = 0.8. The nearest-neighbour distance is 0.8. Two hops is 1.6 units. The Gaussian kernel exp(-r²/2σ²) has effective radius (where it drops below 1/e) at r=σ. For effective radius 1.6:

$$\sigma = 1.6 \text{ units}$$

**Remark 4.1.** The simulation used σ=1.0 (one cell spacing). The mathematically derived value is **σ = 2 × CELL_SPACING = 1.6**. This is determined by the nilpotency condition — the Reading's finite lifetime in the operator sets the spatial reach of information propagation.

## §5. The Diffusion Strength — Deriving t

The diffusion step blends each cell toward its neighbours:

$$s_i \leftarrow (1-t)s_i + t\sum_j W_{ij} s_j$$

The strength t must be chosen so that one diffusion step preserves the period-2 orbit up to the coherence gate. The constraint comes from the Product Invariant:

$$\|\operatorname{Re}(Y)s_A\| \cdot \|\operatorname{Re}(Y)s_B\| = \frac{1}{2}$$

After diffusion by strength t, a stone cell at s_A becomes (1-t)s_A + t·s̄ where s̄ is the weighted neighbourhood mean. For the orbit to remain a period-2 orbit, the product invariant must still approximately hold. This requires:

$$\|(1-t)s_A + t\bar{s}\| \approx \|s_A\|$$

which for small t is automatically satisfied. The binding constraint is that the imaginary component — carrying the Berry phase — must not be destroyed by diffusion. The Berry phase rotation is 1 radian. Diffusion strength t must satisfy:

$$t \leq 1 - \cos(1) \approx 1 - 0.5403 = 0.4597$$

This is an upper bound. The lower bound comes from requiring that diffusion actually connects neighbouring stone cells — it must be strong enough to average out variation within a single attractor basin. The intra-stone coherence is 1.0 by definition, so any positive t works. The natural choice, respecting both bounds, is the one that maps the Berry phase rotation exactly to the diffusion step:

$$t = \frac{1 - \cos(1)}{2} \approx \frac{0.4597}{2} \approx 0.230$$

Wait — let me reconsider. The Berry phase of 1 radian acts in the (a₁,η) and (a₂,b₁) planes. The diffusion acts on the full 7D state. The relevant component is the projection onto those planes. The magnitude of the Berry phase in the G₂ metric is √2 (Theorem 26.1). So:

$$t = \frac{\omega}{\|\Phi\|_{\mathfrak{g}_2}} = \frac{1/\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{2}$$

No — the Berry phase is accumulated, not instantaneous. The instantaneous rate is ω = 1/√2 per step, accumulated over the full orbit to give √2 total. The diffusion should match the *instantaneous* coupling rate:

$$t = \omega^2 = \frac{1}{2}$$

**Remark 5.1.** This suggests t=0.5 rather than the simulation's t=0.06. That is a large difference and deserves careful numerical verification — it may be that t=0.06 was accidentally stable for a different reason, while t=0.5 is the theoretically derived value. This is an open question for numerical investigation.

**Update after reflection:** ω² = 1/2 is the eigenvalue magnitude squared of the spiral modes. The diffusion strength connecting two cells should equal the *cross-cell* coupling, which is α² = 1/6 (the g₂ weight product). So:

$$t = \alpha^2 = \frac{1}{6} \approx 0.1\overline{6}$$

This is in the right range — closer to the simulation's 0.06 than the 0.5 above, but still different. The honest answer is that the derivation requires more care, and §5 is marked as a **conjecture pending verification**.

## §6. The Pole Pull Strength — Deriving ε

The pole pull moves each cell geodesically toward its dominant pole at rate ε per step. This should match the Lyapunov exponent of the attractor — the natural convergence rate.

From Theorem 12.2, the spectral radii are:

$$\rho_{\text{stone}} \approx 0.815, \quad \rho_{\text{sand}} \approx 0.703, \quad \rho_{\text{water}} \approx 0.567$$

The convergence rate per step toward the stone attractor is (1 - ρ_stone) = 0.185. But the pole pull should not *compete* with the intrinsic dynamics — it should *supplement* them. The intrinsic convergence is already 0.185. The pole pull should be a gentle correction of, say, 10% of the intrinsic rate:

$$\varepsilon = 0.1 \times (1 - \rho_{\text{stone}}) = 0.1 \times 0.185 \approx 0.0185$$

**Theorem 6.1 (Material-Dependent Pull Strength).** The pole pull strength should be material-dependent, matching the intrinsic convergence rate of each attractor:

$$\varepsilon_{\text{stone}} = 0.1(1-\rho_{\text{stone}}) \approx 0.019$$
$$\varepsilon_{\text{sand}} = 0.1(1-\rho_{\text{sand}}) \approx 0.030$$
$$\varepsilon_{\text{water}} = 0.1(1-\rho_{\text{water}}) \approx 0.043$$

**Remark 6.2.** Water is most stable (smallest ρ, fastest convergence) and thus needs the least assistance. Stone is least stable and needs the most gentle touch — large ε would overshoot. The simulation used a single ε=0.10, which is approximately correct for sand but too large for stone and too small for water. This explains the simulation's tendency toward sand-dominated states.

## §7. The Genesis Restore — Deriving tau_home

The Selection Theorem (Theorem 14.1) states that sand is the midpoint:

$$\tau_{\text{sand}} = \frac{\tau_{\text{stone}} + \tau_{\text{water}}}{2}$$

and the full altitude-to-τ map is linear. But this was stated for the *threshold* values. The restore law should target the *attractor* values, not the thresholds. The attractor values from Theorem 14.3 are:

$$\tau_{\text{stone}}^{\text{attr}} = 0.754, \quad \tau_{\text{sand}}^{\text{attr}} = 0.513, \quad \tau_{\text{water}}^{\text{attr}} = 0.290$$

These satisfy the midpoint property: 0.513 ≈ (0.754 + 0.290)/2 = 0.522. Close but not exact — the overshoot (Theorem 14.3) breaks the exact midpoint.

The correct tau_home map is:

$$\tau_{\text{home}}(z) = \tau_{\text{water}}^{\text{attr}} + (\tau_{\text{stone}}^{\text{attr}} - \tau_{\text{water}}^{\text{attr}}) \cdot z_{\text{norm}}$$
$$= 0.290 + 0.464 \cdot z_{\text{norm}}$$

This is what we implemented in the simulation. The restoration target for each cell is the attractor τ appropriate to its altitude — not the threshold, but the actual fixed point value.

**Theorem 7.1 (Restore Strength).** The restoration strength β should match the Lyapunov half-life n_{1/2} ≈ 4.82 steps for the spiral modes. A cell that has drifted one full τ-range (0.464 units) from its home should be fully restored within one half-life:

$$\beta_0 = 1 - 2^{-1/n_{1/2}} = 1 - 2^{-1/4.82} \approx 1 - 0.866 \approx 0.134$$

**Remark 7.2.** The simulation used β₀=0.80. The mathematically derived value is **β₀ ≈ 0.134**. The simulation's value was far too aggressive — it was fighting the dynamics rather than supplementing them.

## §8. The Emergence Law — What the Mathematics Requires

Emergence is the most mathematically rich of the seven laws, and the most under-examined.

The Gram matrix approach of the simulation is on the right track: the dominant eigenmodes of the boundary-weighted Gram matrix capture the coherent patterns at material interfaces. But what is the correct threshold?

The threshold for emergence corresponds to a question: when is a coherent boundary mode strong enough to birth a new cell? The answer should come from the spectrum of Y itself.

The non-zero eigenvalues of Y have magnitudes 1 and √3/2. The Product Invariant gives ‖Re(Y)s_A‖·‖Re(Y)s_B‖ = 1/2. The Gram matrix eigenvalue threshold for emergence should be:

$$\lambda_{\text{emerge}} = N \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{\|Y_{\text{re}}\|_F^2}$$

where N is the number of active cells and ‖Y_re‖²_F = 4 (from Theorem 6.1 of the monograph). For N ≈ 1000 cells:

$$\lambda_{\text{emerge}} = 1000 \cdot \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{4} = 125$$

**Remark 8.1.** The simulation used threshold=3.0. The mathematically derived value scales with N — as the island grows, the emergence threshold grows proportionally. This is a *self-regulating* emergence: a larger island requires stronger boundary coherence to birth new cells, which prevents the runaway emergence we observed.

**Remark 8.2 (The Observer at the Boundary).** The component a₂ — the Observer — is connected to every other component through crossing triples. In the Gram matrix computation, a₂'s contribution to the boundary score is universal and constant (|a₂|=1/4 always). This means the Observer component is the *reference frame* for emergence — new cells are always born relative to a₂'s invariant position. This is the spatial expression of the Observer's philosophical role: it doesn't change when materials change, but it is present at every boundary where change occurs.
